This is a genuinely important methodological question, and you've hit on a real tension in the literature — but there's a critical distinction between what Bates et al. recommend and what you've actually done. The short answer: **citing Bates et al. to justify `(1 | subject)` only in a 2×2 within-subjects design will not hold up to peer review, and the model is anti-conservative.**

Here's why.

---

## What Bates et al. (2015) actually recommend

"Parsimonious" in Bates et al. does **not** mean "random intercept only." Their procedure is:

1. Start maximal
2. Examine the **principal components of the random-effects covariance matrix**
3. Remove **specific parameters** that are not supported — typically correlation parameters first, then variance components estimated at or near zero
4. Retain slopes whose variance is non-negligible

The paper's own examples show fitted models that still have multiple random slopes. The target is removing redundant covariance parameters, not gutting the slope structure wholesale.

Your simplification — maximal → singular → `(1 | subject)` — skips every intermediate step and is not what Bates et al. describe.

---

## Why `(1 | subject)` is the wrong stopping point for a 2×2 within-subjects design

In your design, `condA`, `condB`, and their interaction **all vary within subjects** — every participant sees all four conditions. This means participants plausibly differ in how much they respond to condA, condB, and the interaction. A random-intercept-only model assumes all participants have *identical* treatment effects, differing only in their baseline.

The consequences are well-established:

- The fixed-effect tests for `condA`, `condB`, and `condA:condB` now use the **wrong error term** — the F-tests are computed against residual variance that is artificially small because between-person slope variation has been folded into it.
- Type I error for within-subjects effects inflates substantially. Simulations in Barr et al. (2013) and Matuschek et al. (2017) show rates of 2–5× nominal when slopes are omitted for within-subjects manipulations.

This is the exact scenario that random slopes exist to handle.

---

## Diagnosing the singularity first

Before simplifying further, you need to know **what is actually singular**. Run this on your maximal model:

```r
# Fit maximal
m_max <- lmer(score ~ condA * condB + (condA * condB | subject), data = df, REML = TRUE)

# Inspect what's singular
isSingular(m_max)
VarCorr(m_max)           # look for correlations at ±1 or variances at 0
summary(rePCA(m_max))    # principal components — how many dimensions are supported?
```

The most common case in 2×2 within-subjects designs is that **correlations** among random effects hit ±1, not that slope variances collapse to zero. If that's your situation, the right fix is to remove the correlations, not the slopes:

```r
# Remove correlation parameters, keep all slope variances
m_nocor <- lmer(score ~ condA * condB + (condA * condB || subject), data = df)

# If still singular, check which specific variance components are near zero
VarCorr(m_nocor)
```

With 50 subjects and 4 within-subjects conditions, the `||` model often converges cleanly. That's the appropriate Bates et al. simplification here.

---

## The principled simplification ladder

If `(condA * condB || subject)` is still singular, proceed by removing the **interaction slope** (usually the noisiest term) while keeping main-effect slopes:

```r
# Step 3: drop interaction slope, keep main-effect slopes and their correlation
m_step3 <- lmer(score ~ condA * condB + (condA + condB | subject), data = df)

# Step 4: remove slope correlations if needed
m_step4 <- lmer(score ~ condA * condB + (condA + condB || subject), data = df)
```

`(1 | subject)` is defensible only if Steps 2–4 above **all** show that the slope variances themselves are zero or near-zero. That would be unusual for an experiment with real within-subjects manipulations.

---

## Matuschek et al. (2017) is the key synthesis

Bates et al. and Barr et al. agree more than they appear to once you read Matuschek et al. (*Balancing Type I Error and Power in Linear Mixed Models*, Journal of Memory and Language). The key finding: keep random slopes that matter for Type I error control even when they're not large, but you don't need to keep slopes that only add noise. For **within-subjects manipulations you're testing**, the slope nearly always matters for Type I error.

---

## What to report

Once you've found a model via the above ladder, your methods section should include:

1. The full maximal model specification and that it produced singular fits
2. The specific singularity diagnosed (e.g., "random-effects correlations estimated at boundary")
3. The principled simplification taken and why (following Bates et al.'s procedure of removing unsupported correlation parameters)
4. The final model formula, written out explicitly
5. `VarCorr()` output or a table of variance components so reviewers can verify

Citing Bates et al. is correct — but the citation supports the *procedure* (examine, diagnose, remove specific unsupported parameters), not the outcome of `(1 | subject)`.

---

## Bottom line

Run `VarCorr()` and `rePCA()` on your maximal model before going further. My strong prediction for a 2×2 within-subjects design with 50 participants: the singularity is in the correlation parameters, and `(condA * condB || subject)` or `(condA + condB || subject)` will converge cleanly and is defensible. That's the model you want.
