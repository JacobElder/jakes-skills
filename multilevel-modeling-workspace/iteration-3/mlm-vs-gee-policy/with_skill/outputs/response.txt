There are three distinct problems with the current write-up, ranging from potentially serious to definitionally important. Your collaborator is technically correct that OR = 2.34 is what the GLMM gives you — but that doesn't make it the right number to report to policymakers. Here's why, in order of priority.

---

## Problem 1 — Direction check (urgent, verify before anything else)

Your outcome is coded as **readmitted yes/no**, which almost always means **1 = readmitted, 0 = not readmitted**.

With that coding, β = +0.85 (positive) and OR = 2.34 means:

> The intervention is associated with **2.34× higher odds of being readmitted.**

That's a *harmful* intervention. Yet your write-up says "2.34 times the odds of **avoiding** readmission" — the opposite direction — and you're recommending scale-up.

Before doing anything else, check how your outcome is coded:

```r
# In R: check the reference level
table(df$readmitted)         # confirm 0/1
glmer_fit@beta[2]            # positive = higher odds of the 1-category
```

One of these three situations is true, and they lead to very different reports:

| Situation | What OR = 2.34 means | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 = readmitted; β = +0.85 | Intervention *increases* readmissions | Flag a serious concern; do not recommend scale-up |
| 1 = not readmitted; β = +0.85 | Intervention reduces readmissions | Direction is right but rephrase to avoid confusion |
| Coding error somewhere | Unknown | Audit the data pipeline |

Do not proceed with the policy write-up until you've confirmed this.

---

## Problem 2 — Your GLMM gives a *cluster-specific* OR, not a *population-average* OR

This is where your collaborator's reasoning breaks down.

In a **linear** mixed model, the fixed-effect coefficient is simultaneously the cluster-specific and the population-average effect — they're the same number. In a **logistic** GLMM, they are **not the same** because the logistic link is nonlinear. The GLMM coefficient β = 0.85 answers:

> "For a patient in a **specific hospital** (conditional on that hospital's random effect being held fixed), what is the odds ratio for the intervention?"

What policymakers scaling up an intervention need to know is:

> "Across **all hospitals in the state**, what is the expected change in readmission rates if we implement this intervention?" — a **population-average (marginal) effect**

The marginal OR is always **closer to 1** than the cluster-specific OR. How much closer depends on the variance of the random effects (the hospital-level variance, σ²_u). With 42 hospitals, the between-hospital variance is likely non-trivial, and the attenuation can be substantial.

**Three ways to get the right number:**

**Option A — Fit a GEE instead (simplest, most direct)**
GEE is designed to estimate population-average effects and is the natural choice when the policy question is about the population, not individual hospitals:

```r
library(geepack)
fit_gee <- geeglm(readmitted ~ intervention + age + comorbidity,
                  id = hospital_id,
                  family = binomial,
                  corstr = "exchangeable",  # patients within hospital
                  data = df)
summary(fit_gee)
# The OR from this model IS the population-average OR
```

**Option B — Average marginal effects from your existing GLMM**
If you want to keep the GLMM (e.g., you care about hospital-level variance components), use `marginaleffects` to compute the average marginal effect on the probability scale:

```r
library(marginaleffects)
avg_comparisons(glmm_fit,
                variables = "intervention",
                type = "response")
# Returns: average risk difference (intervention vs. control)
# This is interpretable: "On average, the intervention reduces the
# probability of readmission by X percentage points"
```

**Option C — Marginalize by integrating over the random effects**
Analytically more involved; Options A and B are preferred.

## Problem 3 — Odds ratios are the wrong currency for policy communication

Even after fixing the conditional/marginal distinction, an OR of any value is hard for health department decision-makers to act on. ORs are not risk ratios, and when the baseline readmission rate is not rare (hospital readmission rates are typically 10–25%, not <5%), the OR substantially overstates the relative risk.

What policymakers actually need:

| Metric | Why it's better | How to compute |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR)** | "The intervention reduces 30-day readmission from 18% to 12%" — directly actionable | `avg_comparisons()` on probability scale |
| **Relative Risk (RR)** | Less misleading than OR when baseline risk is non-rare | Predicted probabilities: RR = P(readmit\|intervention) / P(readmit\|control) |
| **Number Needed to Treat (NNT)** | "We need to treat 17 patients to prevent one readmission" — directly informs resource allocation | NNT = 1/ARR |

A concrete example of how the framing changes:

> ❌ **Current draft**: "Patients who received the intervention had 2.34 times the odds of avoiding readmission."

> ✅ **Policy-ready**: "Across the 42 hospitals, the intervention was associated with an estimated X percentage-point reduction in 30-day readmission rates (from ~Y% to ~Z%), equivalent to preventing one readmission for every NNT patients treated (95% CI: [lower, upper])."

## Summary checklist before submitting to the health department

1. **Verify outcome coding** — confirm the sign of β is in the beneficial direction. This is non-negotiable.
2. **Re-estimate with GEE or marginaleffects** — replace the cluster-specific GLMM OR with a population-average estimate.
3. **Report ARR and NNT, not OR** — translate statistical output into decision-relevant quantities.
4. **Report the hospital-level ICC** — this tells the health department how much variation exists *between* hospitals in baseline readmission rates, which matters for implementation planning.
5. **Report uncertainty** — confidence intervals on ARR and NNT, not just p-values.

Your collaborator is right that OR = 2.34 is what the GLMM produces. The issue is that it's the wrong estimand for the policy question you're answering.
