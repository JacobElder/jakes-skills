Your collaborator is **not right** as an absolute claim — and this is actually a case where understanding the equivalences protects you from unnecessary complexity.

## The short answer

For a balanced 2-condition within-subjects design with one score per participant per condition, a **paired *t*-test is mathematically identical** to the simplest defensible MLM. You are not getting different inference; you are getting the same inference wrapped in different syntax.

## Why they're equivalent

The paired *t*-test is equivalent to:

1. Computing a difference score (condition B − condition A) per participant, then testing whether the mean differs from zero.
2. An LMM with a random intercept for subject:

```r
lmer(score ~ condition + (1 | subject), data = df)
```

For balanced data with two conditions, these produce **identical fixed-effect estimates, identical standard errors, and identical *p*-values** (modulo minor df approximation differences). The LMM random intercept is absorbing exactly what the paired design already accounts for — between-subject variation in baseline scores.

## Why you can't go more complex here

With only **2 observations per participant** (one per condition), you cannot estimate a random slope variance. There's literally one degree of freedom per subject. The maximal random-effects structure for this design *is* just `(1 | subject)` — which is the paired *t*-test. There is nothing more to add on the subject side.

## When your collaborator *would* be right

MLM earns its keep in this design if any of the following apply:

| Situation | What's needed |
|---|---|
| The "conditions" involve different **stimuli** (items, vignettes, images) that are sampled | `(1 \| subject) + (1 \| item)` — by-item random effects are essential; omitting them is Clark's (1973) fallacy |
| You have **covariates** measured at the subject or trial level | MLM handles them more flexibly |
| There's a **third level** (e.g., participants nested in labs/sites) | Random intercepts at that level are necessary |
| You want to extend to a **growth curve or more conditions** | Then MLM buys you flexibility |

## What to say to your collaborator

The concern behind their advice is legitimate — reviewers in many fields have (rightly) pushed back on analyses that ignore clustering. But the prescription is overcorrected. The appropriate response is:

> "A paired *t*-test is equivalent to a random-intercept LMM for this design. If the task involves sampled stimuli, we should also add by-item random effects — but the basic subject-level dependence is fully handled by the paired structure."

If the journal or reviewer genuinely requires MLM syntax, you can submit the `lmer` formulation knowing it gives you the exact same answer. But "must use MLM or reviewers will reject" as a blanket rule is a methodological myth, not a statistical requirement.
