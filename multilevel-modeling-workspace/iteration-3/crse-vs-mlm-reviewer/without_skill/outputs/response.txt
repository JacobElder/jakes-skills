## Your advisor is right. Reviewer #2 is wrong — but here's how to respond effectively.

### Why your TWFE approach is valid

Your `reghdfe` specification is the **gold-standard identification strategy** in empirical economics for exactly this data structure. Here's what it does and why it's defensible:

| Feature | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Firm FEs** | Differences out all time-invariant unobserved firm heterogeneity | Controls for anything stable about a firm (industry, location, founding culture, etc.) |
| **Year FEs** | Differences out all time-varying shocks common to all firms | Controls for macroeconomic conditions, policy regimes, etc. |
| **Cluster-robust SEs at firm level** | Allows arbitrary serial correlation within firms over time | Correct for within-panel dependence; 800 clusters is well above any threshold for asymptotic validity (rule of thumb: ≥ 30–50) |

This is two-way fixed effects (TWFE) — the workhorse of applied microeconometrics. It appears in thousands of papers in the *AER*, *QJE*, *JPE*, and *ReStud* precisely because it's valid.

---

### Why Reviewer #2's objection is mistaken

The reviewer is likely reasoning from a biostatistics or psychology tradition where multilevel/mixed-effects models are emphasized. The key issue: **fixed effects are strictly more robust than the random effects in a multilevel model**, not less.

**The random effects assumption (required by multilevel models):**
> The firm-level random effects must be *uncorrelated* with your regressors (treatment and controls).

In observational economics data, this assumption almost always fails — firm productivity shocks are correlated with treatment assignment. FE eliminates this bias entirely. Going from TWFE to a multilevel model would be a step *backward* for identification.

A **Hausman test** formalizes this: it tests whether RE and FE estimates differ systematically. In economics panel data, FE almost always wins. The reviewer is asking you to adopt a more restrictive (and likely misspecified) model.

### Where Reviewer #2 might have a kernel of a point (and where they don't)

| Concern | Legitimate? | Response |
|---|---|---|
| "FE doesn't model firm heterogeneity" | No — FE *absorbs* it, which is stronger than modeling it | Standard textbook result (Wooldridge, Cameron & Trivedi) |
| "You need partial pooling / shrinkage" | Unlikely — 8 obs/firm is enough; shrinkage matters more for 1–2 obs/group | T=8 is comfortable for linear FE |
| "Nested structure isn't accounted for" | Only relevant if treatment varies at a *higher* level (e.g., industry) — clarify your design | If treatment is at firm level, firm clustering is correct |
| "Incidental parameters bias" | Real for nonlinear models (logit, Poisson), **not for linear OLS** | Your outcome is linear, so this doesn't apply |

### How to respond to Reviewer #2

Draft language for your response:

> "We respectfully disagree with this methodological recommendation. The two-way fixed effects estimator with firm- and year-level absorbing effects (`reghdfe`) is the standard identification strategy for firm-level panel data in applied microeconomics (Wooldridge 2010; Cameron & Miller 2015). Firm fixed effects nonparametrically absorb all time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity, making the key identifying assumption (conditional on controls and year shocks, treatment is as-good-as-random within firms over time) far weaker than the random-effects assumption required by multilevel models. The latter requires firm effects to be uncorrelated with treatment — an assumption likely to fail in observational data and testable via a Hausman test. Standard errors clustered at the firm level (N=800 clusters) follow Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan (2004) and are well-powered for asymptotic validity. As a robustness check, we have added Table X reporting mixed-effects estimates, which are qualitatively and quantitatively consistent with our main results."

Then **run a multilevel model as a robustness check only** — show coefficients are nearly identical. This defuses the reviewer without conceding the main specification.
