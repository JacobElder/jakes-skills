Before drafting the write-up, I need to flag two issues with the current specification — one is a silent bias problem that affects the interpretation of your main finding, and one is a Type I error concern.

---

## Issue 1: Within/between confound (affects your β = 0.31 interpretation)

You noted that SES varies **both within and between schools**. In a standard random-intercept model, the single SES coefficient conflates two distinct effects:

- **Within-school effect**: how a student's SES *relative to their school's average* predicts achievement
- **Between-school effect**: how school-average SES predicts school-average achievement

These effects can differ substantially in magnitude and even direction (a classic case of Simpson's paradox at the cluster level). Your β = 0.31 is an uninterpretable blend of both. This also creates a direct problem for `school_resources`: it's likely correlated with school-mean SES, which is currently sitting undecomposed inside your SES coefficient — a probable reason it's non-significant.

**The fix** is a within/between decomposition (Raudenbush & Bryk call this "group-mean centering with the cluster mean as a level-2 covariate"):

```r
# Compute school-mean SES
df <- df %>%
  group_by(school) %>%
  mutate(
    SES_school_mean = mean(SES),          # between-school component (L2)
    SES_within = SES - SES_school_mean    # within-school component (L1)
  )

# Decomposed model
lmer(achievement ~ SES_within + SES_school_mean + school_resources + (1 | school))
```

Now `SES_within` = the pure within-school effect; `SES_school_mean` = the between-school contextual effect; and `school_resources` is estimated after properly controlling for school-level SES. The difference between `SES_school_mean` and `SES_within` is the **contextual effect** — and it's a substantively meaningful quantity in educational research.

---

## Issue 2: Missing random slope for SES (affects Type I error)

Since SES varies within schools, the SES → achievement relationship may not be uniform across all 60 schools — some schools might show stronger SES gradients than others. Your current spec forces that effect to be constant. The appropriate starting point for a confirmatory test is:

```r
lmer(achievement ~ SES_within + SES_school_mean + school_resources + 
       (1 + SES_within | school))
```

The random slope for `SES_within` gives each school its own SES gradient and uses the correct error term for testing the SES fixed effect. Without it, the SES test is anti-conservative (per Barr et al., 2013; Schielzeth & Forstmeier, 2009). With 60 schools and ~25 students each, this model is estimable — you have plenty of clusters and observations per cluster.

If it fails to converge or is singular, simplify in this order:
1. Remove the random intercept–slope correlation: `(1 + SES_within || school)`
2. If still singular, consider whether the variance in slopes is near zero (may be genuinely homogeneous)

---

## Recommended revised model

```r
lmer(achievement ~ SES_within + SES_school_mean + school_resources + 
       (1 + SES_within | school),
     data = df, REML = TRUE)
```

Run this and check:
- Does it converge cleanly?
- Is the random-slope variance near zero (suggesting homogeneous SES effects across schools) or meaningfully non-zero?
- How do `SES_within` and `SES_school_mean` compare? Are they similar (no contextual effect) or diverge (a real contextual effect worth reporting)?
