Before writing up the methods, I need to flag two specification issues that should be addressed first — otherwise the write-up would be documenting a model that doesn't match the design. Both are common, both are consequential, and neither is a quick editorial fix.

---

## Issue 1: Missing random slope for SES

SES varies **within** schools — you said so explicitly. That means a random slope belongs in the model. The random-effects structure you have:

```r
(1 | school)
```

allows schools to differ in their baseline achievement (random intercept) but assumes the SES→achievement relationship is **identical across all 60 schools**. That's a strong assumption and almost certainly wrong — schools serving similar SES ranges may show steeper or flatter gradients depending on instructional quality, culture, etc.

More importantly: omitting the random slope means the test of `SES` uses the **wrong error term**. When a predictor varies within clusters, its fixed-effect coefficient is estimated partly from between-subject, partly from within-subject variation, and the denominator of the t-statistic needs to account for the fact that the SES slope itself varies across schools. Without `(SES | school)`, lme4 underestimates the variance in that slope and produces an anti-conservative p-value. In simulation, this inflates Type I error for the focal predictor by 2–5×.

With 60 schools and ~25 students each, you have ample clusters to estimate a random slope. The corrected random-effects structure:

```r
(1 + SES_cwc | school)   # after centering — see Issue 2
```

---

## Issue 2: The single SES coefficient blends two distinct effects

This is the deeper issue. When a predictor varies both **within** and **between** clusters, a grand-mean-centered (or raw) coefficient is a weighted average of two theoretically different effects:

| Effect | Question answered |
|---|---|
| **Within-school** | Do students with higher SES than their schoolmates achieve more? |
| **Between-school** | Do schools with higher average SES achieve more, net of school resources? |

These effects are often quite different in magnitude and are not the same estimand. The β = 0.31 you have now is some blend of both — you cannot tell which is driving it, and reviewers in education research will ask.

The fix is **group-mean centering** (Raudenbush & Bryk's "within/between decomposition"; Hox Ch. 3):

```r
library(dplyr)
library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)

df <- df |>
  group_by(school) |>
  mutate(
    SES_mean = mean(SES),          # school-level mean → level-2 predictor
    SES_cwc  = SES - SES_mean      # group-mean centered → within-school effect
  ) |>
  ungroup()
```

Then the corrected model:

```r
m1 <- lmer(
  achievement ~ SES_cwc + SES_mean + school_resources +
                (1 + SES_cwc | school),
  data = df,
  REML = TRUE
)
summary(m1)
```

Now each coefficient has a clean interpretation:

- **`SES_cwc`**: within-school effect — holding school constant, does a one-unit SES advantage over schoolmates predict achievement?
- **`SES_mean`**: between-school compositional effect — do higher-SES schools achieve more on average, after adjusting for school resources?
- **`school_resources`**: level-2 effect, now estimated with school-level SES properly controlled (critical — these two are probably correlated, which may explain the non-significance)
- **`(1 + SES_cwc | school)`**: schools vary in baseline achievement *and* in the steepness of their within-school SES gradient

A secondary quantity worth pulling out is the **contextual effect**: the degree to which the school's average SES matters *above and beyond* the student's own SES, estimated as `SES_mean` − `SES_cwc`. This is often the policy-relevant question in education ("frog-pond" effect).
