## The Short Answer: Mostly yes, but the p-values deserve scrutiny

The model syntax is correct, the simplification strategy was defensible, and the final structure is a legitimate 3-level random-intercepts model. However, there's a real validity concern that hinges on *which* random slopes you dropped.

---

## What the Model Is Doing

```r
lmer(score ~ time * treatment + (1 | school) + (1 | school:student))
```

This correctly specifies a 3-level nested structure:
- **Level 1**: repeated measurements (residual variance)
- **Level 2**: students within schools `(1 | school:student)`
- **Level 3**: schools `(1 | school)`

The `school:student` notation is right for nested designs where student IDs may repeat across schools. ✓

---

## The Simplification Process Was Defensible

Dropping random effects with variance ≈ 0 is standard practice — those components are on the boundary of the parameter space, which is what triggers the singularity warning. The iterative approach (drop the most problematic term, refit, check again) is the right procedure.

---

## The Concern: Anti-Conservative p-values

Here's the critical question: **were the dropped random slopes for `time`, `treatment`, or their interaction?**

When you drop a random slope for a predictor but keep its fixed effect, you're forcing the model to assume *everyone* has exactly the same slope. The standard error for that fixed effect is then estimated as if there's no person-to-person or school-to-school variability in that effect — which compresses the SE and inflates your t-statistic. The p-value looks better partly because the uncertainty was removed from the model, not because the signal got stronger.

The severity depends on the situation:

| Scenario | Concern level |
|---|---|
| Dropped random slope for a **covariate** (e.g., baseline age) | Low — inference on focal predictors is largely unaffected |
| Dropped random slope for **`time`** at the student level | **High** — your `time` and `time × treatment` fixed effects are the whole analysis, and their SEs may be too small |
| Dropped random slope for **`treatment`** at the school level | Moderate — schools may genuinely vary in treatment effect; ignoring this compresses between-school SE |

---

## What to Actually Check

**1. Which slopes were at zero?**
If the near-zero variances were for a time-invariant covariate or a nuisance term, you're probably fine. If they were for `time` itself — the predictor your p-values are about — there's a real concern.

**2. Do the SEs look plausible?**
Compare the SE of `time` and `time:treatment` from your current model vs. the fuller model. If they shrank substantially after dropping the random slopes, that's the inflation in action.

**3. Consider a robustness check**
Fit a model that keeps the student-level random slope for `time` even if it's near-zero, using `REML = FALSE` and constrained optimization if needed (or `glmmTMB` with `map` argument to hold it near-zero but acknowledged). If the fixed effect estimates and SEs barely change, you're safe. If they shift meaningfully, report the simpler model but note the limitation.

**4. Degrees of freedom for p-values**
`lmer` doesn't give p-values by default. If you're using `lmerTest`, the Satterthwaite approximation is adequate here; Kenward-Roger is more accurate but computationally heavier for 3-level models.

---

## Bottom Line

The model is valid as a descriptive structure and the simplification was principled. The main risk is that **if `time` or the interaction is your key predictor and you dropped its random slope, your p-values may be anti-conservative** — not dramatically wrong, but potentially over-optimistic by enough to matter at borderline thresholds. Run the SE comparison check before concluding the p-values are clean.
