This analysis has a significant methodological problem that reviewers at any major psycholinguistics journal will flag immediately. Here's the full picture.

## What you've done: F1-only analysis

Aggregating to participant means and running a by-participants ANOVA is the **F1 analysis**. It treats your 80 sentences as *fixed* — as if those exact sentences are the only ones you'd ever care about, and your conclusions don't need to generalize to other sentences of the same type. That's almost certainly not what you intend.

This is Clark's (1973) **"language-as-fixed-effect fallacy"**, and it's been known to inflate Type I error for over 50 years.

The intuition: if your effect is partly driven by a few unusual sentences in one condition, the participant-means ANOVA won't detect that — it will just report a significant effect. The item-level variability is entirely invisible.

---

## What should actually concern you

Running F1 only produces **anti-conservative tests for the main effects and interactions you care about**. Your significant effect may be real, or it may be an item-sampling artifact. You genuinely cannot tell from the current analysis.

The traditional fix — still used in some labs — was to run **both F1 (by-participants) and F2 (by-items) ANOVAs**, then either:
- Require both to be significant (conservative), or
- Compute **min-F′**, the harmonic combination that approximates the true F over both subjects and items as random factors

## The right approach: LMM with crossed random effects

Fit a single model with **both participants and items as crossed random factors** simultaneously on the trial-level data (not the aggregated means):

```r
library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)

# Start with the maximal random-effects structure
# (Barr et al., 2013 — what your design justifies)
m_full <- lmer(RT ~ factor_a * factor_b +
                 (1 + factor_a * factor_b | participant) +
                 (1 + factor_a * factor_b | item),
               data = df,
               REML = TRUE)
```

If this doesn't converge (with 60 participants and 80 items, it likely will for random intercepts + slopes but may be borderline for the full interaction structure), you simplify in a principled way — keep random slopes for the effects that are within both subjects and items, drop correlation parameters first, then interaction terms from the random structure.

**Why this is better than F1/F2:**
- Uses all trial-level data (not aggregated means), preserving power
- Accounts for both sources of random variation simultaneously and correctly
- Generalizes to new participants *and* new items
- Handles the unbalanced / missing-data case naturally

Your significant F1 result is **not wrong per se**, but it's incomplete and potentially misleading. Here's what the finding actually means right now: *these participants, on average, showed the effect*. It says nothing about whether the effect generalizes across items.
