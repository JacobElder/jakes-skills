This is a real methodological issue worth addressing before submission — not just a reporting style question. Here's a frank assessment:

## The Core Problem: Language-as-Fixed-Effect Fallacy

Your analysis treats **participants as random** but **items as fixed**. That means your results technically only generalize to those exact 40 sentences per condition — not to the broader population of sentences of that type. Clark (1973) identified this as a fundamental validity problem in psycholinguistics, and it inflates Type I error for item-level generalization.

By-participant ANOVA on condition means ("F1") is how labs have run experiments for decades — but it's been considered insufficient at most journals since roughly 2008–2010.

## What Reviewers at Top Journals Will Expect

**Minimum acceptable (older standard):**
Run a parallel **by-items ANOVA (F2)** — compute mean RT per item per condition, then run the same 2×2 ANOVA on those item means. Report both F1 and F2 (or min-F′). If F2 is not significant, your finding doesn't generalize beyond your specific items.

**Current standard:**
Run **Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LMMs)** with crossed random effects for both participants and items. In R:

```r
library(lme4)
m <- lmer(RT ~ cond1 * cond2 + (1 + cond1 * cond2 | participant) + 
                                (1 + cond1 * cond2 | item), data = df)
```

This simultaneously accounts for both sources of random variability and is what Baayen, Davidson & Bates (2008) and Barr et al. (2013) established as the field standard. If the maximal model doesn't converge, you simplify the random effects structure and justify it.

## What to Report

Regardless of approach:

| What | Why |
|---|---|
| Effect size (partial η² or Cohen's *d*) | Required by most journals; F-values alone are uninformative about magnitude |
| Both F1 and F2, **or** LMM results | Demonstrates item-level generalizability |
| Random effects structure (if LMM) | Reviewers need to evaluate what you controlled for |
| Whether your trimming/exclusion criteria were applied before aggregation | Aggregating first can mask outlier influence |

## Practical Suggestion

Before submitting, run the by-items ANOVA as a quick check. If it's also significant, you can report both and note that LMMs are the planned approach for the full study (or just run the LMM now — it's not much more work if your data are tidy). If F2 is *not* significant, that's important information about the robustness of your finding that you'd want to know before reviewers flag it.

The `lme4` + `lmerTest` combination in R is the standard toolchain; `lmerTest` gives you p-values via Satterthwaite approximation that read similarly to ANOVA output.
