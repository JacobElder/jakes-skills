Your collaborator is wrong — or at least, significantly overstating the case. For this specific design, the paired *t*-test is not just defensible: **it is mathematically equivalent to the MLM you'd actually fit.**

## Why the paired *t*-test is fine here

For a 2-condition within-subjects design with one score per person per condition and no missing data, the appropriate MLM is:

```
score ~ condition + (1 | participant)
```

(A random intercept for participant, fixed effect for condition — there's no room for a random slope because each participant has only one observation per condition, so condition doesn't vary *within* participant beyond the two-level design.)

This model produces **the identical *t*-statistic, *p*-value, and effect size** as the paired *t*-test. They are the same test expressed in two notations. The random intercept in the MLM is doing exactly what the "pairing" does in the *t*-test: it partitions out between-person variance before testing the condition effect.

You can verify this empirically in about 10 lines of R:

```r
library(lme4); library(lmerTest)

# Simulate your design
set.seed(1)
df <- data.frame(
  participant = rep(1:80, each = 2),
  condition   = rep(c(-0.5, 0.5), 80),
  score       = rep(rnorm(80, 50, 10), each = 2) +  # person random intercept
                rep(c(-3, 3), 80) +                  # condition effect
                rnorm(160, 0, 5)                      # residual
)

# Paired t-test
t.test(score ~ condition, data = df, paired = TRUE)

# MLM
summary(lmer(score ~ condition + (1 | participant), data = df))
```

The *t*-value for `condition` in the MLM output will match the paired *t*-test exactly (within floating-point rounding).

## When your collaborator would be right

There are real scenarios where reviewers legitimately push back and require MLM:

| Scenario | Why *t*-test fails | What's needed |
|---|---|---|
| Each condition used **different stimuli/items** sampled from a population | Language-as-fixed-effect fallacy (Clark, 1973) — stimulus variance is ignored | Crossed random effects: `(1\|participant) + (1\|item)` |
| **>2 conditions** or a factorial design | *t*-test can't handle it | MLM or ANOVA (which is also a special case of MLM) |
| **Missing data** | Complete-case paired *t*-test loses power and can bias estimates | MLM handles MAR missingness correctly |
| Participants are **nested in groups** (e.g., classrooms, clinics) | Non-independence at a higher level | Three-level MLM |
| You want to **add participant-level covariates** | Awkward in paired *t*-test | Easier in MLM |

None of these apply to what you described.

## What to say to your collaborator

The paired *t*-test is a special case of MLM — not an alternative to it. For a balanced, complete, 2-condition within-subjects design, it's not a methodological shortcut; it's the exact right tool. If a reviewer objects anyway, you have two good responses:

1. **Statistical equivalence argument:** "The paired *t*-test is algebraically identical to a two-level mixed model with a random intercept for participant and a fixed effect for condition. We used the simpler notation because the design does not justify additional random-effects structure."

2. **Show both:** Run the MLM alongside the *t*-test to demonstrate the results are identical. Include both in supplementary materials if needed.

The one question worth checking: **Were the stimuli in each condition the same items, or different items sampled from a larger pool?** If participants saw different items across conditions (e.g., 20 words in Condition A, 20 different words in Condition B), you likely need by-item random effects and your collaborator's instinct — though misdirected at the *t*-test specifically — points at a real problem. If it's the same items or a single task score per condition, you're fine.
