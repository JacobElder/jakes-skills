## Short answer: The school-level slope drop is probably fine. The student-level slope drop is the problem.

Dropping both and declaring the model clean because "p-values look good" is where this goes wrong — and the good p-values may be a symptom of the problem, not a vindication.

---

## Why the student-level slope for `time` matters

`time` varies **within students** (that's the whole point — repeated measurements). For within-cluster predictors, the fixed-effect test uses an error term that implicitly assumes all units have the same slope. When that assumption is wrong — and individual growth trajectories almost always vary — the standard error for the `time` and `time × treatment` coefficients is too small, and your p-values are anti-conservative.

This is the exact error Barr et al. (2013) document. In simulation, omitting random slopes for within-cluster predictors inflates Type I error to 2–5× the nominal rate. The `time × treatment` interaction is your key inference, and it's the one most exposed here.

---

## The two drops aren't equally defensible

| Drop | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| School-level slope for `time` (σ² → 0) | **Probably defensible** | Schools rarely differ in *how fast* outcomes change; this variance being negligible is plausible. Also, with typical numbers of schools (~10–50), this parameter is weakly identified anyway. |
| Student-level slope for `time` (σ² → 0) | **Needs diagnosis before accepting** | Individual growth trajectories almost always vary. A zero estimate here often signals a scaling issue, not a true zero. |

---

## Before accepting the simplified model, run these checks

**1. Is `time` scaled?** This is the most common cause of spurious singularity.

If time is coded as raw years (2018, 2019, 2020) or large integers, the random slope variance will be numerically tiny even when the effect is real — the optimizer hits numerical precision limits and reports zero. Fix it first:

```r
df$time_c <- scale(df$time)  # or manually center and divide by SD
```

Then refit `(time_c | school:student)` and see if the singularity disappears. Very often it does.

**2. Try removing the intercept-slope correlation before dropping the slope entirely.**

Singularity in `(time | school:student)` is often caused by the intercept-slope correlation hitting ±1, not by the slope variance being zero. Separate them:

```r
lmer(score ~ time * treatment + 
       (1 | school) + 
       (1 | school:student) + 
       (0 + time | school:student))
```

If *this* runs clean, you've kept the slope you need while resolving the correlation issue.

**3. Check `isSingular()` and `VarCorr()` on the original maximal model.**

```r
m_max <- lmer(score ~ time * treatment + 
                (time | school) + 
                (time | school:student), 
              data = df, REML = TRUE)
isSingular(m_max)
VarCorr(m_max)   # look at which component is zero or which correlation is ±1
```

The component that's zero tells you what to drop, not a blanket "drop all slopes."

---

## What to try, in order

```r
# Step 1: Scale time — does singularity go away?
m1 <- lmer(score ~ time_c * treatment + (time_c | school) + (time_c | school:student), df)
isSingular(m1)

# Step 2: If school slope still singular, drop school slope only
m2 <- lmer(score ~ time_c * treatment + (1 | school) + (time_c | school:student), df)
isSingular(m2)

# Step 3: If m2 is still singular, try removing the correlation
m3 <- lmer(score ~ time_c * treatment + 
             (1 | school) + (1 | school:student) + (0 + time_c | school:student), df)
isSingular(m3)

# Only if slope variance is genuinely zero after scaling: your current model
m_final <- lmer(score ~ time_c * treatment + (1 | school) + (1 | school:student), df)
```

---

## One more thing about `treatment`

If treatment is randomized at the school level (the typical cluster RCT design), it only varies between schools — it cannot have a random slope at any level, so that's not the concern. But if treatment is within-student (crossover, or within-school randomization), then by the same logic it needs a random slope by student too.

---

## Bottom line

Your current model is likely **anti-conservative for the `time` and `time × treatment` fixed effects**. The good p-values don't validate it — they may reflect inflated test statistics. Scaling `time` and trying `(0 + time_c | school:student)` takes five minutes and will tell you whether the student-level slope was genuinely zero or a scaling artifact. Do that before reporting.
