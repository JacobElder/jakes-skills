## Don't aggregate — your reviewer has the right concern but the wrong fix

Your reviewer is correct that the school is the right unit for the intervention. But aggregating to school means and running OLS is a lossy, outdated solution to a problem that multilevel modeling was literally designed to solve. Here's what's actually going on and what to do instead.

---

### What's right about the reviewer's concern

If your previous model treated students as independent observations (i.e., ignored clustering), that's a real problem. Standard errors on the school-level intervention effect would be anticonservative — they'd use the within-student error term when they should use the between-school error term. The reviewer is correct that the school is the unit of assignment and should be the unit of inference for the intervention effect.

### Why aggregation is the wrong fix

Aggregating to school means and running OLS solves the clustering problem, but at a real cost:

| Problem | OLS on school means | Properly specified MLM |
|---|---|---|
| Schools with 15 vs 45 students get equal weight | ✗ Ignores this | ✓ Partial pooling handles it |
| Individual covariates (prior achievement, SES) reduce residual variance | ✗ Lost entirely | ✓ Included at level 1 |
| Cross-level interactions ("does effect differ by student subgroup?") | ✗ Impossible | ✓ Estimable |
| Statistical power | Lower (35 data points) | Higher (uses all 1050 students to pin down school-level residuals) |
| Variance decomposition / ICC | ✗ Not available | ✓ Reported |

The aggregation approach literally discards ~97% of your data to answer a level-2 question. MLM uses all of it while correctly attributing variance to the right level.

---

### What the correct model looks like

Your design — students nested within schools, intervention at school level — maps directly to a standard two-level MLM:

```r
library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)  # Satterthwaite df for school-level effect

# The workhorse model
# intervention is school-level (level 2), so NO random slope for it —
# it can't vary within schools. Only the intercept varies across schools.
m1 <- lmer(
  test_score ~ intervention        # school-level treatment (level 2)
             + prior_achievement   # student-level covariate (level 1), grand-mean centered
             + (1 | school_id),    # random intercept: schools differ in baseline
  data = df,
  REML = TRUE
)

summary(m1)
confint(m1, method = "profile")  # CIs on variance components too
```

The fixed effect on `intervention` is exactly the school-level contrast your reviewer wants — it compares schools that received the intervention to those that didn't, using the between-school error term as the denominator. This is precisely what OLS on school means gives you, plus you get:

- Student-level covariates reducing residual noise (more power)
- Proper weighting of schools by size
- ICC to quantify how much clustering matters
- Variance components that tell you whether schools differ in baseline achievement

---

### If you want to show the reviewer they're 90% right

You can actually demonstrate that OLS on school means and MLM give approximately the same intervention coefficient, but that MLM has narrower CIs and richer output. Run both and compare:

```r
# The aggregation approach your reviewer wants
library(dplyr)
school_means <- df |>
  group_by(school_id, intervention) |>
  summarise(mean_score = mean(test_score), n = n(), .groups = "drop")

m_agg <- lm(mean_score ~ intervention, data = school_means)
# Or weighted:
m_wls <- lm(mean_score ~ intervention, data = school_means, weights = n)

# Compare coefficients — they'll be similar
# But MLM SEs will be more precise and you'll have the ICC
```

The point estimates will be close; the MLM wins on efficiency and diagnostics.

---

### Key things to report that satisfy the reviewer's intent

With 35 schools you're solidly in MLM territory (the rule of thumb is ≥30 clusters for stable level-2 inference). Your write-up should include:

1. **The ICC**: `performance::icc(m1)` — tells the reader how much of variance is between-schools, which justifies the multilevel approach
2. **The intervention fixed effect** with SE, CI, and Satterthwaite *p*-value from `lmerTest`
3. **The random-effects variance**: how much schools vary in baseline scores
4. **The random-effects structure**: one sentence — "We included a random intercept for school; a random slope was not applicable because the intervention does not vary within schools"

That last point is important: because `intervention` is a school-level predictor (it takes the same value for every student in a given school), it *cannot* have a random slope. The random intercept for school IS the appropriate structure here. You're not underspecifying — this is the correct model.

---

### Bottom line

Tell your reviewer: "You're right that the school is the unit of analysis for the intervention. I've respecified the model to ensure the intervention effect is estimated at the school level using the between-school error term — that's exactly what the random intercept model accomplishes, and it's equivalent to the aggregated approach while retaining student-level covariates for precision." That should satisfy the concern without sacrificing the advantages of the full dataset.
