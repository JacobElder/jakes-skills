Your reviewer is right about the *level* of the intervention — but wrong about the fix. Aggregating to school means and running OLS is a common instinct that solves one problem (non-independence) while creating new ones. Here's the full picture:

---

## Why the reviewer's instinct is partially correct

The intervention varies at the school level, so schools — not students — are the effective unit of randomization. Treating 1,050 students as independent observations and regressing on a school-level treatment dummy would be pseudoreplication: your standard errors would be far too small and p-values anti-conservative. The reviewer is right to push back on that.

## Why school-means OLS is the wrong fix

The skill's guidance explicitly flags this as an anti-pattern: **aggregating to cluster means inflates Type II error** (throws away power) and discards the within-school variation that helps you estimate parameters more precisely. Specifically:

1. **You collapse N ≈ 1,050 to N = 35.** With 35 observations, you have painfully few degrees of freedom for any covariate adjustment. Add two or three student-level controls (e.g., prior achievement, SES) and you've also had to aggregate those, losing precision and introducing ecological-fallacy risk in the covariate-adjusted estimates.

2. **Each school mean is treated as equally reliable.** With ~30 students per school this is roughly true, but if enrollment is unequal the noisiest means get as much weight as the most stable ones.

3. **You can't disentangle student-level from school-level effects.** Any student-level covariate you want to control for gets aggregated to the school mean, conflating its within-school and between-school components.

4. **You already have few clusters.** 35 schools is on the lower end for detecting school-level effects. Squandering within-school information makes an already low-power design worse.

---

## What to do instead: two-level MLM

With students (level 1) nested in schools (level 2), a mixed-effects model:

- Correctly places the intervention at level 2, where it belongs
- Uses all ~1,050 students to estimate residual variance, which sharpens your level-2 standard errors
- Lets you include student-level covariates (prior achievement, demographics) *at the right level* without aggregating them
- Estimates the ICC, which tells you and readers how much of the outcome variance is between schools
- Produces unbiased fixed-effect estimates under the same assumptions as school-means OLS, but with better precision

A minimal spec in R would be:

```r
library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)

# Intercept-only (null) model first — get the ICC
m0 <- lmer(test_score ~ 1 + (1 | school_id), data = df, REML = TRUE)

# Add the school-level intervention
m1 <- lmer(test_score ~ intervention + (1 | school_id), data = df, REML = TRUE)

# Add student-level covariates (vary within school → can add random slopes if warranted)
m2 <- lmer(test_score ~ intervention + prior_score + ses + (1 | school_id),
           data = df, REML = TRUE)
```

**On random slopes:** Because the intervention is a school-level predictor (every student in a school gets the same value), it *cannot* have a random slope — that variation is fully absorbed by the school random intercept. If you have student-level predictors like `prior_score` that vary within schools and you care about whether their effect differs by school, you could add `(1 + prior_score | school_id)`, but with only 35 schools that will be noisy. Check for singular fits.

---

## A note on statistical power

35 schools is already below the ~50-cluster threshold Hox recommends for stable level-2 inference. School-means OLS gives you those same 35 schools but with inflated standard errors (because each school mean has sampling error that OLS ignores). MLM's partial-pooling of the random intercepts doesn't fully rescue small-cluster problems, but it's still meaningfully better than discarding within-school data. Report your ICC and variance components so readers can assess how much of the signal is between schools.

---

## Bottom line

| Approach | What it gets right | What it gets wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Naive OLS on students | Uses all data | Pseudoreplication; SEs too small |
| OLS on school means | Correct unit of assignment | Discards within-school data; low power; can't cleanly include student-level covariates |
| **Two-level MLM** | Correct unit of assignment; uses all data; right SEs; handles student-level covariates | Requires care on random-effects spec; 35 clusters is modest |

Tell your reviewer the instinct was right (school is the unit) but that MLM is the proper implementation — it respects the hierarchical structure without discarding the within-school information that buys you power.
