## No — this model has a fatal identification problem, plus a missing level of random effects.

There are two distinct errors here, and they compound each other. Let me take them in order.

---

## Error 1: Perfect collinearity — the policy effect is unidentifiable

**Policy is assigned at the district level.** Knowing which district a student belongs to tells you, with certainty, whether that district adopted the policy. Policy is therefore a deterministic function of district membership.

Including 14 district dummies absorbs *all* between-district variation — including, necessarily, the variation due to policy adoption. The policy indicator and the district dummy set lie in the same column space of the design matrix.

```r
# What you'd see in R
lmer(score ~ policy + district_dummies + covariates + (1 | school))
# lme4 will silently drop the collinear column (usually `policy`), or
# produce rank-deficient fixed effects with NA coefficient for policy.
# Either way: you have no policy estimate.
```

This is the "treatment at the group level, fixed effects for groups" problem. District fixed effects answer the question *"what is the within-district effect of policy?"* But policy does not vary within districts — so there's nothing to estimate. You can't use within-district variation to identify a between-district treatment.

The collaborator's instinct — controlling for district-level confounders — is correct. But district fixed effects are the wrong instrument for that goal when the treatment is itself a district-level variable.

---

## Error 2: The random-effects structure is missing an entire level

Your data are **three-level nested**: students (L1) within schools (L2) within districts (L3). The model as written only includes a school random intercept, ignoring the district level entirely as a random-effects structure. This means:

- Within-district school clustering is captured ✓
- Between-district clustering is not modeled ✗
- The error term for the policy coefficient (which lives at L3) is wrong ✗

With a district-level treatment, the relevant "error" for testing policy is variance at the district level — the between-district residual after accounting for policy. Omitting a district random intercept lumps that variance into the residual, deflates the standard error on policy, and produces anti-conservative inference.

---

## The correct model

You need a three-level model with random intercepts at both schools and districts:

```r
library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)  # Satterthwaite df

# Full three-level model
m1 <- lmer(score ~ policy + covariates + (1 | district/school),
           data = dat, REML = TRUE)

# Equivalent explicit form
m1 <- lmer(score ~ policy + covariates + (1 | district) + (1 | school:district),
           data = dat, REML = TRUE)
```

To adjust for district-level confounders (the legitimate goal behind the collaborator's suggestion), include **district-level covariates as fixed effects** — not district dummies:

```r
m2 <- lmer(score ~ policy + district_urbanicity + district_poverty_rate +
             student_covariates + (1 | district/school),
           data = dat, REML = TRUE)
```

This models the confounders explicitly, leaves the policy variable identified, and preserves the between-district variance structure needed for correct inference.

---

## A serious power caveat you need to communicate

With 15 districts as the treatment unit, the effective degrees of freedom for testing the policy effect is approximately **13** (15 districts − intercept − policy), regardless of the 600-student sample size. The policy *p*-value reflects 15 observations at the highest level. That's a sparse base for causal inference on a district-level treatment.

Some direct implications:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Treatment-assigning units | 15 districts |
| Treated / control districts | 7 / 8 (or 8 / 7) |
| Effective df for policy test | ~13 |
| Useful student N for policy test | Limited — students add precision within districts but don't add district-level power |

With only 15 level-3 units, the district-level variance component will also be noisy. Hox recommends 50+ clusters for stable level-2/3 inference. At 15, consider a **Bayesian fit with weakly informative priors** (e.g., `brms`) — it regularizes the variance components rather than letting them collapse to zero or explode, and gives you honest uncertainty quantification via credible intervals rather than a *p*-value that implies more precision than the design supports.

```r
library(brms)

m_bayes <- brm(score ~ policy + district_covariates + student_covariates +
                 (1 | district/school),
               data = dat,
               prior = c(
                 prior(normal(0, 10), class = b),
                 prior(normal(0, 0, 1), class = b, coef = policy),  # weakly informative
                 prior(exponential(1), class = sd)                  # regularizes variance components
               ),
               chains = 4, iter = 4000, cores = 4)
```

## Summary

| Issue | Collaborator's model | Correct model |
|---|---|---|
| Policy identification | ❌ Collinear with district dummies — unestimable | ✓ Varies at L3, identified |
| Random effects | ❌ Missing district level | ✓ `(1 \| district/school)` |
| Confounder adjustment | Fixed effects absorb treatment | District covariates as fixed predictors |
| Inference | Incorrect SE, invalid test | ~13 df; consider Bayesian |

The model is broken at the identification level — it literally cannot produce a policy estimate. The fix isn't adding more covariates; it's restructuring the model to respect the three-level nesting and moving from group-as-fixed-effects to group-level covariates.
