This is a really common reviewer suggestion, and it has surface appeal — but it costs you more than it gains. Let me walk through why.

## Why the reviewer's logic seems reasonable (but isn't the whole story)

The reviewer is right that your intervention varies **between schools**, not within them. So schools are indeed the unit of randomization/assignment. This is a legitimate concern: if you ran a naive OLS on 1,050 students with students as the unit, you'd have pseudoreplication — inflated degrees of freedom and anti-conservative tests.

But **aggregating to school means is the wrong cure.** MLM handles the non-independence problem correctly *without* throwing away data.

## What aggregation actually costs you

**1. You lose the ability to include student-level covariates as controls.**
If you want to adjust for prior achievement, SES, or any student-level characteristic, aggregation forces you to either drop them or aggregate them too — and aggregated covariates don't do the same statistical work. In MLM, you can include student-level covariates at level 1, which both controls for confounding *and* reduces residual variance, sharpening your estimate of the school-level intervention effect.

**2. You lose precision — not gain it.**
With n = 35 school means, your regression has 35 observations. With MLM on ~1,050 students, the level-2 test of the intervention still uses school-level degrees of freedom (correctly), but the student-level data contributes to estimating the outcome variance, covariate effects, and within-school residuals — all of which feed precision upward.

**3. Simple OLS on school means ignores differential reliability.**
A school mean based on 30 students is noisier than one based on 60. OLS treats them identically. MLM naturally weights through the variance structure. (If you do aggregate, at minimum use *weighted* OLS with school *n* as weights — but MLM does this and more.)

**4. You lose variance components and ICC.**
You won't know how much of the outcome variance is between vs. within schools, which is often substantively interesting and always expected in a methods-aware review.

## What you should actually do

Fit a two-level model with students at level 1 and schools at level 2:

```r
library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)

# Minimal correct model for a school-level intervention
m1 <- lmer(test_score ~ intervention          # L2 predictor (varies between schools)
                      + student_covariate_1   # L1 controls (e.g., prior score, SES)
                      + student_covariate_2
                      + (1 | school),         # random intercept for school
           data = df,
           REML = TRUE)
```

The intervention effect here is tested at the school level — the degrees of freedom will be approximately 33 (35 schools − 2), not 1,048. That's correct, and it matches what an OLS-on-means analysis would give you for the denominator df. The difference is you also get to adjust for student-level covariates and properly decompose variance.

**Why `(1 | school)` is sufficient here (and when it isn't):**
Because the intervention is purely between-school, no student-level predictor interacts with school assignment in a way that requires a random slope *for the intervention* — the intervention doesn't vary within schools, so there's nothing to slope over. You *do* need random slopes if you have within-school predictors you're testing (e.g., time, student-level treatment arms, etc.).

## How to respond to the reviewer

The reviewer's underlying concern — that the student-level error term is wrong — is valid. But MLM addresses that concern *correctly*. A reasonable response:

> "We agree that the school is the unit of assignment and that student-level error terms should not be used for the intervention test. Rather than aggregating to school means (which discards student-level covariate information and treats all school means as equally reliable regardless of class size), we fit a two-level multilevel model with students nested in schools. The intervention coefficient is estimated at level 2, with school-level degrees of freedom for inference. This is the standard recommendation for cluster-randomized designs (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002; Hox et al., 2018) and recovers the correct standard error for the school-level effect while also adjusting for student-level covariates."

With 35 schools and ~30 students each, MLM is perfectly well-powered for this structure — you're comfortably above the threshold where random-effect variance estimates are stable.
