## The write-up contains a critical interpretation error

The phrase **"in the population"** signals a **marginal (population-average)** effect. But logistic GLMM coefficients are **conditional (cluster-specific)** estimates. These are numerically different quantities — and conflating them is one of the most common errors in GLMM reporting.

---

### What β = 1.2 actually means in your GLMM

Your model estimates:

> *"For a patient in a **given clinic** (holding that clinic's random effect constant), receiving the intervention multiplies the odds of adhering by 3.32."*

This is a **within-clinic** statement. The random effect for each clinic is implicit in the estimate — the coefficient conditions on it.

---

### Why "in the population" is wrong here

Because the logistic link function is **nonlinear**, you can't simply average a conditional odds ratio across clinics and get the same number back. The population-average OR — what you'd get from integrating over the distribution of clinic random effects — is **always attenuated toward 1** relative to the cluster-specific OR.

The attenuation depends on your clinic-level variance σ²:

$$\text{OR}_{\text{marginal}} \approx \exp\!\left(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{1 + 0.346\,\sigma^2}}\right)$$

A quick illustration with your β = 1.2:

| Clinic-level σ² | Marginal OR (approx.) | vs. your reported 3.32 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.25 (small) | ≈ 3.14 | −5% |
| 0.50 (moderate) | ≈ 3.03 | −9% |
| 1.00 (large) | ≈ 2.81 | −16% |
| 2.00 (very large) | ≈ 2.51 | −24% |

The degree of overclaim depends on how variable your clinics are — which is why you need to report that variance component anyway.

---

### Two paths forward

**Option 1 — Fix the language (easiest, no reanalysis)**

Change the sentence to a conditional interpretation:

> *"In a given clinic, patients who received the intervention had 3.32 times the odds of adhering compared to control (OR = 3.32, 95% CI [x, x])."*

Or more explicitly:

> *"Conditional on clinic-level random effects, the intervention was associated with greater odds of adherence (OR = 3.32)."*

**Option 2 — Compute the marginal OR (if population-average language is substantively important)**

Use `marginaleffects` (R) to average predicted probabilities across the observed covariate distribution and then back-convert:

```r
library(marginaleffects)

# Average marginal effect on the probability scale
avg_comparisons(model, variables = "treatment")

# For a marginal OR, use type = "link" and exponentiate
avg_comparisons(model, variables = "treatment", type = "link") |>
  transform(or = exp(estimate), or_lo = exp(conf.low), or_hi = exp(conf.high))
```

This numerically integrates over your 40 clinics and gives a defensible "in the population" OR — which will be smaller than 3.32 by however much your clinic variance warrants.

---

### Also check: what random-effects structure did you fit?

If your model is only `(1 | clinic)` but the treatment was randomized at the **patient level within clinics** (i.e., treatment varies within clinic), you should also have a random slope for treatment: `(1 + treatment | clinic)`. Without it, the treatment effect test uses the wrong error term and is anti-conservative. If that's the case, it's worth fixing before final reporting regardless of the OR interpretation issue above.

---

**Bottom line:** Report 3.32 as a **clinic-specific** OR; drop "in the population." If population-average language matters for your audience (e.g., a clinical/policy readership), compute the marginal OR via `marginaleffects` — it will be lower than 3.32 by an amount determined by your clinic-level variance.
