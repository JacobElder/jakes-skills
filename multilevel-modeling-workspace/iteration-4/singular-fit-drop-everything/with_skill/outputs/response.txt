## Short answer: probably not — the p-values are likely anti-conservative

The sequential singularity-driven dropping is the exact anti-pattern the skill flags, and the "good p-values" are a symptom rather than a reassurance. Here's what's going on.

---

## The core problem: dropped slopes change the error term

In your design, `time` varies **within students** (repeated measurements). For the test of `time` and `time × treatment` to be valid, the model needs a random slope for time at the student level. Without it, individual variation in growth trajectories gets absorbed into the level-1 residual instead of being partitioned correctly — this deflates the denominator of the test, producing *p*-values that are too small.

The "my model converged cleanly" signal is not reassuring here. Convergence and valid inference are separate things. A model with only random intercepts almost always converges cleanly.

---

## Why the singularity happened — and why dropping slopes isn't the right fix

A near-zero variance estimate can mean several different things:

| Cause | Fix |
|---|---|
| `time` not scaled/centered | Center `time` (e.g., at the first wave), divide by SD or range |
| Too few schools to estimate a school-level slope variance | Justified simplification — see below |
| Optimizer hitting a local saddle | Try `allFit()` to compare optimizers |
| Genuinely near-zero variance | Check with LRT or confidence interval on the variance component before dropping |

The school-level random slope collapsing to zero may be a **genuine data limitation** (few schools → unstable level-3 variance estimates). That one may be defensible to drop. But the student-level slope is a different story — dropping it to achieve convergence while `time` is your key predictor is trading a warning for a wrong answer.

---

## What to actually do

**Step 1 — scale `time` before anything else**
```r
dat$time_c <- scale(dat$time, center = TRUE, scale = TRUE)
```

**Step 2 — try the student-level slope model again with a different optimizer**
```r
library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)

m <- lmer(score ~ time_c * treatment + 
            (time_c | school) + 
            (time_c | school:student),
          data = dat,
          control = lmerControl(optimizer = "bobyqa",
                                optCtrl = list(maxfun = 2e5)))
```

If that's still singular, run `allFit(m)` and check whether the singularity is consistent across all optimizers or an artifact of one.

**Step 3 — if the school-level slope stays at zero across optimizers**

That's likely a genuine level-3 limitation (you probably have relatively few schools). The Bates et al. (2015) / Matuschek et al. (2017) guidance supports dropping it in this case:

```r
m_reduced <- lmer(score ~ time_c * treatment + 
                    (1 | school) + 
                    (time_c | school:student),   # keep student-level slope
                  data = dat,
                  control = lmerControl(optimizer = "bobyqa"))
```

**This is the model to defend in your write-up** — not the intercepts-only version.

**Step 4 — if the student-level slope is still singular**

Before dropping it, check whether the correlation parameter is the issue (a common cause of singularity even when the variance itself is non-trivial):

```r
# Decorrelate the random effects first
m_nocor <- lmer(score ~ time_c * treatment + 
                  (1 | school) + 
                  (time_c || school:student),   # || removes correlation parameter
                data = dat,
                control = lmerControl(optimizer = "bobyqa"))
```

Only drop the student-level slope entirely if the variance is genuinely near zero across multiple optimizers *and* you confirm it with a profile confidence interval:

```r
confint(m_reduced, parm = "theta_", method = "profile")
```

## The question I'd want answered first

**How many schools do you have, and is treatment randomized at the school level or the student level?**

- If treatment is school-level (cluster RCT), you may have 20–40 schools split across conditions — that's the binding constraint on what's estimable at level 3, and it explains the school-level singularity
- If treatment is within-school, the story is different

The answer also determines whether `time × treatment` is a cross-level interaction (school-level treatment × student-level time), which has its own power and reporting implications.

---

## The bottom line for your current model

`(1 | school) + (1 | school:student)` with no random slopes is likely producing *p*-values for `time * treatment` that are too small. The good news is the fix — keeping `(time_c | school:student)` — usually converges once `time` is properly scaled, and it's the model your design actually calls for.
