The model is **correctly specified** as a 3-level random-intercepts structure, and iteratively dropping zero-variance components is defensible. But there are three things worth scrutinizing before you trust those p-values.

---

## What the model is actually claiming

`(1 | school) + (1 | school:student)` partitions variance across all three levels correctly. What it *doesn't* allow is **slope variation**: every school and every student is assumed to follow exactly the same trajectory over time — they differ only in their baseline. That's a strong assumption in a longitudinal study, and it needs to be stated explicitly in your write-up rather than left implicit.

---

## The three things to check

### 1. Why did the random slopes go to zero? (Matters a lot)

There are two very different reasons:

**Scenario A — The fixed effect absorbed the variance.** If treatment is assigned at the school level, the `time × treatment` interaction is already capturing the main source of between-school slope differences. Schools in treatment and control diverge over time, but once you condition on that interaction, the *residual* school-level slope variance really is near-zero. This is a coherent story and makes the singularity interpretable. The student-level slope could similarly collapse if within-school trajectory variation is small relative to noise.

**Scenario B — You didn't have the data to estimate it.** If you have few schools (say, <20), few students per school, or only 2–3 time points, the variance component estimates are too noisy to be reliable. A variance of 0.000 in this case isn't a finding — it's a data limitation. The random slope may exist but be undetectable.

You need to know which scenario you're in. How many schools, students per school, and time points do you have?

### 2. Is treatment at the school level or the student level?

This changes the interpretation substantially. If treatment is **school-level**:
- The main effect of `treatment` and the `time × treatment` interaction are between-school comparisons
- Your effective N for the treatment effect is the number of schools, not students
- The school random intercept is correct here, but effect precision depends almost entirely on school count

If treatment is **student-level** (students within schools randomized):
- You need to think about whether treatment variation is being correctly attributed — the school random intercept absorbs school-level clustering, but if treatment is randomized within school, the model handles it appropriately

### 3. How are those p-values computed?

`lmer` in base lme4 deliberately omits p-values because df are ambiguous in unbalanced mixed models. If you're seeing p-values, they're coming from somewhere — make sure it's the right somewhere:

- ✅ `lmerTest` with Satterthwaite (default) or Kenward-Roger approximation
- ✅ Likelihood-ratio tests via `anova()` with `REML = FALSE`
- ⚠️ `summary()` output with Wald z-tests (anticonservative, treats df as infinite)
- ⚠️ Any output that looks like it came from a normal/z distribution — this underestimates uncertainty, especially for treatment effects at the school level with few clusters

The "p-values look good" framing is worth pausing on. If the singularity cascade and the p-value check happened in the same session, there's a risk the model was simplified until it produced a desired result. That's not what you're doing deliberately, but it's worth being conscious of.

## The bottom line

| Issue | Status |

| 3-level nesting syntax | ✅ Correct |
| Dropping zero-variance slopes | ✅ Defensible, but report it transparently |
| Parallel trajectories assumption | ⚠️ Strong — state it explicitly |

The model can absolutely work. The main thing it asks of you is intellectual honesty in the write-up: you have a random-intercepts-only model, slopes were not estimable from these data (or were genuinely zero), and the fixed `time × treatment` interaction is your primary inferential target. State that directly and reviewers won't have a problem with it.
