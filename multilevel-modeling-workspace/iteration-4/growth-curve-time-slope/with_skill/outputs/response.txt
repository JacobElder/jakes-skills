Your advisor's heuristic is widely used, but it's specifically the wrong decision for a growth model where the interaction you care about is `time × treatment` — and the write-up question has a real answer: **not yet, not with this model.** Here's why, and how to fix it.

---

## The core problem: you're testing a slope effect without modeling slope variance

The `time × treatment` interaction tests whether the *rate of change over time* differs between groups — i.e., it's a test about **individual growth trajectories**. The proper error term for that test comes from between-person variation in those trajectories, which is exactly what a random slope for time captures.

When you drop `(time | subject)` down to `(1 | subject)`, the model has no term for "people vary in how fast they change." That variance doesn't disappear from your data — it gets absorbed into the level-1 residual. The residual now conflates two things:

- **True within-person noise** (measurement error around an individual's trajectory)
- **Between-person trajectory heterogeneity** (some people improve quickly, others slowly)

The result: the standard error on `time:treatment` shrinks, your *t*-statistic is inflated, and the test is anti-conservative. The "significant" interaction may or may not survive a correctly-specified model.

---

## Why the near-zero variance estimate doesn't justify dropping the slope

The variance being estimated near 0.003 tells you the model's *estimate* is small — not that individuals truly don't differ in their growth rates. With only 3 time points per person, you have very little information to separate each person's slope from residual noise. A boundary estimate near zero is the expected outcome of too little level-1 data per person to identify slope variance precisely, not evidence that the true variance is zero.

The advisor's logic — "check the estimate, drop if small" — is a form of data-driven model selection based on a parameter that directly affects the validity of your inferential test. The decision about whether to include the random slope should be driven by the **design** (does time vary within subjects? yes) not by whether the ML estimate happens to be large.

---

## What to actually do

### Step 1: Fit the random-slopes model and check for singularity

```r
library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)

# Maximal model (with correlation between intercept and slope)
m_max <- lmer(depression ~ time * treatment + (time | subject), data = df, REML = TRUE)
isSingular(m_max)
```

With 3 time points, this model is **identified** (3 observations can estimate a random intercept, slope, and correlation), but the covariance between intercept and slope is often poorly determined. A common first simplification is to remove the covariance:

```r
# Intercept-slope correlation freed (zero-correlation parameterization)
m_zcp <- lmer(depression ~ time * treatment + (1 | subject) + (0 + time | subject), 
              data = df, REML = TRUE)
isSingular(m_zcp)
```

This is almost always preferable to dropping the slope entirely. The `(0 + time | subject)` term estimates slope variance without constraining it to be correlated with the intercept variance.

### Step 2: Look at the fixed-effect test before and after

```r
# Compare the interaction p-value across models
summary(m_max)        # or m_zcp
summary(m_intercept)  # your current model

# Also: likelihood ratio comparison (REML=FALSE for fixed effects)
m_zcp_ml   <- update(m_zcp, REML = FALSE)
m_intercept_ml <- update(m_intercept, REML = FALSE)
```

If the `time:treatment` p-value holds up (or even grows slightly more conservative), your conclusion is robust. If it evaporates, that's important information — the result was artifact of the misspecified error term.

### Step 3: If `m_zcp` is still singular for the slope

A near-zero estimate with singularity, even after decoupling the correlation, suggests the 3-timepoint design genuinely can't separate individual slope variation from residual noise in this sample. At that point your options are:

| Option | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Keep `m_zcp`, report slope variance near-zero, acknowledge limitation** | Defensible if you explicitly note the random slope is retained by design and the boundary estimate is a precision issue, not evidence of zero variance |
| **Fit in brms with a weakly informative prior on slope SD** | Priors prevent collapse to zero; even `prior(normal(0, 1), class = sd)` on the slope regularizes appropriately |
| **Report the intercept-only model as a sensitivity check, not the primary model** | Flip the framing — your main model includes the slope; the advisor's simpler model is the check |

## What a defensible write-up looks like

You need to specify the random-effects structure **and your rationale for it**. Something like:

> We fit a linear mixed-effects model with depression as the outcome, time, treatment, and their interaction as fixed effects, and by-subject random intercepts and slopes for time (zero-correlation parameterization: `depression ~ time * treatment + (1 | subject) + (0 + time | subject)`). The random slope for time was retained *a priori* because time varies within subjects and the interaction of interest concerns differential rates of change; variance in individual growth trajectories was estimated as [value]. Degrees of freedom and *p*-values were estimated using Satterthwaite's method (lmerTest).

If a reviewer asks why you kept the slope when its variance is small, the answer is: **the test of differential growth rates requires that the error term account for individual trajectory variation; dropping the slope conflates trajectory heterogeneity with residual noise and produces anti-conservative inference.**

---

## The bottom line

The advisor's heuristic describes common practice — but "common" here means "commonly produces inflated Type I error on the effect you care most about." The fact that the variance estimate is near zero doesn't make the random-intercepts model right; it means the 3-timepoint design gives you imprecise slope variance estimates. Retain the slope, use the zero-correlation parameterization, check for singularity, and report what you find. Only then can you write it up with confidence.
