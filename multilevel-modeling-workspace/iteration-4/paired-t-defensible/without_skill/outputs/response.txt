Your collaborator is **wrong** — or at least significantly overstating the case. For your specific design, a paired *t*-test is not a methodological shortcut; it is the correct, fully defensible analysis, and it is **mathematically equivalent** to the MLM your collaborator is insisting on.

---

## Why the paired *t*-test is appropriate here

Your design — 80 participants, 2 conditions, one observation per person per condition, balanced, no missing data — is precisely the textbook use case for a paired *t*-test. It handles the within-subjects structure directly by computing participant-level difference scores, which absorbs between-person variance exactly as a random-intercept MLM would.

---

## The equivalence result

An MLM for your data would be:

```
score ~ condition + (1 | participant)
```

With this design (balanced, complete, 2 conditions), the fixed-effect estimate for `condition`, its standard error, and the resulting *p*-value from this MLM are **algebraically identical** to those from a paired *t*-test. You are not approximating the MLM — you are computing the same quantity. This is a known result, not a rule-of-thumb.

---

## What MLM genuinely adds that you don't have here

MLM earns its keep when your design has features yours lacks:

| Feature | Your design | MLM advantage |
|---|---|---|
| Missing data | None | FIML handles dropout |
| Unbalanced cells | Perfectly balanced | MLM more efficient |
| >2 time points | 2 conditions | Models growth curves |
| Random slopes | 1 obs/condition/person | **Cannot be estimated** |
| Level-2 predictors | Not mentioned | MLM required |

That last row on random slopes is important: with exactly one observation per person per condition, there is no within-person residual variance left over to estimate a random slope. The MLM collapses to random intercepts only — i.e., a paired *t*-test.

---

## What to say to your collaborator (and reviewers)

The "reviewers will reject without MLM" claim reflects a **common overgeneralization**: the true principle is that repeated/nested data require an analysis that accounts for non-independence, and MLM is one way to do that. But the paired *t*-test accounts for non-independence too — it was designed for exactly this structure. The principle doesn't mandate MLM specifically.

If a reviewer raises this, a one-sentence response suffices:

> "We used a paired *t*-test, which is mathematically equivalent to a random-intercept mixed model for this balanced, complete within-subjects design with two conditions (e.g., Judd, Westfall, & Kenny, 2012)."

---

## The one case where you'd reach for MLM anyway

If your paper is in a modeling-heavy tradition, or you have **between-person covariates** you want to include as interactions with condition (e.g., does the condition effect depend on age, trait anxiety, etc.), then MLM is the natural extension. In that context you'd want it not because the paired *t*-test is wrong, but because MLM handles the covariate integration cleanly. But that's a research question choice, not a methodological requirement.

---

**Bottom line:** Run the paired *t*-test. Note its equivalence to the random-intercept MLM if you anticipate pushback. Your collaborator is confusing "MLM can handle this" with "MLM is required here" — those are very different claims.
