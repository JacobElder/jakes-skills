# Experience & Aesthetics: Designing the Felt Experience

SKILL.md conviction 2 says *start from the experience, not the mechanic* — yet mechanics are easy to write about and experience is hard, so this is the layer designers (especially systems-minded ones) most often skip. This reference is the toolkit for the thing the convictions point at: the *feeling* a game produces. Read it at Phase 1 (defining the experience) and whenever a game is mechanically sound but emotionally flat.

## The core reframe: aesthetics → dynamics → mechanics (MDA)
The MDA framework (Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek) splits a game into three lenses, and the crucial insight is the **direction of causality**:
- **Mechanics:** the rules and components (what you *write*).
- **Dynamics:** the run-time behavior that emerges when players interact with the mechanics.
- **Aesthetics:** the emotional responses evoked in the player (what they *feel*).

**Designers build mechanics; players experience aesthetics; dynamics are the bridge.** You design left-to-right (mechanics → dynamics → aesthetics) but you must *think* right-to-left (decide the aesthetic you want, then find dynamics that produce it, then mechanics that produce those dynamics). Starting from a mechanic and hoping a feeling emerges is backwards — it's how you get a technically-fine game nobody loves.

Practical use: before designing a mechanic, name the *aesthetic* it's supposed to serve. If you can't, you're decorating, not designing.

## The 8 kinds of fun (LeBlanc) — name your target
"Fun" is not one thing. LeBlanc's taxonomy gives you vocabulary to target a *specific* pleasure. A game usually leads with 1–2 and supports others:
1. **Sensation** — pleasure of the senses; tactile bits, satisfying components, sound/animation (digital). Push-your-luck reveals live here.
2. **Fantasy** — make-believe; *being* the merchant, the spirit, the tomb-raider.
3. **Narrative** — the drama of a story unfolding (campaign/legacy, thematic games).
4. **Challenge** — the obstacle course; mastery, optimization (euros, abstracts).
5. **Fellowship** — social fabric; cooperation, table talk, shared experience (co-op, social deduction, negotiation).
6. **Discovery** — exploring an unknown (legacy unlocks, variable setup, hidden info).
7. **Expression** — building/showing something that's *yours* (tableau engines, deck construction, asymmetric play).
8. **Submission** — pleasant pastime; the low-stakes mindless-fun groove (fillers, gateway games).

Why this matters: **a mechanic that serves your target fun is good; the same mechanic serving a different target is noise.** Heavy optimization (Challenge) bolted onto a party game (Fellowship/Submission) ruins it. Name your 1–2 target funs in Phase 1 and audit every mechanic against them — cut what doesn't serve them (conviction 5, compression, has an emotional version too).

## Theme integration (the depth ladder)
"Theme" isn't binary. Place your design on this ladder consciously:
- **Pasted-on:** theme is art + flavor text; mechanics would be identical with any other skin. (Many older euros.) Legitimate but leaves memorability and teachability on the table.
- **Evocative:** theme is chosen to *resonate* with the mechanics even if not simulated (Azul's tile-laying *feels* like mosaic work).
- **Integrated:** the mechanic *is* the thing it represents — Pandemic's spreading cubes feel like contagion; Wingspan's engine feels like a thriving aviary; Bohnanza's hand-order restriction feels like a stubborn bean farmer. **This is the high-value target.** When the mechanic and theme are the same idea, the theme *carries the rules in the player's memory* (you don't memorize "discard 2 to draw 1," you remember "you trade beans"), the game teaches faster, and it's more memorable.

The failure mode (conviction 7) is the *incoherent middle*: a theme that promises an experience (epic space war!) the mechanics don't deliver (spreadsheet of cube conversions). Either deepen the integration or commit to a clean abstract — both are fine; the mismatch is not. Pure abstracts (Chess, Hive, Azul-as-abstract) opt out of this ladder entirely and that's a legitimate, even elegant, choice — just don't paste on a theme the mechanics ignore.

## The emotional arc of a session
Games are experienced *over time*; design the shape of that time, not just the average. A flat experience (every turn feels the same) is forgettable even if balanced. Tools:
- **Rising tension toward the end.** The endgame should feel different from the opening — stakes up, options narrowing, the race tightening. Engine builders do this naturally (slow→cascade); make sure yours doesn't *deflate* into mechanical point-counting (engine-building failure mode).
- **Beats and texture.** Not every decision should be agonizing (that's exhausting and causes AP). Alternate hard strategic choices with easy tactical ones to give players breathing room — "turn angst" in moderation, not constant teeth-gnashing (see the downtime/AP discussion).
- **Memorable moments ("swingy spikes").** The story players retell afterward is almost never "I optimized efficiently"; it's "the tomb collapsed on three of us at once." Design *for* the anecdote — moments of big reveal, reversal, or triumph. (This is partly why the luck debate exists: swing manufactures stories. Thematic games lean in; euros find quieter spikes like a perfectly-timed scoring round.)
- **The opening and the ending are special.** First impressions (the first real decision should arrive fast and feel meaningful) and last impressions (a satisfying climax and a clean resolution, not a fizzle) carry disproportionate weight in how the game is remembered.

## How to actually use this layer
1. **In Phase 1**, write the target aesthetic(s) from the 8-kinds list and the intended emotional arc, alongside the central decision. This is as important as the mechanic and is the thing the rest of the design serves.
2. **When a game tests as "fine but flat,"** the problem is almost always here, not in the mechanics: the mechanics work but serve no clear felt-experience, or the session has no arc. Diagnose by asking playtesters "what's the story of that game?" — if they can't tell one, you have an aesthetics problem.
3. **When choosing between two mechanically-equal options,** pick the one that better serves the target fun and the theme integration. That tiebreaker is where good games separate from competent ones.
4. **Resist the systems-designer trap:** a beautiful, balanced machine that evokes nothing. Elegance is necessary, not sufficient. The machine has to make someone *feel* something.
