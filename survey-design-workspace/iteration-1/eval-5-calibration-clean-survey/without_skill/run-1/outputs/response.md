# Survey Review

Your exit survey is well-constructed overall. It's concise, logically ordered, and the post-task placement is a sound choice for avoiding demand characteristics. Here are my observations:

## Strengths

- **Post-task placement** is appropriate. Administering the survey after the task avoids priming effects on behavior.
- **Logical ordering** makes sense: overall satisfaction → task difficulty → specific confusion points → future intent. This flows naturally for respondents.
- **Anonymous self-administration** reduces social desirability bias.
- **Item 3 (open text)** is well-designed — the "leave blank if none" instruction prevents forced responses and distinguishes genuine absence of confusion from non-response.
- **5-point Likert-style scales** are appropriate for this type of attitudinal measurement.

## Issues to Address

### 1. Item 1 and Item 2 may be partially redundant — but that's mostly fine
"Overall experience" and "task difficulty" are related but distinct constructs. Difficulty is one component of experience. Keeping both is reasonable if you want to separate hedonic/affective evaluation from perceived effort, but be aware they will likely correlate. This is worth noting if you plan to treat them as independent variables in analysis.

### 2. Response scale anchoring on Item 1 could be more neutral at the midpoint
"Fair" as the midpoint of the experience scale is slightly negative in connotation — "fair" typically implies something mediocre or below expectation. Consider "Neither poor nor good" or "Acceptable" for a more neutral middle anchor, consistent with the explicit neutral labels used in Items 2 and 4.

### 3. Item 4 assumes the participant is a researcher
"In your research" is embedded in the future-use item. If some participants are not researchers (e.g., practitioners, students not conducting studies), this assumption could feel alienating or confusing. Consider "in your work or studies" or simply removing the qualifier ("How likely are you to use this type of tool again?").

### 4. No item measures what the tool was supposed to do well
The survey captures satisfaction, difficulty, confusion, and reuse intent, but not perceived utility or task success. Consider adding one item along the lines of: "How well did the tool support you in completing the task?" This would give you a more complete picture of the user experience and help distinguish between cases where something was easy-but-useless vs. difficult-but-valuable.

### 5. Randomization consideration
You mention not randomizing because of "logical topic order." That reasoning is sound for item order (it would be disorienting to shuffle these four items). However, if you have multiple parallel versions of any items (e.g., different phrasings you're comparing), within-item response option order is sometimes randomized on unipolar scales. For these fully-anchored bipolar scales, fixed order (negative to positive, or positive to negative — just be consistent) is standard and appropriate. No action needed unless you expand the survey.

## Summary

The survey is well-thought-out and avoids many common pitfalls (no leading questions, appropriate timing, no unnecessary items). The main actionable suggestions are:
1. Revisit the "Fair" midpoint anchor on Item 1.
2. Remove or broaden the "in your research" assumption in Item 4.
3. Consider adding a perceived utility item to round out the measurement model.
