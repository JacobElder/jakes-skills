## Problem 1: The 2-Point Scale for Attitude Items

A 2-point Agree/Disagree scale (sometimes called a dichotomous item) is one of the worst formats you can choose for measuring attitudes — not a minor stylistic tradeoff.

**What goes wrong:**

- **You throw away real variance.** Reliability and validity rise as you add response points and plateau around 5–7 (Krosnick & Presser 2010; Alwin & Krosnick 1991). Below 4 points you're discarding genuine differences in how strongly people hold their views. Two categories — Agree or Disagree — forces someone who "slightly agrees" and someone who "strongly agrees" to look identical in your data.

- **Acquiescence is fully undetectable.** Agree/Disagree formats already invite acquiescence bias — the tendency to agree regardless of content, which is stronger among lower-education respondents and in high-deference cultures. With 5 or 7 points you can partially detect and balance this. With 2 points there is no room for that at all; you cannot tell whether someone endorsed the item because they agree with its content or simply because "Agree" is always an easy button to press.

- **The format is already suboptimal at 5 points, and you're going further.** Research directly comparing agree/disagree scale formats (Revilla, Saris & Krosnick 2014) found that 5-category AD scales outperform 7 and 11 on measurement quality. Two points is well below the floor of that evidence base.

- **"Seen in older surveys" is not evidence it works.** Binary agree/disagree was common in early polling before the psychometric literature caught up. It is not a contemporary best practice.

**The fix:**

For attitude items, the two best routes are:

1. **Item-specific rating scale (preferred):** Instead of a statement + Agree/Disagree, reframe the question directly. For example, instead of "I find the product easy to use — Agree / Disagree," ask "How easy is the product to use?" with a 5-point scale: *Very difficult / Somewhat difficult / Neither easy nor difficult / Somewhat easy / Very easy.* This eliminates the agreement dimension entirely, which removes acquiescence as a confound and typically yields higher data quality (Saris et al. 2010).

2. **5-point Agree/Disagree if you prefer the statement format:** Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neither agree nor disagree / Agree / Strongly agree. Label every point with words — not numbers alone. This is the evidence-backed minimum for AD-style items.

If you have several attitude items, I'd strongly recommend converting them to item-specific scales. It takes a little more upfront work but produces clearly better data.

## Problem 2: "Select All That Apply" for Product Features

"Select all that apply" (check-all) is a popular format and I understand why it feels intuitive — let people check everything relevant. The problem is what it does to respondent behavior.

**What goes wrong:**

- **Satisficing causes early stopping.** When asked to check all that apply, many respondents check a few options that feel true and stop — not because nothing else applies, but because the task of evaluating every option is effortful, and once they have "enough" checked, the cognitive pressure to continue drops. This means items appearing later in the list will be systematically under-endorsed (Smyth et al. 2006).

- **Answers are not comparable across respondents.** One person may have checked 3 items after careful review; another checked 3 after glancing at the first few. You cannot tell the difference from the data. The endorsement rates you get are systematically compressed and biased toward the top of the list.

- **You lose the ability to distinguish "does not apply" from "skipped."** A blank cell in check-all data could mean "this feature is not relevant to me" or "I stopped reading." In a forced-choice format, you know.

**The fix:**

Use a **forced-choice yes/no grid**: present each product feature as a row, with "Yes" and "No" as the two column options, and require a response for every row. This format:

- Ensures respondents evaluate every feature, not just the first few
- Produces comparable data across respondents (each gets the same decision task)
- Generates more endorsements overall, because the non-evaluation default is eliminated

The tradeoff is that it takes more respondent effort than check-all. If your feature list is very long (more than 8–10 items), consider trimming it or splitting it into a shorter screener step rather than presenting a very long grid, which creates its own fatigue problem.
