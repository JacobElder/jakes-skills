# 5-Point vs 7-Point Likert Scales: Which Should You Use?

The "more is always better" claim is wrong — but so is a blanket preference for either number. The right answer depends on two things: **what the construct is** and **what format you're using**. Here's how to decide.

---

## The core finding from the research

Reliability and validity do rise as you add scale points — but only up to a point. Below about 4 options you're throwing away real variance (coarse measurement biases your estimates). Gains flatten around **5–7 points**. Beyond roughly 7, the marginal information is small, you can no longer label all the points cleanly, and quality often *drops* (Krosnick & Presser 2010; Alwin & Krosnick 1991).

So the range 5–7 is genuinely the sweet spot. The question is which end of that range fits your situation.

---

## The key decision: is your construct bipolar or unipolar?

This single distinction drives most of the 5-vs-7 choice.

**Bipolar constructs** have a meaningful opposite at each end — agree/disagree, favor/oppose, good/bad. The midpoint is a genuine neutral position, not just "not much." Examples: attitude toward a policy, agreement with a statement, semantic differential pairs (warm–cold, strong–weak).

- Use **7 points** for bipolar items. You need room on both sides of the neutral midpoint. With only 5 points, you're squeezing two positions below neutral and two above, which is workable but tight for respondents who genuinely make fine distinctions.

**Unipolar constructs** run from "none/not at all" to "a lot," with no meaningful negative end. Examples: satisfaction, frequency, confidence, intensity of a feeling. There's no true opposite; the low end is essentially zero.

- Use **5 points** for unipolar items. A 7-point unipolar scale creates phantom distinctions at the low end that respondents struggle to differentiate — it adds apparent precision without real information.

---

## The exception for agree/disagree specifically

If you're using agree/disagree (Likert-type) statements rather than item-specific rating scales, **use 5, not 7 or 11**. A multimethod study by Revilla, Saris & Krosnick (2014) found that 5-category agree/disagree scales produced *higher* measurement quality than 7- or 11-category versions. This is counterintuitive, but agree/disagree already imposes extra cognitive burden (respondents must decode the statement, then evaluate their agreement level), and more points compound the difficulty without adding useful signal.

---

## Other moderators worth knowing

**Population matters.** If you're surveying children, lower-literacy respondents, or anyone in a low-motivation context (short attention span, high burden), lean toward 4–5 points. Fewer points with clear labels is easier to use well than more points with murky labels.

**Labeling and point count are linked.** You can fully label 5 points easily, 7 points with effort, and rarely more than that. "More points" and "fewer or weaker labels" tend to arrive together — and unlabeled points reduce data quality because respondents have to invent verbal meanings for bare numbers. A 7-point scale with every point labeled beats a 7-point scale with only endpoint labels. If you can't label all points clearly, don't add the points.

**Match resolution to real cognitive distinctions.** More scale points only help if respondents actually make finer distinctions. For most everyday attitudes, people have maybe 5 meaningfully different positions. Pushing to 9 or 11 points typically redistributes responses into the same psychological buckets with more noise.

---

## Practical recommendation

| Situation | Use |
|---|---|
| Bipolar attitude (agree/disagree, favor/oppose, good/bad) | **5 points** (not 7 or 11) |
| Bipolar adjective pairs / semantic differential | **7 points** |
| Unipolar rating (satisfaction, frequency, intensity) | **5 points** |
| Expert raters or respondents who genuinely make fine distinctions | **7 points** |
| Children or low-literacy respondents | **4–5 points** |
| Single global rating (life satisfaction, NPS-style) | 0–10 is an acceptable exception |

Whatever you choose, **label every point with words**, not just the endpoints. Fully verbal labels give each point a shared meaning, reduce response burden, and consistently improve data quality — especially for lower-education respondents.

---

## Bottom line

"More points is always better" is a myth. Gains plateau at 5–7; beyond that you add cognitive burden without adding information. For most Likert-type attitude items — especially agree/disagree batteries — **5 points is the empirically stronger choice**. Reserve 7 for bipolar constructs like semantic differentials where respondents genuinely need room at both poles, and where you can still label each point distinctly.
