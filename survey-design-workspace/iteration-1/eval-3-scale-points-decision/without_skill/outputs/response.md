# 5-Point vs. 7-Point Likert Scales: What the Evidence Says

The "more points is always better" claim is an oversimplification. The research is actually fairly nuanced here, and the right answer depends on your context. Here's the honest picture:

## The Core Trade-off

**More scale points = more variance captured, but with diminishing returns and real costs.**

## What the Research Shows

### Reliability peaks around 5–7 points
The psychometric literature (e.g., Krosnick & Fabrigar, 1997; Preston & Colman, 2000) consistently finds that reliability and validity tend to peak somewhere in the 5–7 point range. Below 5, you're losing meaningful variance. Above 7, respondents struggle to reliably distinguish adjacent categories — the extra points become noise rather than signal.

Preston & Colman (2000) directly tested 2- through 11-point scales and found 7-point scales had the highest levels of reliability, validity, and discriminating power, with 5-point scales close behind.

### The difference between 5 and 7 is often small
In practice, 5-point and 7-point scales tend to produce very similar results after analysis. Correlations between constructs measured on either scale are usually nearly identical. If your goal is detecting relationships between variables, the choice rarely changes your conclusions.

### When 7-point has a real advantage
- **Continuous constructs** with genuine gradient: attitude strength, emotion intensity, agreement along a spectrum
- **When you need fine-grained discrimination** (e.g., tracking small changes over time in a longitudinal study)
- **When your sample is large enough** to make use of the added variance

### When 5-point is the better choice
- **Cognitive burden matters**: Older respondents, low-literacy populations, or long surveys where fatigue is a concern
- **Mobile surveys**: Fitting 7 response options clearly on a phone screen is genuinely harder
- **Speed is a constraint**: 5-point scales are slightly faster to complete
- **Simple, binary-adjacent constructs**: Some attitudes really only have 5 meaningful positions

## The "Neutral" Point Question

Both 5-point and 7-point scales typically include a midpoint (3 or 4). If you're worried about acquiescence bias or people defaulting to the middle, this is a separate design decision from the number of points — you can force-choose by dropping the midpoint on either scale.

## Practical Recommendation

- **Default to 5-point** if you're building a general survey, have a mobile audience, or are replicating existing validated measures that used 5-point scales.
- **Use 7-point** if you're measuring attitudes along a clear continuum and need to detect subtle differences, or if your field's standard instruments use 7-point scales (common in psychology and organizational research).
- **Don't use 10-point or 11-point scales** for Likert-style items — those work better for rating scales (e.g., satisfaction, pain) where respondents have strong numeric intuitions.

## Bottom Line

The empirical answer is: **5 and 7 are both defensible; the gap between them is small.** Pick based on your population, mode of administration, and whether you're matching an established instrument. The claim that "more is always better" stops being true around 7 points — beyond that, you're adding cognitive load without gaining meaningful data quality.
