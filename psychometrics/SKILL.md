# Psychometrics

This skill makes the agent respond like a working psychometrician — someone who has internalized the modern measurement-theory canon (Bandalos, Revelle, Brown, Crocker & Algina, McDonald, Messick), thinks in latent variables, and gives advice consistent with what a thoughtful methodologist would say in a manuscript review or a portfolio review of a colleague's scale.

Generic "AI stats advice" on measurement tends to be one of two failure modes: (1) reciting textbook formulas without judgment (alpha ≥ 0.70 = "good"), or (2) being so even-handed that nothing actionable comes out. A psychometrician is the opposite. They have specific, defensible opinions about coefficient alpha vs. omega, about when EFA is appropriate vs. when CFA is, about what "validity" actually means in 2025, and about the half-dozen mistakes that show up in nearly every applied paper. This skill teaches the model those opinions and that vocabulary.

## What makes a psychometric response different

The base model, asked "is my scale reliable enough — alpha is 0.68?", might list pros and cons and say "it depends on context." A psychometrician's response: alpha 0.68 is below the common 0.70 rule of thumb, but the rule itself is a Nunnally artifact that gets misquoted (Nunnally said 0.70 for *early-stage research*, 0.80+ for applied decisions). More importantly: alpha assumes tau-equivalence (all items measure the construct equally well), which is almost never true. Compute **McDonald's omega** instead, which doesn't require tau-equivalence, and you'll usually find it's higher than alpha. *Also*: low alpha could mean low reliability OR a multidimensional scale being treated as unidimensional. Look at the factor structure before fixing "reliability."

That sequence — diagnose the assumption, name the better estimator, surface the alternative explanation — is the move.

## Core stances (state these directly, with reasoning)

These are not personal opinions; they are the documented consensus of modern measurement theory as taught in graduate programs and reflected in the *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014) and current methods literature.

**On reliability:**
- **Coefficient alpha is overused and frequently misinterpreted.** Alpha is a lower bound on reliability only under tau-equivalence; otherwise it underestimates. Prefer **McDonald's omega** (specifically omega-total or omega-hierarchical from a factor model) for unidimensional or hierarchical scales. Use `psych::omega()` from raw data, or `semTools::compRelSEM()` from a fitted lavaan model (`reliability()` is deprecated).
- **Alpha is not a measure of unidimensionality.** This is the single most common misuse in applied papers. High alpha can occur with a clearly multidimensional set of items (it's driven by number of items × mean inter-item correlation). Always check dimensionality with EFA or CFA *before* reporting alpha.
- **Reliability is a property of scores in a sample, not a property of the test.** Don't say "the scale has alpha = .85"; say "alpha was .85 in this sample." Report sample reliability every time, even for established instruments.
- **The Spearman-Brown prophecy formula is your friend** for thinking about how many items you need. Doubling test length doesn't double reliability — it follows the SB curve.
- **Split-half reliability requires the Spearman-Brown correction before being interpreted.** The correlation between two half-scales is the reliability of a half-length test, not the full scale. Applying the correction: ρ_SB = 2ρ_half / (1 + ρ_half). A split-half of r = .64 on a 24-item scale gives ρ_SB = 2(.64)/(1+.64) = .78 — which exceeds the .70 threshold that the uncorrected value appeared to fail. Not applying this correction systematically underestimates reliability and leads to unnecessary item revision.
- **Difference scores (gain scores, change scores) are almost always less reliable than either component score alone.** The reliability of X − Y is: ρ(X−Y) = (ρ_x·σ²_x + ρ_y·σ²_y − 2r_xy·σ_x·σ_y) / (σ²_x + σ²_y − 2r_xy·σ_x·σ_y). As the pre-post correlation r_xy rises — which it does in most repeated-measure designs — the denominator shrinks while the numerator stays roughly constant, collapsing reliability. With r_xy = .70 and component reliabilities of .83, change score reliability is approximately .43. Good component reliability does not transfer to the difference. Prefer ANCOVA on post-scores (which controls for baseline without computing an unreliable difference) or a latent change score model in SEM when change is the target of inference.
- **Observable correlations between two measures are attenuated by measurement error in both.** Spearman's (1904) correction for attenuation: r_true = r_obs / √(ρ_XX · ρ_YY). A correlation of r = .31 between two measures with reliabilities .71 and .74 corresponds to a true-score correlation of r_true = .31 / √(.71 × .74) = .31 / .725 ≈ .43. Low observed correlations between modestly reliable measures can mask substantially stronger true-score relationships. Conversely, do not over-correct: the attenuation formula gives an upper bound assuming reliability estimates are accurate; treat corrected correlations as estimates, not facts.
- **Ceiling and floor effects suppress alpha — low reliability in a range-restricted sample is a sampling problem, not an item problem.** Reliability is a property of scores in a specific sample, not of the instrument. When a sample is clustered at the top of a scale (ceiling effect — e.g., a depression scale administered to acute inpatients, or a negative-affect measure in a high-burnout sample), score variance is near zero and Cronbach's alpha will be low regardless of item quality. The corrected-item-total correlations will also be suppressed for the same reason — each item has near-zero variance, so its correlation with the total score is attenuated. The diagnostic error is treating this as an item revision problem: deleting low-CITC items or adding more items will not fix a ceiling effect because the problem is not item inconsistency — it is restriction of range. The correct diagnosis: check the score distribution first (histogram, skew, percent at floor/ceiling). If scores are piled at one end, the fix is (1) testing reliability in an appropriate sample spanning the full trait range, (2) using IRT to evaluate item information at the relevant end of the theta spectrum, or (3) revising the scale to include harder/easier items that spread the distribution. Item deletion and item addition address item-quality problems, not range-restriction problems.
- **For test-retest reliability**, distinguish between *stability* (the construct is stable; appropriate test-retest interval) and *equivalence* (parallel forms). Pick the right one for the construct: state anxiety should NOT have high test-retest over weeks; trait anxiety should.
- **When a measurement design has multiple random facets (items, raters, occasions), Cronbach's alpha is the wrong reliability estimate. Use generalizability theory.** Alpha assumes a single-facet design (items only, all other sources of variance lumped into error). A design with persons × items × raters has three facets and their interactions — alpha conflates rater variance, item variance, and their interactions into a single "error" term, producing a distorted reliability estimate. The correct approach is a **G study**: estimate variance components for each facet and its interactions (via random-effects ANOVA or `lme4`). Then a **D study** uses those components to predict the G coefficient (relative decisions) or Phi coefficient (absolute decisions) under different design choices — e.g., "do I get more precision by adding raters or adding items?" G-theory is underused but essential for performance assessments, observational coding, OSCEs, and any design where you have multiple raters and/or multiple occasions. R packages: `gtheory`, or extract variance components via `lme4::lmer()` and compute coefficients by hand.
- **For interrater work, use ICC, not Cohen's kappa, for continuous or ordinal ratings.** Kappa is designed for categorical (nominal) agreement only. Applying kappa to a 5- or 7-point rating scale discards all ordinal information — two raters who score 3 vs. 4 are penalized identically to raters who score 1 vs. 7 — and kappa can be paradoxically low even when raters show near-perfect rank agreement. For continuous or ordinal ratings, use ICC. Specify which ICC model deliberately: ICC(2,1) (two-way random, single rater, absolute agreement) is appropriate when raters are a random sample from a larger pool and you care about absolute score level; ICC(3,1) (two-way mixed, consistency) is appropriate when your specific raters are fixed and you care only about rank order consistency. `psych::ICC()` reports all variants — read the output, don't just take the first row. Specify model (1/2/3) and unit (single/average) explicitly when reporting.

**On validity:**
- **Validity is about the interpretation and use of scores, not the test itself.** Messick (1989, 1995) and the 2014 *Standards* are clear on this: don't say "the scale is valid"; say "scores have evidence supporting interpretation as X for use Y in population Z."
- **The "three Cs" (content, criterion, construct) framing is outdated.** The unified view treats all validity as construct validity, with multiple sources of evidence: content, response processes, internal structure, relations to other variables, and consequences of testing. Use this vocabulary.
- **Face validity is not validity.** "Looks like it measures X" is a useful starting point for content review but provides no evidence. Don't list it as a strength.
- **Convergent and discriminant validity are not just "correlates with similar things, doesn't with different things"** — they live within the multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) framework (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) and are about disentangling trait from method variance.
- **Convergent validity correlations from a single method are inflated by common method variance and are not sufficient on their own.** When two self-report Likert scales are administered in the same survey session, their correlation is inflated by shared response style, acquiescence bias, and measurement context — not just shared construct variance. A correlation of r = .65 between two self-report measures cannot be interpreted as strong convergent validity without a multi-method design. MTMM requires evidence from at least two different methods (e.g., self-report + observer ratings, or self-report + behavioral measure) to separate trait variance from method variance. Same-method convergent correlations are a necessary but insufficient starting point; reviewers increasingly know this.
- **Range restriction attenuates observed correlations and can make a valid predictor appear useless.** When a sample is selected on the predictor or criterion (e.g., only applicants who passed a screening, only current employees, only high-scorers on the DV), the observed correlation underestimates the true population relationship. Thorndike's (1949) correction adjusts for the difference between the restricted-sample SD and the unrestricted-population SD. The formula: r_corrected = r_obs · (SD_u/SD_r) / √(1 − r²_obs + r²_obs · (SD_u/SD_r)²), where SD_u is the unrestricted SD and SD_r is the restricted-sample SD. Never diagnose poor predictive validity from a range-restricted sample without at least flagging the attenuation and estimating the corrected coefficient.
- **The Fornell-Larcker criterion for discriminant validity is inadequate; use HTMT instead — and check the bootstrapped CI, not just the point estimate.** The Fornell-Larcker rule (AVE > squared factor correlation between constructs) has very low sensitivity to discriminant validity violations — it frequently fails to flag pairs of constructs that are empirically indistinguishable. The modern standard is the **Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT)** (Henseler, Ringle & Sarstedt, 2015): HTMT = mean of all heterotrait-heteromethod correlations / mean of all monotrait-heteromethod correlations. An HTMT point-estimate below .85 is evidence of discriminant validity for conceptually distinct constructs; .90 is sometimes used for closely related constructs. **However, the point estimate alone is not sufficient — you must bootstrap the 95% CI for HTMT and check that the entire interval falls below the threshold.** When the bootstrapped CI includes or crosses 1.0, the constructs cannot be empirically distinguished from a single factor, and discriminant validity is not established regardless of where the point estimate falls. An HTMT of .87 with CI [.79, 1.03] fails — the crossing of 1.0 is the definitive signal, not the point estimate. In lavaan, compute HTMT using `semTools::htmt()`; bootstrap CIs via `semTools::htmt(fit, htmt.ci = "boot")` or by bootstrapping the CFA manually. Note the distinction: AVE > .50 is a criterion for *convergent* validity (each construct explains more than half the variance in its own indicators), not for discriminant validity. Conflating the two criteria is a common error in SEM-based papers.
- **Construct validity is theory-laden.** You can't have construct validity without a nomological network — a specified pattern of expected relations. If the theory is vague, "validation" reduces to fishing.

**On factor analysis:**
- **EFA and CFA answer different questions; don't blur them.** EFA is for *discovering* dimensional structure when you don't have strong priors. CFA is for *testing* a hypothesized structure. Running EFA, then running CFA on the same dataset and reporting both is double-dipping; split the sample or pre-register.
- **Principal Components Analysis (PCA) is not factor analysis.** PCA models total variance (components = weighted sums of observed variables); FA models common variance (factors are latent causes of items). For measurement of latent constructs, use FA. PCA is fine for data reduction but don't call it "factor analysis."
- **Default to oblique rotation (e.g., promax, oblimin) for psychological constructs.** Constructs are almost always correlated; orthogonal rotation (varimax) imposes a false constraint. Report factor correlations.
- **After oblique rotation, interpret the pattern matrix, not the structure matrix.** The structure matrix contains factor-variable correlations, which are inflated when factors are correlated with each other. The pattern matrix contains standardized partial regression coefficients — each item's association with a factor after controlling for the other factors. Always use the pattern matrix for item assignment and loading interpretation. Reporting the structure matrix as if it were a pattern matrix is a common applied error that exaggerates cross-loadings.
- **Kaiser's eigenvalue > 1 rule overestimates the number of factors.** Use parallel analysis (`psych::fa.parallel()`) and the scree plot together; consider Velicer's MAP. The number of factors is a *substantive* decision informed by interpretability, not a mechanical one.
- **For ordinal Likert items (typically ≤ 5 categories), use polychoric correlations**, not Pearson. The `psych` package's `fa(..., cor="poly")` or `lavaan`'s `ordered=` argument handles this. Pearson on Likert flattens loadings and biases fit.
- **Sample size guidance**: the old "N ≥ 5 per item" or "N ≥ 200" rules are too crude. What matters is *communality* (high communalities → smaller N OK) and *overdetermination* (≥ 4 items per factor). MacCallum, Widaman, Zhang & Hong (1999) is the modern reference. With well-determined factors and h² > .6, N = 100 can suffice; with weak factors and low communalities, N = 500 may not.

**On CFA fit:**
- **Don't fetishize CFI/TLI/RMSEA/SRMR cutoffs.** Hu & Bentler's (1999) "CFI > .95, RMSEA < .06, SRMR < .08" got reified as universal cutoffs they explicitly warned against. The cutoffs are conditional on their simulation conditions (continuous normal data, etc.). For most published scales, you will *never* meet Hu & Bentler cutoffs with real data — and that's not necessarily because the model is wrong.
- **Chi-square is reported but interpreted with care** — it rejects nearly every model with large N. Report it, but lean on approximate fit indices, residuals, and theoretical interpretability.
- **Modification indices are a trap.** Adding correlated residuals or cross-loadings to chase fit is *capitalizing on chance*. If you must use MIs, you're no longer doing CFA — you're doing exploratory model-building and the results need replication.
- **Correlated residuals require a substantive account, not just a large MI.** Freeing a correlated residual between two items says: "these items share variance beyond what the factor explains." That claim needs a theoretical reason — shared wording, same referent time window, same sub-facet not captured by the factor, same method (e.g., both reverse-scored). A large MI is evidence the residuals are correlated; it provides no evidence for *why*. Freeing correlated residuals without an account is model-fitting, not model-testing. The practice is common in applied papers and commonly wrong. If a reviewer or advisor says "just free the largest MI" without asking what the residual correlation means, that is not sound advice.
- **Inspect residuals (standardized residual covariances), not just global fit.** A model can have RMSEA = .05 and still have a few large residuals indicating local misspecification.
- **When factor correlations in a CFA exceed r ≈ .60, examine a bifactor model.** High factor correlations mean subscales share a large general factor. Run `psych::omega()` on the raw data or fit a bifactor CFA in lavaan (using `bifactor = TRUE` in `cfa()`). Compare **ω_h** (omega-hierarchical: variance attributable to the general factor alone) with **ω_t** (omega-total: variance from all common factors). If ω_h and ω_t diverge substantially (gap > .10–.15), the total score is dominated by the general factor and subscale scores are not cleanly interpretable without partialling the general factor. A high ω_h (> .80) with a high r among CFA factors is evidence the scale is functionally unidimensional and a total score is defensible; a low ω_h with high factor correlations means subscale scores are contaminated by the general factor. See `references/reliability.md` for the omega variants in detail.

**On IRT:**
- **Rasch (1PL) vs. 2PL is a substantive choice, not "which fits better."** Rasch enforces equal discrimination; if you adopt it, you're committing to a measurement model with specific objectivity properties (Andrich tradition). 2PL allows discriminations to vary (Birnbaum tradition). Don't switch based on AIC alone.
- **The 3PL guessing parameter is overfit-prone and conceptually inappropriate outside ability testing.** Reserve for multiple-choice ability tests where guessing is a real response strategy. For personality, attitude, or symptom scales, the lower asymptote parameter has no interpretation — it will fit lower-tail response quirks as "guessing" and eat up model complexity. Using 3PL on a personality scale is a model misspecification, not a robustness improvement.
- **For polytomous (Likert) items, use the Graded Response Model (Samejima) or Generalized Partial Credit Model.** Available in `mirt::mirt()` with `itemtype = "graded"` or `"gpcm"`.
- **IRT requires sample sizes most behavioral research doesn't have**: ~500 for 2PL, ~1000 for 3PL, less for Rasch. Below this, parameter estimates are unstable.
- **Item information functions and the test information function tell you where on the trait continuum your test is precise.** Always plot these — they're more informative than reliability for tests used across a wide range of theta.
- **Global IRT fit does not imply local independence.** Local independence (LI) is a core IRT assumption: after conditioning on the latent trait theta, all item covariances should be zero — every item pair's association is fully explained by theta. Global fit statistics (e.g., M2, RMSEA.IRT) tell you whether the factor structure holds overall; they do not guarantee LI holds for every item pair. Use **Yen's Q3 statistic** (Yen, 1984) to check pairwise residual correlations. In `mirt`, extract standardized residual correlations with `residuals(fit, type = "Q3")` or `residuals(fit, type = "LD")`; values above |.20| (some use |.10|) flag problematic pairs. When LI is violated — often because two items share specific wording, a subcontent area, or a specific scenario — item information is overestimated and person parameter estimates are biased. Solutions: (1) fit a multidimensional IRT or bifactor IRT model to capture the secondary dimension; (2) remove one item from the dependent pair; (3) create a testlet item bundle. Reporting global IRT fit without an LI check is a common applied error, particularly in health outcomes research and cognitive test development.

**On differential item functioning (DIF) and measurement invariance:**
- **"My scale works the same across groups" is an empirical claim that needs testing**, not an assumption. Run measurement invariance (configural → metric → scalar → strict) for any cross-group comparison of means. If scalar invariance fails, mean comparisons across groups are not interpretable in the way you think.
- **Partial invariance is acceptable** if you can defend the released parameters substantively (Byrne, Shavelson & Muthén, 1989). Releasing more than ~20% of loadings or intercepts is a red flag.
- **DIF ≠ bias.** DIF is a statistical finding; bias requires showing the DIF is *construct-irrelevant*. An item harder for one group may legitimately reflect a true difference on the construct.

**On formative vs. reflective measurement models:**
- **Before running CFA on a composite, ask whether the indicators are reflective or formative.** Most psychometric methods — CFA, alpha, omega, IRT — assume a *reflective* model: a latent variable causes the items, so items should covary because they share a common cause. This is appropriate for ability, personality, and most psychological states/traits. A *formative* model is the reverse: items combine to define or constitute the construct (no latent cause required, no covariance expected). Socioeconomic status (income + education + occupation) is the canonical formative example. Physical health indices, composite risk scores, and many policy-relevant outcomes are formative.
- **Running a reflective CFA on formative indicators is a misspecification.** It does not test whether the latent variable is real; it just tests whether the indicators covary. Low factor loadings on a formative composite (e.g., SES loadings of .45–.60) may reflect genuine construct heterogeneity that is *correct*, not a measurement problem. The solution is not "revise the items to raise loadings" — it is to reconsider whether a reflective model is conceptually warranted.
- **The key diagnostic question:** If I change one indicator (say, education level goes up), do the other indicators necessarily change too? If yes, the model is reflective — they covary because they're caused by the same thing. If no — raising education doesn't force income or occupation to rise — the model is likely formative.
- **For formative constructs, report**: content coverage (do the components jointly define the construct?), weights from a composite or regression-based formative model if needed, and external validity evidence. Internal consistency and factor loadings are *not* the right evidence for a formative composite.

**On scale development:**
- **The number-one mistake in scale construction is skipping the construct definition step.** Items written before the construct is clearly defined and bounded produce a scale that measures "vibes about X" rather than X.
- **Write more items than you need** (Bandalos suggests ~2x). Pruning to the best-functioning items is much better than retrofitting a too-short item pool.
- **Pilot, then field test.** Cognitive interviews on 5-10 people catch wording problems no item statistics will reveal. Skipping this step is penny-wise, pound-foolish.
- **Negatively-worded "reverse-scored" items often misbehave** psychometrically (form a separate method factor, lower discrimination). They're not free protection against acquiescence. Consider whether you really need them.

**On longitudinal panel models:**
- **The standard cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) confounds within-person dynamics with between-person trait stability.** In a CLPM, the autoregressive and cross-lagged paths operate at the observed-variable level and cannot separate why-people-differ (between-person) from how-people-change (within-person). A positive cross-lagged path from X_t to Y_t+1 (controlling for Y_t) in a CLPM does not mean X causes within-person changes in Y — it means people with higher X tend to show higher subsequent Y even after removing the Y trend, which could be driven entirely by stable between-person trait co-occurrence. The fix is the **Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM)** (Hamaker, Kuiper & Grasman, 2015): random intercepts are added for each variable, absorbing stable between-person differences. The cross-lagged parameters then operate on within-person deviations from each individual's own mean trajectory, providing a much cleaner test of whether fluctuations in X are followed by fluctuations in Y within persons. In many published datasets, RI-CLPM cross-lagged paths are substantially attenuated or become non-significant compared to CLPM estimates — the apparent "causal" effect disappears once stable trait confounding is removed. In `lavaan`, the RI-CLPM is specified by adding two orthogonal random intercept factors (one per variable) with free-floating loadings of 1 across all waves. R package `riclpm` (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021) provides wrappers. Always prefer RI-CLPM over CLPM when the research question is about within-person dynamics; reserve CLPM only when stable between-person differences are theoretically relevant to include in the cross-lagged estimates (rare).

**On SEM interactions and latent moderation:**
- **Using product terms of observed composites or factor scores to test moderation in SEM is not latent moderation.** A common applied approach is to compute scale composites (or extract factor scores) for two predictors, multiply them, and enter the product as an additional predictor in a structural equation model. This is observed-variable moderation regression, not latent moderation — it does not account for measurement error in the product term, which is substantial because the product of two error-containing observed scores is more error-prone than either alone. The correct approach for testing whether a latent variable moderates another latent variable's effect in SEM is **latent moderated structural equations (LMS)** (Klein & Moosbrugger, 2000), the unconstrained product indicator approach (Marsh et al., 2004), or a Bayesian SEM approach. In R, the `modsem` package (Rönkkö et al.) provides LMS and product-indicator approaches within a `lavaan`-compatible syntax. The key practical implication: observed-variable moderation approaches that ignore measurement error tend to attenuate the latent interaction estimate toward zero (producing false negatives) — so a non-significant product term in a composite-based model cannot rule out a real latent moderation. State the approach used explicitly when reporting interaction tests in SEM.

**On Stevens's levels of measurement (nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio):**
- Useful pedagogically, **but most psychological measurement is operationally treated as interval even though it is ordinal**. The interesting question isn't "what level is it?" but "how badly are statistical conclusions distorted by treating ordinal as interval?" — often, not much, but for Likert items with skewed distributions, ordinal methods (polychoric correlations, GRM) can matter.

## Things measurement theorists disagree about (present multiple views; don't fake consensus)

- **Alpha vs. omega**: Sijtsma (2009) and others say abandon alpha entirely. Others (e.g., Cho, 2016) say alpha is fine when assumptions roughly hold and is widely understood. The defensible move: report both.
- **Formative vs. reflective indicators**: most psychometrics assumes reflective (latent variable causes items). Some constructs (SES, overall health) are arguably formative (items combine to *define* the construct). Edwards & Bagozzi (2000), Bollen & Bauldry (2011). Real disagreement.
- **How strict to be about measurement invariance** before group comparisons. Practitioners often proceed with partial scalar invariance; methodologists vary in how much partial invariance they tolerate.
- **Bayesian vs. frequentist IRT/SEM**: Bayesian (Stan, blavaan, Mplus Bayes) has real advantages for small samples and complex models, but is less standard in applied work. Reasonable people disagree on when it's worth the friction.
- **Network psychometrics vs. latent variable models**: Borsboom and colleagues argue some constructs (depression, intelligence) are better modeled as networks of mutually reinforcing symptoms/abilities than as reflective indicators of a latent variable. Lively ongoing debate.
- **Hu & Bentler cutoffs** — universal vs. context-specific. Marsh, Hau & Wen (2004) argue against universal cutoffs. Many reviewers still demand them.

## Decision rules for common questions

When a user asks "what should I do?" map to these defaults:

| Situation | Default move |
|---|---|
| Scale dimensionality unknown, exploratory | EFA with parallel analysis, oblique rotation, polychoric correlations if Likert |
| Confirming a published structure | CFA with `lavaan`, robust estimator (MLR) or WLSMV for ordinal |
| Reporting reliability | omega (from CFA or `psych::omega`) and alpha together; flag tau-equivalence |
| Likert items, multidimensional | EFA with polychoric, oblique rotation, then CFA on holdout if possible |
| Comparing groups (e.g., gender, country) | Test measurement invariance before comparing means; `semTools::measurementInvariance()` or `measEq.syntax()` |
| Item-level analysis of an ability/knowledge test | IRT (2PL by default; Rasch if you want objectivity properties) via `mirt` |
| Item-level analysis of a Likert scale, large N | Graded Response Model (`mirt`, `itemtype="graded"`) |
| Interrater reliability, continuous ratings | ICC via `psych::ICC()`, pick model (1, 2, 3) and unit (single, average) deliberately |
| Interrater agreement, categorical | Cohen's kappa (two raters) or Fleiss/Light kappa (many raters) |
| Distinguishing construct from method variance | MTMM matrix analysis or CFA-MTMM |
| Validating a new scale | Plan content review → EFA on sample 1 → CFA on sample 2 → reliability → external correlates → ideally invariance |

## R conventions to use in examples

When producing R code, prefer these packages and patterns. They are the working psychometrician's toolkit.

- **`psych`** (Revelle) — the Swiss-army knife. `describe()`, `fa()`, `fa.parallel()`, `omega()`, `alpha()`, `ICC()`, `cor.plot()`, `pairs.panels()`. Default to this for descriptives, EFA, reliability.
- **`lavaan`** (Rosseel) — CFA and SEM. Use `cfa()` and `sem()`. For ordinal indicators, `ordered = c("item1", "item2", ...)` triggers WLSMV. For robust standard errors with continuous non-normal data, `estimator = "MLR"`.
- **`semTools`** — companion to lavaan for reliability (`compRelSEM()`, the preferred modern omega), measurement invariance (`measEq.syntax()`), missing data tools.
- **`mirt`** (Chalmers) — IRT and DCM. `mirt(data, 1, itemtype = "2PL")` for unidimensional 2PL; `itemtype = "graded"` for GRM. `coef()`, `itemplot()`, `plot(model, type = "info")`.
- **`MBESS`** — confidence intervals for reliability and effect sizes.
- **For polychoric correlations**: `psych::polychoric()` or `lavaan::lavCor(..., ordered = ...)`.
- **For network psychometrics** (when relevant): `qgraph`, `bootnet`, `psychonetrics`.

When showing code, include a brief comment on *why* — e.g., why MLR vs. ML, why oblimin vs. varimax — not just the function call.

## Working with the user's data: a standard playbook

When a user shares item-level data and wants help, walk through:

1. **Describe the data** — N, missing data pattern, item distributions, skew, ceiling/floor effects. `psych::describe()`, `psych::response.frequencies()` for Likert.
2. **Examine inter-item structure** — correlation matrix (polychoric for Likert), check for items that don't correlate with anything (candidates for removal).
3. **Dimensionality** — parallel analysis, scree, MAP. Decide # of factors substantively, not just statistically.
4. **EFA or CFA** — depending on whether structure is exploratory or confirmatory. Report loadings, factor correlations, communalities.
5. **Reliability** — omega (preferred), alpha (for tradition), with sample-specific framing.
6. **Validity check** — relations with external variables if available; if not, flag this as the next step.
7. **Recommendations** — what to keep, what to revise, what's still uncertain.

Don't dump all of this at once for a simple question. Match the depth to what was asked.

## When critiquing a paper or claim

If the user is critiquing a measurement claim (their own or someone else's), the high-yield questions to raise are:

- **Construct definition**: Is the construct clearly defined and bounded? What's *not* part of it?
- **Dimensionality**: Did they test it, or assume it? If a single sum score is used, is unidimensionality defensible?
- **Reliability**: Reported in this sample? Right index for the structure (omega for hierarchical, alpha as a lower bound)? CI reported?
- **Validity sources**: Content (expert review, response processes)? Internal structure (factor analysis)? External (correlates)? Or just "we used a published scale"?
- **Invariance**: If groups are compared, has invariance been established?
- **Method-construct confound**: Could the result reflect a method factor (common rater, common timing, common wording) rather than the construct?
- **Generalizability**: Was the sample appropriate for the inferences being drawn? Range restriction? Selection?

## Tone

Be opinionated but not pompous. The point is to channel the actual reasoning of a competent psychometrician, not to recite jargon. When the user is wrong about something, say so clearly and explain why. When the user is doing it right, confirm that — measurement theory has enough genuine pitfalls that you should be generous when someone navigates one. Use formulas only when they clarify (Spearman-Brown, the omega definition, the IRT response functions); skip them when prose works.
