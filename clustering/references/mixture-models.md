# Mixture Models, LCA, and LPA

Model-based clustering: assume the data is generated by a **mixture** of probability
distributions, one per latent group, and recover the groups by estimating that model.
This buys you three things the geometric methods don't have: a **generative model**,
**per-point uncertainty** (posterior membership probabilities), and **principled model
selection** (you can compare 2 vs 3 vs 4 groups with information criteria instead of
eyeballing). The cost: you commit to distributional assumptions, and the fit is a
non-convex optimization that needs care.

## One family

Latent class analysis, latent profile analysis, Gaussian mixture models, and "finite
mixture models" are **the same machinery** — a mixture of component distributions fit by
maximum likelihood — wearing different clothes for different research communities. The
differences that matter are:

1. **What distribution each component is** (Gaussian for continuous indicators,
   Bernoulli/multinomial for categorical ones), and
2. **The research culture** around them (exploratory ML segmentation vs. confirmatory,
   theory-driven, fit-statistic-heavy social-science modeling).

The mathematical distinction between "LPA" and "a Gaussian mixture model" is essentially
nil. The label tells you which literature someone trained in, not what they computed.
Saying this out loud prevents a lot of confusion when a psychologist and an ML engineer
discover they've built the same model and called it different things.

## Naming map

| Term | Indicator type | Same thing as |
|---|---|---|
| Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) | continuous | LPA; "Gaussian finite mixture model"; mclust's model |
| Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) | continuous | GMM |
| Latent Class Analysis (LCA) | categorical (binary/multinomial) | "binomial/multinomial finite mixture model" |
| Finite Mixture Model (FMM) | any (a component distribution per type) | the umbrella term for all the above |
| Mixture model / model-based clustering | any | FMM |
| Latent Transition Analysis (LTA) | categorical, over time | longitudinal LCA |
| Growth Mixture Model (GMM — collision!) | continuous trajectories | mixture of growth curves |

Watch the acronym collision: in the longitudinal/SEM world "GMM" often means **Growth
Mixture Model**, not Gaussian Mixture Model. Disambiguate explicitly when the context is
trajectories over time.

## GMM

**Mechanism.** Model the data as a weighted sum of k multivariate Gaussians. Each
component has a mixing weight π, a mean μ, and a covariance Σ. Fit by Expectation-
Maximization: E-step computes each point's posterior probability of belonging to each
component ("responsibilities"); M-step updates π, μ, Σ given those soft assignments.
Final clustering: assign each point to its highest-posterior component (or keep the soft
memberships).

**Why it's "soft k-means done honestly."** k-means is the limiting case of a GMM where
every component has identical spherical covariance (σ²I) and assignments are hardened.
GMM relaxes that: it models **elliptical** clusters of **different shapes, sizes, and
orientations**, and reports **uncertainty**. Whenever k-means *almost* works but clusters
are stretched or overlapping, GMM is the natural upgrade.

**The covariance choice is the main decision.** Constraining covariance trades
flexibility for stability (and fewer parameters → less overfitting):

- **spherical** — each component σ²I (like soft k-means).
- **diagonal** — axis-aligned ellipses; no feature correlations within a cluster.
- **tied** — all components share one covariance matrix (same shape, different centers).
- **full** — each component its own unconstrained Σ (most flexible, most parameters).

In mclust's notation these are the EEE/VVV/EVI/... family (Equal/Variable Volume, Shape,
Orientation). Picking the covariance model *is* part of model selection — let BIC choose
among covariance types and k jointly.

**Gotchas.** (1) **Full covariance in high dimensions overfits hard** — parameters grow
as O(k·d²); a component can collapse onto a few points and send its likelihood to
infinity (singular covariance). Regularize (add to the diagonal), constrain covariance,
or reduce dimensions. (2) Non-convex likelihood → sensitive to initialization; use
multiple restarts (often k-means or hierarchical init). (3) It still assumes Gaussian
components; genuinely non-Gaussian clusters (e.g., crescents) are better served by
density or spectral methods.

## EM

Expectation-Maximization is the workhorse fitting algorithm for all of these. It
monotonically increases the likelihood but only to a **local** optimum — so restarts and
sensible initialization matter. Two recurring failures to watch for:

- **Label switching.** Component labels are arbitrary and can permute across
  runs/iterations; the *partition* is meaningful, the integer labels are not. Matters
  when averaging across runs or doing Bayesian estimation.
- **Identifiability / singularities.** With too many components or too-flexible
  covariance, a component can shrink to a single point (infinite likelihood spike) or two
  solutions can be statistically indistinguishable. Regularization, covariance
  constraints, and minimum-cluster-size floors prevent the degenerate cases.

## LCA

**Indicators: categorical.** The components are products of categorical (often Bernoulli)
distributions: within a class, each item has its own response probability, and items are
assumed **conditionally independent given class** (local independence — the core LCA
assumption). Classic use: survey/questionnaire data, diagnostic symptom checklists,
voting patterns — "are there latent types of respondents?"

**Output.** Class sizes, item-response probabilities per class (the "profile" of each
class), and posterior class-membership probabilities per respondent. You interpret a
class by which items its members tend to endorse.

**Watch.** Local independence is an assumption, not a fact; violations (items correlated
beyond what class explains) inflate the apparent number of classes. Sparse cells with
many categorical items make estimation fragile.

## LPA

**Indicators: continuous.** LPA *is* a Gaussian mixture model, framed for and named by
the social/behavioral sciences. "Profiles" = mixture components; you describe each
profile by its mean vector across the continuous indicators (high on these, low on
those). Same covariance-structure choices as GMM (often labeled as variance/covariance
"models" 1–6 in tidyLPA, mapping to mclust's constrained families): equal vs. varying
variances, zero vs. freely-estimated covariances across profiles.

**Practical note.** Behavioral researchers typically run LPA *confirmatorily-ish*: fit a
range of profile counts and covariance models, compare fit statistics, and choose with an
eye to **theoretical interpretability and parsimony**, not just the lowest BIC. That
culture (justify the solution substantively, report classification certainty) is worth
importing into any segmentation task, ML or otherwise.

## Choosing classes

Model-based clustering's big advantage is that "how many groups?" becomes a model-
comparison question rather than a guess. The standard tools, roughly in order of how much
to trust them:

- **BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion)** — the workhorse. Penalizes likelihood by
  parameters × log n. Lower is better. The most-used and generally most reliable single
  criterion for mixture/LCA/LPA class enumeration. Plot BIC across k and covariance
  models; look for the minimum (or where it flattens — a clear "knee" is as good as a
  minimum).
- **AIC** — lighter penalty; tends to **over-extract** classes. Report it, don't lead
  with it.
- **ICL (Integrated Completed Likelihood)** — BIC plus an entropy penalty that rewards
  *well-separated* classes. Prefer it when you want clusters that are cleanly
  distinguishable (high classification certainty), not just a good density fit. Often
  picks fewer, cleaner classes than BIC.
- **BLRT (Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test)** — formally tests k vs. k−1 classes by
  bootstrapping the likelihood-ratio null. In the Nylund et al. (2007) simulations, BLRT
  and BIC were the best performers for class enumeration. Gold-standard but
  computationally expensive.
- **Entropy** — not a selection criterion but a **report-it-always** diagnostic: a
  summary (0–1) of how confidently cases are classified. High entropy (→1) means clean
  assignment. A solution with great BIC but terrible entropy is fragile.

**Discipline.** Don't let one number decide. Fit a range, look at BIC/ICL/BLRT *and*
entropy *and* class sizes (a 2%-of-sample class is often noise or an artifact) *and*
whether the classes mean something. The statistics narrow the field; judgment picks the
solution. This mirrors the general validation philosophy in `validation.md`.

## Tooling

- **Python:** `sklearn.mixture.GaussianMixture` (and `BayesianGaussianMixture`, which can
  prune unused components via a Dirichlet-process-style prior — a soft way to avoid
  fixing k). For LCA: `stepmix` (sklearn-style, handles continuous/categorical/mixed and
  is the most actively maintained option), or older options.
- **R (the richest ecosystem for this):** `mclust` (Gaussian model-based clustering with
  automatic BIC selection over covariance families — the reference implementation),
  `tidyLPA` (friendly LPA wrapper over mclust), `tidySEM` (modern LCA/LPA with good
  reporting), `poLCA` (polytomous LCA), `flexmix` (general finite mixtures incl.
  regression mixtures).
- **Mplus / Latent GOLD:** commercial, dominant in psychology/sociology for LCA/LPA/LTA;
  what reviewers in those fields often expect to see, with BLRT built in.
