# Identify (Define): frame the problem and the target behavior

Goal of this stage: end with (a) a problem stated as an *outcome*, (b) a rough theory of change,
and (c) one or more **target behaviors** stated as observable actions. Get this wrong and
everything downstream is wasted motion — you'll diagnose the wrong behavior and design for a
barrier that doesn't matter.

## 1. State the problem as an outcome, then ask "why is this a problem?"

People almost always arrive with the problem already framed as a solution:

- "Parents won't use oral rehydration therapy." (presumes ORT is the fix)
- "Effluent from latrines leaks into the water supply." (presumes sanitation engineering)
- "Users aren't watching our onboarding videos." (presumes videos are the path to value)

Repeatedly asking **"why is that a problem?"** ladders up to the outcome that actually matters
(infants are dying of preventable diarrhea; new users never reach first value). The broader
outcome reopens the full solution space. Stop laddering when you reach the outcome the
organization actually cares about — not before (you'll over-narrow), not after (you'll boil the
ocean).

**Don't over-fixate on "root causes."** Understanding causes matters, but the most effective
intervention often does not address the deepest cause. Malaria is caused by a parasite, but
distributing treated bed nets beats trying to eradicate every mosquito. Solve where there's
leverage, not where the textbook says the "real" cause is.

## 2. Identify beneficiaries and their actual needs

Who is this a problem *for*, and what do they need? Sometimes obvious (sick infants and their
caregivers), often not (the "homeless population" is individuals, families, veterans, people
with mental-health conditions — plus the businesses and residents affected). Mis-scoping the
beneficiary hides the behaviors that matter. Use ethnography/observation, not assumption —
outsiders routinely guess wrong about why people do what they do (e.g., open defecation
persisting for safety and cultural reasons a sanitation engineer never considered).

## 3. Draft a theory of change (logic model)

Lay out the causal chain from what the program does to the outcome it wants:

```
Activities / Outputs   ──>   Intermediate Outcomes   ──>   Ultimate Outcome
(what the org does)          (what other actors do)        (the goal)
```

Example (chlorinate water to prevent infant diarrhea):

```
Chlorine made available   ──>  Caregiver adds chlorine to jug   ──>  Children don't get sick
at the water source            AND only treated water used at home   from contaminated water
```

The point of writing it out: **every arrow is a place the chain can break**, and the breaks
that involve a *person choosing or acting* are your behavioral targets. Ask at each step: does
someone have to do (or stop doing) something for this arrow to hold? Whose behavior?

## 4. Specify the target behavior as an observable action — the heart of this stage

For each behavioral arrow, name the **target behavior**: the specific action a specific person
must take. The discipline that matters most:

> State the intended **action**, not a presumed **driver** of the action.

| ❌ Not a target behavior (smuggled solution) | ✅ Target behavior (observable action) |
|---|---|
| "Caregiver understands the value of chlorine" | "Caregiver squirts chlorine into the water jug" |
| "Employee is aware of the 401(k) match" | "Employee completes 401(k) enrollment" |
| "Developer sees the value of the AI tool" | "Developer runs the tool on a real task in week 1" |
| "Patient is motivated to take the medication" | "Patient takes the medication daily" |
| "Student values financial aid" | "Student submits the FAFSA renewal" |

Why this is non-negotiable: the moment you write "understands," "is aware," "is motivated," or
"values," you have *already decided* the barrier is a knowledge/awareness/motivation deficit and
the solution is education/marketing/persuasion. That's a hypothesis — usually the weakest one —
masquerading as a problem statement. Naming the behavior as an action keeps the diagnosis open,
and it's the only form you can actually observe, count, and test.

A single strategy usually decomposes into **several** target behaviors, each handled separately.
"Improve fitness via gyms" requires prospective members to (1) join, (2) go, and (3) exercise
effectively once there — three different behaviors with three different barriers. Don't blur
them.

## 5. Separate the behavioral problem from the structural one

Before handing anything to Diagnosis, sanity-check that the gap is even behavioral. A surprising
share of "people won't do X" problems are partly or wholly structural — no money, no transport,
no childcare, no supply, no network access, or a product that simply doesn't work. Behavioral
design has nothing to offer a missing bus route, and pretending otherwise quietly blames the
person for a system failure. For each target behavior, ask: *if this person genuinely wanted to
act right now, could they?* Where the answer is "no, the resource/access/product isn't there,"
that's structural — name it, route it to whoever owns it, and carve it out. What remains —
"they could act, but something in the context stops them" — is the behavioral problem this skill
works. The honest behavioral/structural split is often the single most useful output of Identify.

## Checklist before moving to Diagnose

- [ ] Problem is stated as an outcome, not a favored solution
- [ ] You've laddered "why is this a problem?" to the outcome that truly matters
- [ ] Beneficiaries and their real needs are identified (from observation, not assumption)
- [ ] A theory of change connects activities → intermediate outcomes → ultimate outcome
- [ ] Each behavioral arrow has a **target behavior stated as an observable action**
- [ ] No target behavior is phrased as "understands / aware / motivated / values"
- [ ] Multi-behavior strategies are split into separate target behaviors
- [ ] The structural part of the problem (resource/access/price/supply/product) is named and carved out; only the genuinely behavioral remainder proceeds
