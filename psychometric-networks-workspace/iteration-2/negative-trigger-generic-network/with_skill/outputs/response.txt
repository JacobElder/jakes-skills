## Betweenness Centrality

**Betweenness centrality** measures how often a node sits on the *shortest path* between other pairs of nodes in the network. Formally, for a node *v*:

$$BC(v) = \sum_{s \neq v \neq t} \frac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}$$

where σ\_st is the total number of shortest paths from node *s* to node *t*, and σ\_st(*v*) is the number of those paths that pass through *v*.

In plain terms: **a node with high betweenness is a bridge or bottleneck — information, traffic, or influence flowing across the network tends to pass through it.**

---

### Intuitive example

Imagine a small airline route map:

```
NYC ── Chicago ── Denver ── LA
          |
        Dallas
```

Chicago sits on nearly every path between eastern and western cities. If Chicago's airport closes, many routes are severed. Chicago has high betweenness; Dallas, a leaf node, has low betweenness even if it's well-connected locally.

---

### When betweenness is most useful

| Domain | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| **Transportation/logistics** | Identifying infrastructure bottlenecks (roads, airports, pipelines) |
| **Social networks** | Finding brokers or gatekeepers who bridge otherwise disconnected communities |
| **Internet/communication networks** | Routers that, if removed, would fragment the network |
| **Supply chains** | Single-supplier chokepoints |
| **Citation/knowledge networks** | Papers that connect disparate research areas |

The classic applied case is **network attack / robustness analysis**: if you want to disrupt a network efficiently, removing high-betweenness nodes tends to fragment it faster than removing high-degree nodes.

---

### A few caveats worth knowing

- Betweenness assumes traffic literally travels along shortest paths, which is often a simplification. In real systems (e.g., electrical grids, social influence), flow doesn't always take the geodesic route.
- It's computationally expensive on large graphs — O(VE) for unweighted graphs via Brandes' algorithm.
- In **dense, weighted graphs** (as opposed to sparse, binary ones), betweenness can behave counterintuitively because "shortest path" becomes sensitive to how edge weights are defined. For those settings, degree-based or flow-based centrality measures are often more interpretable.

For sparse, clearly-structured networks with meaningful path semantics — transportation, social brokerage, communication — betweenness is one of the most insightful centrality measures available.
