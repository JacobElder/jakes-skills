Node selection is one of the deepest conceptual issues in psychometric networks, and a reviewer raising it on Y-BOCS data is asking exactly the right question. Let me break this down carefully.

---

## The core conceptual issue

Under the network theory of psychopathology, **nodes are supposed to be the psychological constituents whose direct causal interactions constitute the disorder**. The whole argument — that "OCD" is the equilibrium behavior of a self-sustaining symptom system rather than the reflection of an underlying disease entity — only holds if the nodes are genuinely that: the elemental symptoms that activate and maintain each other.

The problem with the Y-BOCS is that **its items are not symptoms at all. They are severity dimensions applied to whatever symptoms a given patient happens to have.** Each item asks something like "how much time do your obsessions occupy?" — a meta-level rating that collapses all obsession content (contamination fears, harm intrusions, symmetry urges, religious obsessions) into a single scalar. The node labeled "time occupied by obsessions" is not a discrete psychological entity that can activate or be activated by other symptoms; it's a summary statistic over heterogeneous content.

So the reviewer is really asking: *What are you claiming when you draw an edge between "interference from obsessions" and "distress from compulsions"? Is that a causal relationship between two psychological constituents? Or is it a correlation among severity ratings that would follow mechanically from the fact that severe OCD patients score high on everything?*

That is a hard question, and the network theory gives you no easy out on it.

---

## Specific structural problems with the Y-BOCS as a node set

**1. Within-dimension redundancy.** The Y-BOCS has near-parallel structure: five obsession items (time, interference, distress, resistance, control) and five compulsion items with the same structure. The "time occupied by obsessions" and "time occupied by compulsions" items share a severity dimension (frequency/time) but differ only in symptom type. These pairs may produce strong edges not because the underlying phenomena causally interact but because they are functionally near-duplicate items with nearly identical conditioning sets. Run `networktools::goldbricker(data)` before estimation — you may find several flagged pairs.

**2. Collapsing symptom heterogeneity.** If the network approach is meant to reveal *which* OCD symptoms activate *which others*, the Y-BOCS cannot answer that question. It was designed as a severity measure for clinical trials, not as a symptom-content instrument. The OCI-R, the Dimensional OCS, the Y-BOCS Symptom Checklist, or item-level data from broader OCD batteries would give you nodes with actual content heterogeneity.

**3. The resistance items are theoretically peculiar nodes.** "Resistance to obsessions" and "resistance to compulsions" are arguably coping/control variables, not symptoms in the same sense as the other items. Treating them as equivalent nodes implicitly claims they participate in the causal network in the same way as, say, "distress from obsessions." That deserves explicit justification.

---

## How to justify Y-BOCS nodes — if you can

There is a defensible path, but it requires reframing your research question honestly:

**Reframe as a network of severity dimensions, not symptoms.** If your question is "how do the time, distress, interference, resistance, and control dimensions of obsessions and compulsions co-occur and reinforce each other in OCD patients," the Y-BOCS is a reasonable instrument for those nodes and your network is descriptively interesting. You are not claiming to model the micro-level symptom interactions Borsboom (2017) envisions; you are modeling the covariance structure of clinician-rated severity facets. This is legitimate but requires you to be explicit about it and to not make claims about "which symptoms drive OCD" that the nodes cannot support.

**Pragmatic comparability.** If your study needs to be integrated with clinical literature where Y-BOCS is the standard, that is a genuine scientific justification — comparability and accumulated base rates matter. Acknowledge the limitation, don't pretend it doesn't exist.

**Cite the node-selection literature directly.** The canonical references the reviewer is implicitly invoking are Fried (2017, *J Affect Disorders*, "The 52 symptoms of major depression") and Fried, Epskamp, Nesse, Tuerlinckx & Borsboom (2016) "What are 'good' depression symptoms?" Both argue that node selection is a theoretical act with large consequences for estimated network structure. Engaging these papers directly — showing you know the critique and have a considered response — will satisfy most reviewers even if your answer is "we used Y-BOCS for comparability and acknowledge these limitations."

---

## What the response to the reviewer should look like

A strong response would do four things:

1. **Acknowledge the theoretical issue explicitly**: Y-BOCS items are severity dimensions, not discrete symptom entities, and the network should be interpreted accordingly.
2. **Justify the choice on scientific grounds**: comparability with the OCD severity literature, the specific research question you're addressing (severity-dimension covariance vs. symptom-content interactions), or constraint by available data.
3. **Report goldbricker results** to demonstrate you checked for node redundancy — this shows methodological rigor and may preempt a follow-up question.
4. **Delimit your causal claims** to match what the nodes actually support. If your nodes are severity ratings, don't claim the network shows which "OCD symptoms" drive disorder maintenance; claim it shows how severity facets covary in this sample.

---

The deeper lesson here — which Fried has pushed hard — is that the network approach is not a method you apply to *any* item set and read off disorder structure. The nodes you choose *are* the theory. Choosing Y-BOCS items is choosing to model OCD as a system of severity facets, and that choice needs to be owned and defended rather than treated as a neutral data-collection decision.
