## Strength vs. Expected Influence for your affect network

The short answer: **report Expected Influence as your primary index**, and consider reporting both — because the *divergence* between them is substantively informative in a mixed-valence network like yours.

---

### The core difference

| Index | Formula | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength** | Σ \|w_ij\| | Total connectivity — treats a strong positive edge and a strong negative edge identically |
| **Expected Influence** | Σ w_ij (signed) | Net activating influence — negative edges *subtract*, so the sign of the relationship matters |

Expected Influence was introduced by Robinaugh, Millner & McNally (2016, *J Abnormal Psychology*) precisely because Strength conflates nodes that strongly *excite* their neighbors with nodes that strongly *inhibit* them.

---

### Why this distinction bites in your specific network

Your 8 items form two natural valence clusters:

```
Positive: enthusiastic — active — inspired — alert
Negative: distressed — upset — hostile — afraid
```

You will almost certainly have **negative cross-cluster edges** — positive affect items partially suppress negative affect items and vice versa, especially in GGM estimation on ordinal/Likert items. That means:

- A node like **distressed** will have positive edges *within* the negative cluster and negative edges toward the positive cluster.
- **Strength** sums all of those absolute values → distressed looks maximally central.
- **Expected Influence** subtracts the negative cross-cluster weights → distressed's net influence is lower, which is arguably the more interpretable quantity.

Strength will systematically inflate the apparent centrality of nodes that sit at the *boundary* between your two clusters (the bridge items), because it treats their inhibitory connections as equally central-making as their excitatory ones.

---

### Practical diagnostic to run first

Before deciding, look at your edge weight matrix:

```r
# Get signed adjacency
net$graph  # or getWmat(net) in older qgraph

# Quick check: are there any negative edges?
sum(net$graph < 0)

# Compare the two rankings side by side
library(qgraph)
s  <- centrality(net)$ShortestPathLengths  # ignore this
ei <- expectedInfluence(net)               # from networktools or qgraph

cbind(Strength = sort(centrality(net)$OutDegree, decreasing=TRUE),
      EI       = sort(ei, decreasing=TRUE))
```

If the rankings are nearly identical, your between-cluster edges are sparse or near-zero (plausible in cross-sectional data where PA and NA are relatively orthogonal after conditioning), and either index is fine. If the rankings diverge — especially if mid-valence or more ambiguous items (e.g., "alert" can read either positive or anxious) shift substantially — that divergence is itself a finding worth reporting.

---

### Reporting recommendation

**Report Expected Influence as primary**, Strength as secondary/supplementary, with a sentence explaining why:

> "Because the network included negative edges between positive and negative affect items, we report Expected Influence as the primary centrality index (Robinaugh et al., 2016), which accounts for the direction of each edge weight. Strength is reported for comparison."

If you're short on space and the rankings are nearly identical, a footnote noting the convergence is sufficient.

---

### One thing to flag

"Alert" sits at a conceptual boundary — in the PANAS literature it loads positive affect, but phenomenologically it can reflect anxious arousal. If it has a meaningfully different Strength vs. EI profile compared to the other positive items, that's worth noting explicitly: it may be functioning as a bridge node between your two clusters, which has interpretive consequences for what it means to be "alert" in your sample's affect dynamics.
