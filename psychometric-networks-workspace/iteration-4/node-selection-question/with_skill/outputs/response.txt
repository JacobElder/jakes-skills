Good question, and it's a sharp reviewer comment — node selection is genuinely one of the most theoretically loaded decisions in a psychometric network analysis, and the Y-BOCS raises some specific issues worth thinking through carefully.

## The fundamental conceptual point

Under the network approach, nodes are not neutral observables — they are **theoretical commitments about what the meaningful psychological entities are**. The network structure you estimate is entirely determined by which nodes you put in. Add a node, remove a node, split a node, and you get a different graph. This is what Fried and colleagues have been pressing since at least 2015–2016 (the "52 symptoms of major depression" paper; "What are 'good' depression symptoms?"). The reviewer is essentially asking: *why is your node set the right partition of the OCD symptom space?*

The answer needs to address two things simultaneously: (a) **theoretical justification** — why these constructs belong in the graph, and (b) **empirical vigilance** — whether the items as operationalized behave in ways that won't distort the network.

---

## Y-BOCS-specific issues to address

### 1. Severity dimensions, not symptom content

The Y-BOCS's 10 items measure *severity dimensions* — time, interference, distress, resistance, and control — separately for obsessions and compulsions. These are not fine-grained symptom contents (contamination fears, checking, hoarding, etc.); they're clinician ratings of functional impact. In network terms, you need to be explicit about whether you're estimating a **severity-dimension network** (how does resistance to obsessions relate to interference from compulsions?) or a **symptom-content network** (how does contamination relate to harm avoidance?). These are different scientific questions. The Y-BOCS items answer the former, and your introduction should frame your question accordingly.

### 2. Node redundancy risk

This is the empirically urgent issue. Several Y-BOCS items — particularly within the obsessions subscale (time occupied, interference, distress, resistance, control) — are conceptually adjacent enough that they may have nearly identical conditioning sets. When that happens, the graphical lasso produces a spuriously strong edge between them, and those nodes' centrality estimates get inflated by the redundancy rather than by genuine psychological importance.

Run `networktools::goldbricker()` before estimation:

```r
library(networktools)
goldbricker(data, p = 0.05, method = "hittner2003", threshold = 0.25)
```

This flags pairs of nodes whose partial-correlation profiles with all other nodes are so similar that they are likely measuring the same construct twice. If the distress and interference items get flagged, you have a few options: prune one, composite them, or retain both and explicitly acknowledge the redundancy limitation in your discussion. Whatever you do, report the goldbricker output — it demonstrates you took the reviewer's concern seriously empirically, not just conceptually.

### 3. Small node set

Ten nodes is at the lower end for psychometric network estimation. A GGM estimates O(p²) edge parameters, and with p = 10 you have 45 edges to estimate. The graphical lasso will handle this, but with few nodes the network is also structurally constrained — you can't find community structure, bridge symptoms, or emergent dynamics that require more nodes to manifest. Be honest in your limitations section that the Y-BOCS node set constrains the theoretical scope of what you can conclude.

---

## What a strong node justification looks like

A good response to this reviewer does three things:

**Theoretical grounding:** State explicitly that these nodes represent severity dimensions of OCD phenomenology as operationalized by the field's gold-standard clinical measure. Cite the theoretical reason you're interested in *this* question (e.g., understanding how functional interference and subjective distress propagate through the obsession-compulsion cycle). Acknowledge what the node set cannot capture — symptom heterogeneity, subtype-specific content — and bound your claims accordingly.

**Precedent and field convention:** If other published OCD networks have used the Y-BOCS (there are some), cite them and note you're enabling comparability. Compat with prior literature is a legitimate justification as long as you acknowledge the inherited limitations.

**Empirical redundancy check:** Report goldbricker results. If no pairs were flagged above threshold, that's evidence the 10 nodes carry sufficiently distinct information. If pairs were flagged, report what you did and why.

## Is there a stronger alternative worth considering?

Depending on what your research question is, the reviewer's concern might point to a genuine design improvement rather than just a framing fix:

- If you want a **symptom-content network**, the Y-BOCS symptom checklist (the 58-item checklist that precedes the severity scale) or the OCI-R (18 items with more dimensional coverage) gives you content-level nodes the severity scale can't.
- If you want to model **both severity and content**, some published OCD networks layer the severity scale onto symptom dimensions — you get a richer graph but more complex interpretation.
- If your clinical question is specifically about **severity dynamics** (how does distress feedforward into avoidance?), the Y-BOCS is actually well-matched, and framing it that way preempts the "wrong nodes" critique.

The clearest version of your response to the reviewer is: *"The Y-BOCS items were selected to model OCD severity dimensions rather than symptom content; this constrains and focuses the scientific question to X; we verified empirically that item pairs were not flagged as redundant (goldbricker, threshold = 0.25); and we acknowledge in the limitations that symptom-content heterogeneity is not captured by this node set."*

That's a defensible, methodologically informed position — and it shows you understand what the question is actually about.
