## Betweenness Centrality

**What it measures:** Betweenness centrality quantifies how often a node sits on the *shortest path* between other pairs of nodes. Formally, for node *v*:

$$C_B(v) = \sum_{s \neq v \neq t} \frac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}$$

where σ_st is the total number of shortest paths from node *s* to node *t*, and σ_st(v) is the number of those paths that pass through *v*.

A high-betweenness node is a **broker or bridge** — remove it, and many shortest routes between other nodes get longer or break entirely.

---

### Intuitive example

Imagine an airline hub-and-spoke network:

```
Seattle ── Chicago ── New York
              │
           Dallas ── Miami
```

Chicago has high betweenness: almost every cross-country route runs through it. Seattle and Miami have low betweenness — they're endpoints, not connectors.

---

### When betweenness is useful

| Domain | Application |
|---|---|
| **Infrastructure / logistics** | Identifying single points of failure — which routers, power stations, or road intersections, if disrupted, most fragment the network |
| **Social networks** | Finding information brokers — people who connect otherwise-separate social cliques (the classic "structural hole" concept from Burt, 1992) |
| **Epidemiology** | Targeting vaccination or quarantine at individuals who bridge communities, maximally slowing pathogen spread |
| **Supply chains** | Pinpointing bottleneck suppliers that sit between many buyers and raw-material producers |
| **Citation / knowledge networks** | Identifying "gateway" papers that connect disparate research communities |

---

### Key properties to keep in mind

- **It's a flow/distance concept.** It assumes things travel along shortest paths, which is realistic for some systems (routing, logistics) but not others (social influence, which tends to follow random walks or decay across hops).
- **Edge weights matter** — in weighted graphs, "shortest" usually means least-cost, so you need a sensible weight-to-distance conversion (weights as *similarities* need inverting before path calculations).
- **Scales with network size** — raw betweenness values grow with *n*, so normalize (divide by the maximum possible value) when comparing nodes across different-sized networks.
- **Computationally expensive** on large graphs — Brandes (2001) gives the standard O(VE) algorithm, implemented in `networkx.betweenness_centrality()` and `igraph::betweenness()`.

One worth noting: betweenness was designed for **sparse, unweighted or flow-based networks**. In dense weighted graphs (like partial-correlation matrices), shortest-path logic fits less naturally and the measure can become harder to interpret — in those settings you'd want to think carefully about whether the shortest-path metaphor actually matches your theoretical model.
