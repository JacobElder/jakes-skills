## Interpreting your bootnet results

Here's a node-by-node breakdown of what your numbers permit and what they don't.

---

### CS-coefficients — the decision tree

| Index | CS | Threshold | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expected Influence | 0.44 | ≥ 0.25 ✓, approaching 0.50 | **Report with moderate confidence** |
| Strength | 0.36 | ≥ 0.25 ✓, below 0.50 | **Report with caveats** |
| Closeness | 0.13 | < 0.25 ✗ | **Do not interpret** |
| Betweenness | 0.05 | < 0.25 ✗ | **Do not interpret** |

The rule of thumb from Epskamp, Borsboom & Fried (2018): CS ≥ 0.50 is *good*, ≥ 0.25 is the *floor for any interpretation at all*, < 0.25 means the ordering collapses when you drop a modest fraction of your sample.

Closeness and Betweenness should be dropped from your writeup for two compounding reasons: (1) their CS-coefficients are well below interpretable range, and (2) these indices don't translate well to dense weighted partial-correlation graphs even in principle — the Bringmann et al. (2019, *J Abnormal Psych*) critique applies directly here. You lose nothing substantive by removing them.

---

### What to report for Strength and Expected Influence

**Expected Influence (CS = 0.44)** is your strongest result. Since your network likely has mixed-sign edges (common in 18-node symptom/item networks), Expected Influence is also the *right* choice over Strength because it preserves edge sign rather than summing absolutes. Report the centrality ordering as a descriptive summary of the estimated graph — but use appropriately hedged language:

> "Expected Influence values were moderately stable (CS = 0.44), suggesting the ordering of nodes by Expected Influence is reasonably reproducible, though precise rank differences between similarly-valued nodes should be interpreted cautiously."

**Strength (CS = 0.36)** is interpretable but sits in the lower half of the acceptable range. You can include it as a supplementary index, or as a robustness check showing it largely agrees with Expected Influence ordering. Do not present absolute Strength values as precise estimates.

For both, **use the bootstrapped difference tests** (the `plot(boot, "strength")` / `differenceTest()` output) to identify which pairwise centrality comparisons are actually distinguishable. In practice, for a sample-size-constrained GGM, you'll often find that only the top 2–3 nodes are reliably separable from the rest — that's a normal and honest finding, not a failure.

---

### Wide, overlapping edge-weight CIs — what this means

Wide overlapping CIs are the norm in most published psychometric networks, not a red flag in isolation. What matters is what you do with them:

1. **Don't interpret specific edge values as precise estimates.** You can say edges exist (present vs. absent), and you can roughly rank *groups* of edges (strong vs. weak), but exact magnitudes shouldn't be the focus.
2. **Use the bootstrapped difference test for edges** before claiming any two specific edges differ in strength. Most pairs probably won't survive this test — that's fine and should be reported.
3. **The graph structure** (which edges are present/absent after EBICglasso regularization) is generally more stable than edge magnitudes. Focus your substantive interpretation there.

In your manuscript, the minimum credible reporting given these results:

```
Network accuracy was assessed via nonparametric bootstrapping (n = 1,000 bootstraps; 
Epskamp et al., 2018). Edge-weight confidence intervals were wide and overlapping for 
most pairs, indicating that precise edge-magnitude differences should not be 
over-interpreted; the bootstrapped difference test was used to identify

(CS = 0.36) met the minimum stability threshold for interpretation (CS ≥ 0.25); 
Closeness (CS = 0.13) and Betweenness (CS = 0.05) fell below this threshold and 
are not reported. Centrality indices are interpreted as descriptive summaries of 
the estimated network structure and do not imply causal importance.
```

### The broader framing

Your results are honest and defensible. The field has largely moved past expecting high-precision centrality estimates from modest samples — Robinaugh et al.'s (2020) decade-in review is candid that centrality instability has been a systemic issue. What your results support is: a network structure (which connections exist), rough groupings of more vs. less central nodes via Expected Influence, and a responsible statement that node-level ordering should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. That's a legitimate scientific contribution.

If sample size expansion is possible, the modern approach is to run a power analysis via `bootnet`'s simulation functions (Constantin, Schuurman & Vermunt, 2022) to estimate what *n* would yield CS ≥ 0.50 for your node count — useful for a limitations section or preregistered follow-up.
