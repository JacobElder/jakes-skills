This is one of the most important and frequently misunderstood questions in the field — and the short answer is: **no, the network approach has not disproved or replaced latent variable models**. The popular framing in some papers overstates what the evidence actually shows. Here's a precise account of what *is* and *isn't* settled.

---

## What the network approach actually claims

The core theoretical claim, articulated most clearly in Borsboom (2017, *World Psychiatry*), is a **generative story**: instead of symptoms being caused by an underlying disease entity (the common-cause latent variable model), symptoms *directly interact* with each other and a disorder is the stable equilibrium behavior of that symptom network. Depression isn't a thing that causes sadness, fatigue, and concentration problems — rather, sadness causes fatigue, fatigue causes concentration problems, and so on, in a self-reinforcing web.

This is a substantively different causal claim. But notice what kind of claim it is: **philosophical and causal**, not purely statistical.

---

## The mathematical problem with claiming "disproof"

The network and latent-variable frameworks are not as distinguishable as the rhetoric sometimes implies:

- **The Ising model and a unidimensional latent variable model with appropriate item parameters are mathematically equivalent** in terms of the data they generate — they produce the same joint probability distribution over binary items. You literally cannot tell them apart from observed data alone (Marsman et al., 2018; this builds on van der Maas et al., 2006).
- A **Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM)** and a **factor model** are not generally equivalent, but they can fit the same cross-sectional data well and there is typically no decisive statistical test that picks one over the other.
- A **qgraph plot with a spring layout looks nothing like a factor model diagram**, which creates a powerful visual impression of difference — but a visualization choice is not evidence.

The upshot: **you usually cannot distinguish the two models from cross-sectional observational data alone.** The "disproof" would require intervention studies, experimental manipulation of individual symptoms, or strong longitudinal evidence — not a graphical lasso applied to a survey.

---

## What the replication record shows

The early enthusiasm about network structures replicating across samples has been tempered. Forbes, Wright, Markon & Krueger (2017, *J Abnormal Psychology*) showed that cross-sectional symptom networks often do not replicate well across independent datasets, which prompted an important methodological exchange with the Amsterdam group. The field's response was correct but sobering: **you need large samples, stability metrics (CS-coefficients), and bootstrapped edge CIs before trusting a network structure** — and many early published networks didn't have these.

Robinaugh, Hoekstra, Toner & Borsboom (2020, *Psychological Medicine*), written partly *by* the Amsterdam group, is an honest decade-in assessment. Their own conclusion: the network approach has been theoretically fruitful and has produced real methodological advances, but **the empirical case that symptom networks cause psychopathology (rather than merely reflecting it) remains unestablished**.

---

## What the network approach *has* genuinely contributed

It would be wrong to dismiss the framework — it has produced real advances:

1. **It revealed that DSM symptom items are not interchangeable indicators.** Treating all depression items as equivalent reflections of one latent "depression" obscures meaningful heterogeneity. Fried & Nesse (2015), Fried (2017) on the 52 symptoms of MDD — these are genuine empirical contributions.
2. **Node selection is a scientific choice**, not a neutral data step. Asking *which* symptoms to include and *why* is now a recognized methodological obligation, not an afterthought.
3. **Temporal/ESM networks** (graphical VAR, `mlVAR`) provide a genuinely new window into within-person symptom dynamics that latent variable models weren't designed for.
4. **It changed how clinicians think about comorbidity** — overlapping symptoms between disorders (e.g., sleep problems appearing in both depression and anxiety) are now understood as potential bridges, not just measurement noise.

---

## The bottom line

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "The network approach has disproved the latent variable model" | **False** — the models are often statistically indistinguishable from cross-sectional data |
| "The network approach has replaced factor analysis" | **False** — both are actively used; choice depends on theory and data structure |
| "The network approach offers a different and sometimes more useful causal story" | **True, but unproven** — it's a hypothesis requiring causal evidence, not an established fact |
| "Network methods have contributed genuine methodological and conceptual advances" | **True** — especially for heterogeneity of symptoms, temporal dynamics, and comorbidity bridges |

The strongest version of what you can defensibly say is: **the network approach has provided a theoretically coherent alternative to the latent disease entity model, has produced useful descriptive tools, and has raised important questions about symptom heterogeneity — but it has not empirically displaced latent variable models, and the causal claims at its heart remain to be tested.**

If the paper you're reading claims more than that, it's outrunning the evidence.
