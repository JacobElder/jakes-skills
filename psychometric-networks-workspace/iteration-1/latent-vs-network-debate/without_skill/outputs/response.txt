This is a genuinely important debate in psychopathology, and the "network analysis has disproved latent variable models" claim is significantly overstated — though the network approach has made real, valuable contributions. Let me break this down carefully.

---

## What the two models claim

**Latent variable (common cause) model:** Symptoms co-occur because an underlying disorder *causes* them. Depression causes low mood, insomnia, fatigue, anhedonia, etc. The covariance among symptoms is fully explained by this common cause. This is what factor analysis operationalizes.

**Network model (Borsboom, Cramer, et al.):** Symptoms are *causally connected to each other directly*. Insomnia causes fatigue, fatigue causes concentration problems, concentration problems cause hopelessness, etc. The "disorder" is the network of activated, mutually reinforcing symptoms — not a latent entity causing them.

---

## Why "disproved" is too strong

### 1. Mathematical equivalence is a serious problem for the strong claim

Several researchers have shown that Gaussian graphical models (the most common network approach) and latent variable models can be **mathematically equivalent or empirically indistinguishable** from the same cross-sectional data:

- **Marsman et al. (2018)** showed the Ising model (used in binary symptom networks) is equivalent to a multidimensional IRT model — same data, two "different" models, identical fit.
- **Epskamp et al.** demonstrated that partial correlation networks and factor models are related through the inverse of the covariance matrix — you can move between them algebraically.
- **van der Maas et al. (2006)** showed a mutualism network model fits IQ data as well as the *g*-factor model.

If both models fit the same data equally well, the data haven't *disconfirmed* either model — you have an **underdetermination problem**, not a refutation.

### 2. Cross-sectional networks don't establish causal dynamics

Borsboom's theoretical claim is fundamentally about **causal dynamics** — symptoms triggering symptoms over time. But the vast majority of published network studies use **cross-sectional data**. A partial correlation network estimated at one time point cannot tell you whether symptoms cause each other; it just tells you they're correlated after controlling for others. The empirical work and the theoretical claim are operating at different levels.

### 3. Local independence violations aren't fatal to factor models

Borsboom's early argument (notably in his 2008 *Psychological Inquiry* paper) was that the latent variable model requires local independence — once you condition on the latent variable, symptoms shouldn't correlate. Violations of this, he argued, suggest direct symptom-symptom relationships. But:

- Most applied factor models already accommodate residual correlations (bifactor models, correlated residuals in CFA)
- Violation of strict local independence doesn't *disprove* that a latent variable also exists; it just means the simple common-cause model is incomplete
- This is a modeling refinement, not a paradigm demolition

---

## Where the network approach genuinely adds value

Despite the overclaiming, the network perspective has contributed real things:

- **Symptom heterogeneity:** Fried & Nesse (2015) and others have shown that symptoms within a diagnosis like "depression" are not interchangeable — they have different correlates, predictors, and treatment responses. This is a genuine finding that the aggregate latent variable approach obscures.
- **Comorbidity:** The "bridge symptom" idea (Cramer et al., 2010) — that comorbidity arises from symptoms that belong to multiple disorders connecting their networks — is a theoretically interesting account that the latent variable model handles awkwardly.
- **Idiographic/individual-level analysis:** Network methods have driven interest in *N*=1 time series (idiographic networks), which is genuinely important since population-level factor structures may not apply to individuals (Molenaar's ergodicity argument).
- **Treatment targeting:** Identifying central or bridge symptoms as intervention targets is clinically useful regardless of whether the underlying metaphysics are "really" network or latent variable.

---

## The current state of play

The field has largely moved away from the framing that these are mutually exclusive:

- Many researchers now argue both models may capture **different real features** — there may be latent biological vulnerabilities *and* dynamic symptom-symptom feedback loops
- Hybrid models (e.g., latent variables with network dynamics) are being developed
- There are serious **replication problems** with network structures — the same disorder in different samples often produces quite different networks, raising questions about stability
- Borsboom himself (e.g., Haslbeck, Ryan, Robinaugh, Waldorp & Borsboom, 2021) has increasingly emphasized *formal theoretical models* of dynamics rather than cross-sectional network estimation as the way forward

---

## Bottom line

The network approach hasn't disproved latent variable models — it has:

1. Challenged the naive interpretation that latent variables are disease entities with simple common-cause structure
2. Contributed useful methods for examining symptom-level heterogeneity
3. Opened important theoretical questions about causal dynamics in psychopathology
4. **Not** provided data that definitively discriminates between network and factor models, because those models are often mathematically equivalent given typical data

The authors you're reading are likely overstating a genuinely interesting theoretical challenge as an empirical refutation. The debate is real and productive — but it's still very much a debate, not a settled verdict.
