There are five distinct methodological problems here, ranging from the wrong centrality index to an invalid causal inference. Let me go through each precisely.

---

## 1. Betweenness centrality is the wrong index for this data — full stop

This is the most fundamental error. **Betweenness centrality should not be reported as the primary index in a psychometric network**, and the field has an explicit published consensus on this: **Bringmann et al. (2019), "What do centrality measures measure in psychological networks?" (*Journal of Abnormal Psychology*)**.

The problem is conceptual, not just empirical: betweenness was designed for sparse, unweighted graphs where "flow" passes through intermediary nodes (think: roads, social ties). A GGM of anxiety items is a *dense, weighted, partial-correlation* graph. In that structure, betweenness measures something difficult to interpret meaningfully — a node's position on shortest paths between other nodes — and in dense graphs those paths are often trivially short through many routes simultaneously. Betweenness is also the *least stable* of the standard centrality indices under bootstrapping, which makes it doubly problematic here.

**What they should have used:** Strength (sum of absolute edge weights) is the defensible default — it maps directly onto the model's parameters. If edges can be negative (which they can in a GGM with mixed-valence items), **Expected Influence** (Robinaugh, Millner & McNally, 2016, *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*) is preferable because it preserves sign information. Betweenness answers a different question than "which symptom is most connected," which appears to be what the authors intended.

---

## 2. No stability analyses, which means the centrality claim is uninterpretable

Epskamp, Borsboom & Fried (2018, *Behavior Research Methods*) — the tutorial paper that established field standards — made accuracy and stability checks a **non-negotiable second step**. The `bootnet` package exists primarily to make this routine. Three checks are required:

- **Bootstrapped edge-weight CIs** (nonparametric bootstrap, ≥1000 iterations) to assess edge precision.
- **Case-dropping subset bootstrap** to compute the **CS-coefficient** (centrality stability coefficient): the largest fraction of cases droppable while keeping centrality order correlated ≥0.7 with the original (at 95% probability).
- **Bootstrapped difference tests** to determine which pairwise centrality comparisons are actually distinguishable.

The rule of thumb the field uses: **CS ≥ 0.5 is acceptable; CS < 0.25 means do not interpret centrality at all.** Without running this, there is literally no way to know whether "restlessness has the highest betweenness" is a stable structural feature of the data or sampling noise. Given the sample size (see below), the latter is plausible.

---

## 3. N=180 is undersized for stable centrality in a 15-node network

N=180 for 15 nodes is not obviously catastrophic — you have 12 cases per node, which passes some informal rules of thumb — but the field has moved away from rules of thumb toward simulation-based power analysis (Constantin, Schuurman & Vermunt, 2022) and toward reporting CS-coefficients as the empirical answer. The concern is real: centrality *ordering* (not just absolute values) in GGMs is sensitive to sample size, and betweenness in particular degrades quickly. 

For context: Epskamp et al. (2018) note that "several hundred cases" are typically needed for stable centrality; in simulations, betweenness tends to require larger N than strength does for comparable stability. Without CS-coefficients we can't know, but the authors should have run the simulation and reported it — and the absence of any stability check is itself a reportable gap regardless of what the numbers would show.

---

## 4. The causal inference from centrality to treatment target is not supported by the design

This is arguably the most serious error in the manuscript's *conclusions*. The authors commit what the field has flagged repeatedly: **centrality is not causal importance, and a cross-sectional GGM does not support intervention recommendations.**

A high-strength (or high-betweenness) node tells you that, in *this sample*, this symptom's partial correlations with others are large. It does not tell you:
- that the symptom *drives* others (that requires a temporal design with lagged effects — a graphical VAR, not a cross-sectional GGM),
- that intervening on this symptom will change others (that requires a causal model, not an association model),
- that this node is a good intervention target in any individual (cross-sectional group-level graphs don't generalize to individual dynamics without further assumptions).

Robinaugh, Hoekstra, Toner & Borsboom (2020, *Psychological Medicine*) — the decade-in review of the network approach — is explicit on this: the translation from cross-sectional centrality to treatment target was an overpromise the field has walked back. The authors need to either (a) replace this conclusion with the appropriate hedged interpretation or (b) substantially redesign the study using ESM/EMA data and a temporal network if intervention targeting is the actual scientific goal.

---

## 5. Missing reporting elements relative to current standards

**Burger et al. (2023), "Reporting standards for psychological network analyses in cross-sectional data" (*Psychological Methods*)** is the authoritative checklist. Against it, this manuscript is missing:

| Required element | Status |
|---|---|
| Bootstrapped edge-weight CIs | ❌ absent |
| Bootstrapped difference tests for centrality | ❌ absent |
| CS-coefficients | ❌ absent |
| Justification for node selection (why these 15 items?) | not mentioned — likely missing |
| Tuning parameter (EBIC gamma) reported | unknown |
| `cor_auto` / polychoric correction noted | unknown |

The node-selection point deserves a flag: **Fried (2017)** and **Fried et al. (2016)** have shown that which items you include as nodes materially changes network structure and centrality estimates. If the authors pulled 15 items from a broader instrument or combined subscales without justification, that's an additional gap.

---

## Summary for the review

The manuscript's core claim — that restlessness is the most important anxiety symptom because it has the highest betweenness centrality, and should therefore be a treatment target — is unsupported on **three independent grounds**: (1) betweenness is the wrong index for weighted partial-correlation graphs and is the least stable centrality measure, (2) no stability analyses were conducted so the ordering cannot be trusted regardless of which index was used, and (3) cross-sectional centrality does not license causal intervention claims. These are not minor presentation issues; they require substantive revision before the network analysis can support the conclusions drawn.
