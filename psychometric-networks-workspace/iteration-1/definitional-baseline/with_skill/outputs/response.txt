## Psychometric networks vs. factor models

Both are models of the same raw material — a correlation matrix among psychological items — but they differ in their **generative story**, which changes everything downstream.

---

### The factor model: common cause

A factor model says the correlations among items exist *because* a latent variable causes all of them. Depression items co-occur — sad mood, sleep disturbance, concentration problems — because an underlying disease entity (or trait) drives them all in parallel. Remove the latent variable, and the items become independent. The latent variable is real; the item intercorrelations are just its shadow.

```
        F (latent)
       /  |  \
      i1  i2  i3   ← items are conditionally independent given F
```

The model's parameters are **factor loadings** — how strongly each item reflects the latent cause.

---

### The psychometric network: direct interactions

A network model says the correlations exist because items *directly activate each other*. Sad mood causes fatigue; fatigue causes concentration problems; concentration problems cause hopelessness. There's no underlying entity — the disorder *is* the pattern of interactions among symptoms at equilibrium. The model's parameters are **partial correlations** (in the Gaussian case) — the association between two nodes after conditioning out every other node.

```
      i1 — i2
      |  ×  |      ← items are directly connected; no hidden layer
      i3 — i4
```

---

### The estimation difference

| | Factor model | Psychometric network (GGM) |
|---|---|---|
| **Estimated parameters** | Loadings on latent variable(s) | Pairwise partial correlations |
| **Zeros mean** | No relationship to the factor | No direct relationship between nodes |
| **Key regularization** | Fit indices (CFI, RMSEA) | Graphical lasso + EBIC tuning |
| **What you interpret** | Factor scores, loadings | Edge weights, centrality (strength) |

---

### The uncomfortable mathematical fact

Here's the important caveat: **you often cannot distinguish them statistically from cross-sectional data alone.** A unidimensional factor model and an Ising network model can be mathematically equivalent given the right parameters (Marsman et al., 2018). A GGM and a factor model can fit the same correlation matrix with similar adequacy.

The network claim is therefore **more philosophical than statistical** — it's an assertion about the causal generative process (direct interactions rather than a common cause), not something you can read off a model fit index. Distinguishing them requires intervention data, longitudinal designs, or strong theory.

---

### Why it matters practically

The distinction has real consequences for clinical reasoning:

- **Factor logic**: if items are just reflections of a latent disease, you treat the disease and all items remit together.
- **Network logic**: if items are directly connected, the network has its own dynamics — some nodes are more "central" (strongly connected to everything else), and targeting those could be a better intervention point than treating a latent entity that may not exist.

This is why Borsboom (2017, *World Psychiatry*) introduced network models as a substantive alternative — not just a different visualization of the same structure, but a different theory of what mental disorders are.

The honest summary: same data, very different stories, and the data alone usually can't adjudicate between them.
