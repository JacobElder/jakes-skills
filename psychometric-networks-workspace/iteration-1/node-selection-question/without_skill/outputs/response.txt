This is a genuinely important methodological question, and the reviewer is right to push on it. Here's how to think through it:

## The Core Conceptual Issue: What Should a Node *Be*?

Network psychopathology assumes nodes represent **causally distinct, mutually activating symptom entities** — things that can turn each other on and off. The theoretical payoff of the network approach (over latent variable models) depends entirely on this assumption. So node selection is not cosmetic; it determines whether your network represents a causal system or just a correlation structure with circles drawn around it.

## The Specific Problem with Y-BOCS Items

The Y-BOCS creates a particular tension here that's worth naming explicitly:

**The Y-BOCS measures severity dimensions, not symptom content.** Its 10 items assess time, interference, distress, resistance, and control — *for whatever obsessions/compulsions the patient already has*. This means:

1. **The items may not be causally independent.** "Time occupied by obsessions" and "distress from obsessions" are not distinct symptom entities that activate each other — they're correlated facets of severity, likely driven by the same underlying intensity. High correlations between them could reflect shared method variance or shared severity rather than genuine symptom dynamics.

2. **They're plausibly reflective, not formative.** If these items are reflective indicators of a latent severity dimension, then the covariances your network recovers are driven by that common cause — which is precisely what the network approach is supposed to bypass. You'd be estimating a network of a latent variable's indicators, which is a different (and more contested) claim than a network of symptoms.

3. **You can't observe symptom heterogeneity.** Two patients with identical Y-BOCS profiles could have completely different OCD presentations (contamination vs. harm obsessions, checking vs. ordering compulsions). The Y-BOCS collapses that variance before you even start.

## How to Frame Your Justification

You have a few defensible positions, each with different tradeoffs:

**Option A: Pragmatic/comparative justification**
Acknowledge the limitation directly. Argue that the Y-BOCS is the most widely used severity measure, enabling cross-study comparison, and that your network indexes *severity-dimension interdependence* rather than symptom-content interdependence. Be explicit that this is a different (narrower) claim than OCD as a causal symptom network.

**Option B: Theoretical reframe**
Argue that the five severity facets (time, interference, distress, resistance, control) *do* have theoretical meaning as distinct psychological processes — e.g., distress and control failure can be causally ordered, resistance predicts maintenance, etc. You'd need to cite a model of OCD that actually gives these nodes causal status.

**Option C: Scope limitation**
Frame the analysis as characterizing the *functional architecture of OCD severity* — how severity facets co-vary and reinforce each other within a patient — rather than mapping OCD's symptom network. This is defensible but narrows your claims considerably.

## The Deeper Issue Worth Acknowledging

The reviewer may be gesturing at the **Haslbeck/Fried critique**: many published psychopathology networks are estimated from scales originally designed as reflective measures, so the resulting network topology partly reflects scale construction choices rather than disorder structure. If your Y-BOCS network has a specific topology (say, distress is central), that may tell you something about what the scale measures more than what OCD *is*.

**Bottom line for the response:** Don't just assert that Y-BOCS items are valid nodes — explain what claim you're making about what those nodes *are*, what causal or functional role they play, and what the network topology is supposed to represent. That reframing will address the reviewer's concern more convincingly than a defensive justification.
