Your supervisor's conclusion is understandable but psychometrically imprecise — and the reasoning has the causal arrow pointing in the wrong direction. Here's why.

---

## The Core Problem: Alpha Is Variance-Dependent

Cronbach's alpha is not an intrinsic property of a scale. It is a function of the sample you compute it in, and it is highly sensitive to **score variance**. The formula makes this explicit:

$$\alpha = \frac{k}{k-1} \left(1 - \frac{\sum \sigma^2_i}{\sigma^2_T}\right)$$

Alpha rises when total score variance (σ²_T) is large relative to item-level variance. Equivalently, using the mean inter-item correlation formulation:

$$\alpha = \frac{k\bar{r}}{1 + (k-1)\bar{r}}$$

Alpha is a monotonic function of the **average inter-item correlation**, and inter-item correlations are attenuated by **range restriction**. Less spread in scores → lower item correlations → lower alpha. This is a mechanical statistical artifact, not evidence of poor reliability.

---

## What Is Almost Certainly Happening in Your Inpatient Sample

Your inpatient sample has a mean of **26.4 out of 30** — sitting at **88% of the scale maximum**. That is a severe ceiling effect. Concretely:

- Most items are being endorsed at or near their maximum by most patients
- Items with near-zero variance contribute essentially nothing to inter-item covariance
- The total score distribution is heavily compressed toward the ceiling, drastically reducing σ²_T
- This mechanically deflates alpha **even if the items are measuring depression perfectly consistently**

The drop from α = .81 to α = .59 is almost certainly an artifact of this range restriction, **not evidence that the scale measures depression less reliably in severe patients**.

---

## What the Low Alpha Actually Signals

The more defensible interpretation is a **ceiling effect / discriminative validity problem**, not a reliability problem:

| What the Supervisor Claims | What the Data More Likely Show |
|---|---|
| Scale is unreliable in severe populations | Scale lacks item density at the high end of the severity continuum |
| Internal consistency breaks down | Score variance collapses near the ceiling |
| Scale should not be used with severe patients | Scale cannot *discriminate among* severely depressed patients |

These are meaningfully different. A scale can measure depression reliably but still fail to tell you whether a severely depressed patient scores a 26 versus a 28 — because it wasn't calibrated to spread people out in that range.

---

## Why the Recommendation Is Problematic

Your supervisor's conclusion leads to an odd clinical outcome: the scale is only recommended for the populations that need it least (mild-to-moderate), and excluded from the populations where measurement arguably matters most (acute/severe). The issue isn't that the scale *fails* in severe populations — it's that it was probably never designed to *discriminate within* the severe range.

Furthermore, restricting the recommendation based on a sample-dependent statistic like alpha, without correcting for range restriction, overstates the psychometric case considerably.

---

## What Analyses Would Be More Informative

To make a defensible recommendation, you'd want:

1. **Item-level ceiling inspection** — What % of inpatients are endorsing each item at maximum? Items with >80% ceiling endorsement are non-informative.

2. **Alpha with range-restriction correction** — Disattenuate the inter-item correlations using the Thorndike or similar correction before drawing conclusions.

3. **McDonald's ω (omega)** — More robust than alpha, less sensitive to distributional artifacts, and gives you both ω_hierarchical and ω_total for factor-based scales.

4. **IRT test information function** — This directly shows you *where on the severity spectrum* the scale provides reliable measurement. You will almost certainly see high information in the mid-range and rapid degradation near the ceiling. This is the right tool for the recommendation you're trying to make.

5. **Measurement invariance testing** — A CFA-based test of whether factor loadings and intercepts are equivalent across your two samples. If intercepts differ at the item level, that's more meaningful evidence of differential functioning than alpha alone.

---

## Bottom Line

The supervisor's **practical instinct** (the scale may not serve severe populations well) might be directionally correct, but the **psychometric reasoning** is flawed. Low alpha in the inpatient sample almost certainly reflects ceiling-induced range restriction, not genuine unreliability. The appropriate conclusion is that the scale **lacks discriminative sensitivity at the severe end of the continuum** — a ceiling/validity problem — not that it is unreliable there. Those are different problems with different implications for scale revision, use-case guidance, and clinical interpretation.
