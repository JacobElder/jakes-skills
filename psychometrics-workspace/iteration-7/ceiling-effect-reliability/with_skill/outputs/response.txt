## Your co-author is only half right — and the half she's missing is the decisive one.

She's correct that the **point estimate** (.87) clears the .90 threshold for conceptually related constructs (Henseler, Ringle & Sarstedt, 2015). But HTMT assessment has **two independent criteria**, and the second one fails here in a way that overrides the first.

---

### The Two-Part HTMT Decision Rule

| Criterion | Your Result | Pass/Fail |
|---|---|---|
| Point estimate < .90 (related constructs) | HTMT = .87 | ✅ Pass |
| 95% CI excludes 1.0 | CI = [.79, **1.03**] | ❌ **Fail** |

---

### Why the CI Is the More Informative Test

The bootstrapped CI is essentially a significance test of the null hypothesis that HTMT = 1.0 (i.e., the two factors are perfectly interchangeable — one construct, not two).

- An upper bound of **1.03 > 1.0** means you **cannot statistically distinguish your two factors from a single factor** at the 95% confidence level.
- The point estimate below .90 tells you your *sample estimate* is in an acceptable range, but the CI tells you the **true population value could plausibly be ≥ 1.0**.
- These two pieces of evidence are not interchangeable — you need *both* to confirm discriminant validity. Failing the CI criterion is a substantive finding, not a technicality.

This interpretation is consistent with Franke & Sarstedt (2019) and the broader consensus in the SEM literature: if the CI includes 1.0, discriminant validity is **not supported**, regardless of where the point estimate sits.

---

### What This Actually Means Substantively

For emotional exhaustion and depersonalization specifically, this finding is theoretically meaningful, not just a statistical inconvenience. These two MBI subscales are closely related by design, and an HTMT approaching or including 1.0 raises a legitimate question: **are you modeling two correlated facets of a single construct, or genuinely distinct dimensions?**

---

### What to Do From Here

1. **Report both pieces honestly.** The point estimate clears the threshold; the CI does not. Both should appear in your paper. Selectively reporting only the point estimate would misrepresent the evidence.

2. **Consider a bifactor or higher-order model.** If EE and DP share a large common variance component, a bifactor model (general burnout factor + specific residual factors) may fit better and be more theoretically defensible.

3. **Check convergent validity too.** Run AVE for each factor (Fornell-Larcker criterion) as a secondary check. If AVE for either subscale is < .50, you have compounding evidence of a measurement problem.

4. **Examine your sample size and item count.** CIs for HTMT widen substantially in smaller samples. If *n* < 200 or you have few indicators per factor, a sensitivity analysis with larger simulated samples can clarify whether this is a precision issue or a true construct overlap issue.

5. **Be explicit in the limitations section.** Even if you proceed with the two-factor structure (which may be theoretically justified), the CI result warrants a direct acknowledgment that the discriminant validity evidence is mixed.

---

**Bottom line:** Your co-author is using only half the criterion. The CI [.79, 1.03] contains 1.0, which is the threshold that matters for the confirmatory test of discriminant validity. The correct conclusion is that discriminant validity is **not confirmed** under the full HTMT framework.
