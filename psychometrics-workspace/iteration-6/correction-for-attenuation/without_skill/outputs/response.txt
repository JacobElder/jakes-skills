Your advisor's characterization is reasonable. An r = .31 is conventionally described as a small-to-modest correlation, and it's worth unpacking what that means for your study.

**What r = .31 tells you**

By Cohen's (1988) benchmarks — r = .10 small, r = .30 medium, r = .50 large — your correlation sits at the lower end of the "medium" range, which is consistent with your advisor calling it "modest at best." In terms of explained variance, r² = .31² ≈ .096, meaning working memory scores account for roughly 9.6% of the variance in academic achievement. That leaves 90% of the variance explained by other factors — study habits, prior knowledge, motivation, socioeconomic background, test-taking skills, and so on.

**The statistical significance piece**

The p < .001 result at N = 220 tells you the correlation is reliably different from zero — you're not looking at a chance finding. But statistical significance and practical significance are different things. With N = 220, you have enough power to detect even small correlations reliably. The fact that a correlation is significant doesn't mean it's large or practically important.

**Putting r = .31 in context**

Working memory is generally considered an important cognitive predictor of academic performance, and correlations in the .20–.40 range are typical in the literature for this relationship (Daneman & Carpenter, 1980; Gathercole et al., 2008). So your r = .31 is in the expected range — not surprising, but not pointing to a uniquely strong connection either.

Your advisor's suggestion that the two may "not be strongly related" is perhaps slightly overstated — a moderate correlation does indicate a real relationship — but the practical implication is fair: working memory alone is not a powerful predictor of academic achievement, and any intervention or assessment model relying primarily on working memory capacity would explain relatively little variance in outcomes.

**Recommendations**

1. Report both the r value and r² so readers can interpret effect size in terms of explained variance.
2. Consider whether there are moderating variables (e.g., academic domain, task demands) that might strengthen or attenuate the relationship in subgroups.
3. If you are building a predictive model, working memory would likely need to be combined with other predictors (executive function measures, prior GPA, domain knowledge) to achieve meaningful predictive accuracy.

Overall, r = .31 is a meaningful and statistically robust finding, but your advisor is right that "modest" is a fair description of its magnitude.
