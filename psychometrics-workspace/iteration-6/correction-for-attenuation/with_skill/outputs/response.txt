Your advisor's interpretation is not fair to the data — it conflates measurement imprecision with weak substantive relationships, which is a common and consequential error.

**The core issue: attenuation by measurement error**

When two measures each have imperfect reliability, their observed correlation (r_obs) is systematically lower than the correlation between the underlying true scores. This is not a sample artifact or a power issue — it is a structural property of observed-score correlations. Spearman (1904) formalized this with the correction for attenuation:

  r_true = r_obs / √(ρ_XX · ρ_YY)

where ρ_XX is the reliability of the predictor (working memory composite, alpha = .71) and ρ_YY is the reliability of the criterion (achievement test, test-retest = .74).

**Applying it to your numbers:**

  r_true = .31 / √(.71 × .74)
         = .31 / √(.5254)
         = .31 / .725
         ≈ .43

The disattenuation estimate is approximately r = .43 — a moderate-to-strong relationship by conventional benchmarks (Cohen, 1988: r = .30 medium, r = .50 large). Your advisor's "modest at best" framing is grounded in an observed correlation that has been pulled downward by measurement error in both variables. The substantive relationship between working memory capacity and academic achievement appears meaningfully stronger once you account for that error.

**Why this matters for interpretation**

Your advisor is effectively treating the observed correlation as though it reflects the relationship between the constructs themselves. It doesn't — it reflects the relationship between two imperfect indicators of those constructs. With reliabilities in the .70s (which are not unusual and not terrible), the attenuation is substantial: you're seeing roughly 72% of the true-score signal (since √(.71 × .74) ≈ .725, which is the "shrinkage factor"). The observed r = .31 is not "what's really there" — it's "what's really there, muffled."

This distinction matters especially for theory-building. Working memory and academic achievement are both latent constructs measured with error. Comparing your observed r = .31 to other published correlations in the literature is only appropriate if those studies had similar reliability levels. If others used higher-reliability measures, their observed correlations will look larger — not because the relationship is different, but because the measurement error is smaller.

**Caveats: the corrected value is an upper bound**

The corrected r ≈ .43 assumes your reliability estimates are accurate, which they may not be. Coefficient alpha for the working memory composite is a lower bound under tau-equivalence; if the composite is multidimensional or the items are not parallel, alpha underestimates true reliability. If alpha is actually higher than .71, r_true would be lower than .43. Conversely, test-retest reliability of .74 for an achievement test is reasonable but may conflate true score instability with measurement error (depending on the retest interval). The corrected value should be treated as a best estimate, not a definitive figure.

A cleaner formulation: the true-score correlation almost certainly lies somewhere between your observed r = .31 (lower bound, assuming maximum attenuation) and r ≈ .43 (upper bound, assuming the reliability estimates are accurate and all attenuation is from random measurement error).
