Your advisor's conclusion is based on an error — specifically, forgetting that split-half reliability requires the Spearman-Brown correction before it can be compared to any full-scale standard.

Here's what happened: when you split your 24 items into two 12-item halves and correlate the half-scale scores, the r = .64 you obtain is the reliability of a 12-item test, not a 24-item test. You've halved the scale, so you've measured the reliability of something half as long. Comparing that uncorrected value to a .70 threshold for a full-scale reliability coefficient is an apples-to-oranges comparison — of course it looks lower, you've cut the instrument in half.

The fix is the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula, which corrects for the fact that reliability increases as test length increases:

  rho_SB = 2 * rho_half / (1 + rho_half)

Plugging in your value:

  rho_SB = 2(.64) / (1 + .64) = 1.28 / 1.64 ≈ .78

Your full 24-item scale has an estimated reliability of approximately .78 — which clears the .70 threshold your advisor cited. The scale is adequately reliable as-is. Item revision to improve reliability is premature, and potentially counterproductive: you'd be solving a problem that doesn't exist.

This is one of the more consequential routine errors in applied psychometrics. The Spearman-Brown correction is not optional when interpreting split-half coefficients — it is definitionally required to translate a half-test correlation into a full-scale reliability estimate. Any split-half r, regardless of the scale length, must go through this correction before being compared to standards like .70 or .80. Not doing so systematically underestimates reliability and has led researchers to revise or discard perfectly adequate scales.

A few additional notes on your situation:

1. The .70 threshold itself comes from Nunnally (1978), who specified it for early-stage or basic research — he recommended .80+ for applied decisions (e.g., individual assessment). Know which standard actually applies to your use case.

2. If your scale has a plausible factor structure you haven't confirmed, it's worth running a quick parallel analysis and EFA alongside the reliability check. Low reliability can sometimes signal multidimensionality (items spread across sub-constructs rather than all loading on one factor), not just weak items.

3. For your writeup, report the corrected value: "Split-half reliability, corrected with the Spearman-Brown formula, was rho_SB = .78." Some reviewers may also ask for Guttman's Lambda-2 as an alternative estimate; it makes fewer assumptions than split-half SB.

Bottom line: your scale is fine. The advisor's recommendation was based on treating the uncorrected half-test correlation as if it were the full-scale reliability — once you apply the required correction, you're above threshold.
