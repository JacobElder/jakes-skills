Your advisor is right to flag this. A reliability coefficient of r = .64 falls below the widely-used .70 threshold (Nunnally, 1978), and for a 24-item scale that is a meaningful concern worth addressing before you proceed with data collection or analyses.

Here is what the finding tells you and what you can do about it:

**What r = .64 means**

The split-half correlation of .64 indicates that the two halves of your scale are not measuring the construct with sufficient consistency. A value below .70 is generally considered inadequate for research purposes, suggesting that items are not all pulling in the same direction — some may be poorly worded, ambiguous, or tapping a somewhat different construct than the others.

**Steps to improve reliability**

1. **Examine item-total correlations.** Run a corrected item-total correlation analysis for each of your 24 items. Items with corrected item-total correlations below .30 are weak candidates contributing noise. Removing or revising these is the most direct path to raising reliability.

2. **Review item content.** Look carefully at items with low inter-item correlations. Ask whether any items are double-barreled, negatively worded in a way that creates confusion, or simply measuring something adjacent rather than central to your construct.

3. **Consider item revision or replacement.** If several items cluster poorly with the rest, rewrite them to be more clearly indicative of the construct. In some cases, replacing problematic items entirely (and running a new pilot) is more efficient than revising marginal ones.

4. **Check for reverse-scored items.** Make sure all reverse-scored items have been correctly recoded before computing reliability. Failing to recode reverse items is a common cause of artificially deflated reliability.

5. **Pilot the revised scale.** After revising problematic items, pilot the updated scale with a small sample (n = 30–50) before proceeding to full data collection, to confirm that reliability has improved to an acceptable level.

**Benchmarks to aim for**

For most research purposes, reliability of .70–.79 is acceptable; .80+ is preferred for scales that will be used in high-stakes or applied contexts. Given that you have 24 items, there is room to improve reliability through targeted revision without drastically shortening the scale.

Your advisor's guidance to address this before moving forward is sound. Proceeding with a scale at r = .64 would limit the statistical power of downstream analyses and invite criticism in peer review.
