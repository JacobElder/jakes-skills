WHY r = .71 SUPPORTS CONVERGENT VALIDITY

Convergent validity refers to the degree to which a measure correlates with other measures of the same or theoretically related constructs. Emotional exhaustion and burnout are closely related constructs — exhaustion is widely considered a core component of burnout — so a high correlation between them is exactly what you would predict from theory.

r = .71 is a strong correlation by conventional standards (Cohen's benchmarks: small = .10, medium = .30, large = .50). A correlation this size, statistically significant at p < .001 with presumably adequate sample size, indicates that your new scale and the established burnout measure are measuring overlapping constructs in the way the theory predicts. This is the definition of convergent validity.

WHAT THIS TELLS YOU

- The two scales share approximately 50% of their variance (r² = .50), which is consistent with them measuring the same psychological domain.
- Related constructs should correlate — this is expected. The fact that r = .71 and not, say, r = .20, tells you the scales are tapping the same psychological space.
- The statistical significance (p < .001) means this is not a chance finding.

A convergent validity correlation in the range of .60–.80 for conceptually overlapping burnout-related scales is well within the range reported in the literature. Many established scales demonstrate convergent validity using correlations in exactly this range.

WHAT TO REPORT

In your manuscript, you can report: "The new emotional exhaustion scale demonstrated strong convergent validity, correlating r = .71 (p < .001) with scores on [burnout measure], consistent with the theoretical overlap between emotional exhaustion and burnout."

You may also want to check discriminant validity — that your scale does not correlate too highly with theoretically unrelated constructs. If your exhaustion measure correlates, say, .20 with a measure of job satisfaction (a distinct construct), that pattern of high convergent and low discriminant correlations strengthens the overall validity argument.

Overall, your co-author is right: r = .71 is strong convergent validity evidence for your scale.
