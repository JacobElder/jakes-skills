Your co-author is half-right, but the claim needs to be qualified carefully before it appears in a manuscript — because a single-method design creates a ceiling on what r = .71 can actually tell you.

THE COMMON METHOD VARIANCE PROBLEM

Emotional exhaustion and burnout are both self-report Likert scales administered in the same online survey session. That shared method — same rater (the participant), same format (Likert), same context (single survey sitting) — injects variance into both scores that has nothing to do with either construct. Response style effects (e.g., a person who consistently endorses higher scale points), acquiescence bias (tendency to agree regardless of content), and general negative affectivity all create covariance between your scales that inflates the observed correlation beyond what the true trait relationship would produce. The r = .71 is a trait-plus-method correlation, and you have no way to partition those two sources with a single-method design.

This is not a minor caveat. Podsakoff et al. (2003) estimated that common method variance inflates correlations by roughly .15–.25 on average in single-source studies; for two scales measuring psychologically adjacent constructs (emotional exhaustion is a central component of burnout in the Maslach model), the inflation can be larger. Your true-construct correlation could plausibly be substantially lower than .71.

WHAT THE MTMM FRAMEWORK REQUIRES

Campbell and Fiske (1959) introduced the multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) matrix precisely to address this. The logic: convergent validity is demonstrated when two measures of the same construct correlate highly across different methods (e.g., self-report exhaustion with supervisor-rated exhaustion). Discriminant validity is demonstrated when measures of different constructs within the same method correlate lower than measures of the same construct across methods. A same-method convergent correlation — even a large one — cannot establish convergent validity in the MTMM sense, because you cannot rule out that the correlation is driven by shared method rather than shared construct.

In your design, you have one trait (exhaustion/burnout) measured by one method. You have no off-diagonal MTMM entries — no cross-method comparisons — so you cannot satisfy the MTMM criterion for convergent validity. The MTMM framework requires at least two different methods to begin separating trait variance from method variance.

WHAT THE r = .71 DOES AND DOES NOT ESTABLISH

What it does establish: your new scale co-varies with an established burnout measure in the expected direction, at a magnitude consistent with the theoretical expectation that exhaustion is a central facet of burnout. This is a meaningful and necessary starting point — a correlation near zero would be a serious problem.

What it does not establish: that the covariance is driven by shared construct variance rather than shared method variance. r = .71 from a same-method, same-session design is necessary but not sufficient evidence for convergent validity. Any reviewer familiar with measurement will flag this.

WHAT STRONGER EVIDENCE REQUIRES

To make a defensible convergent validity claim, you need at minimum one of the following:

1. A second measurement method: Supervisor or observer ratings of exhaustion or burnout, a behavioral indicator (absenteeism records, performance data), or a physiological measure (cortisol, HRV). The correlation between your self-report scale and a non-self-report measure of the same construct is the MTMM-relevant convergent coefficient.

2. A CFA-MTMM model: If you collect data from two methods on both constructs, a confirmatory factor analytic version of the MTMM design (Widaman, 1985; Marsh & Grayson, 1995) lets you explicitly model trait factors and method factors and test convergent validity after partialling method variance.

3. At minimum, temporal or source separation: Administering one measure at Time 1 and another at Time 2 (separate sessions), or having a different respondent complete one measure, partially decouples the methods and reduces — though does not eliminate — common method inflation.

THE PRACTICAL FRAMING FOR YOUR PAPER

Do not discard the r = .71 — report it as preliminary convergent evidence with appropriate hedging. Suggested language: "As preliminary convergent validity evidence, scores on the new exhaustion scale correlated strongly with an established burnout measure (r = .71, p < .001). Both measures were self-report Likert scales administered in the same survey session; the observed correlation is therefore likely inflated by common method variance (Podsakoff et al., 2003) and should be interpreted as an upper bound on the true trait relationship. Multi-method designs consistent with the MTMM framework (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) are needed to separate trait variance from method variance and provide stronger convergent validity evidence."

That framing is accurate, shows methodological sophistication, and preempts the reviewer objection before it arrives.
