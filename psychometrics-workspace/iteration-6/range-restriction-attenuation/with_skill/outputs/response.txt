Your HR director's conclusion is premature, and the reason is a classic validity study design problem: range restriction.

**The core issue: you tested a range-restricted sample**

You administered the test to 180 *current employees* — not to the full applicant population. That sample is restricted in two ways:

1. **Predictor restriction**: Employees were already screened using (presumably) some ability-related criteria during hiring. Candidates who scored poorly likely never made it in the door. This truncates the ability score distribution from below.
2. **Criterion restriction**: Employees who performed poorly were likely managed out or left voluntarily over time. The retained workforce is not a random draw from all who were ever hired.

Both forces narrow the variance in both the predictor (cognitive ability scores) and the criterion (supervisor ratings). And when variance is compressed in either variable, the observed correlation shrinks — even if the underlying relationship is strong. This is the attenuation effect of range restriction. Your r = .19 is almost certainly *understating* the true validity of this test against the full applicant distribution.

**How much does it matter? Thorndike's (1949) correction**

The standard remedy is Thorndike's correction for range restriction (his Case 2, direct restriction on the predictor):

r_corrected = r_obs · (SD_u / SD_r) / √(1 − r²_obs + r²_obs · (SD_u / SD_r)²)

Where:
- r_obs = .19 (your observed coefficient)
- SD_r = SD of ability scores in your employed sample
- SD_u = SD of ability scores in the unrestricted applicant population (often available from test publisher norms)

To apply this, you need two numbers: the SD from your current-employee sample, and an estimate of the SD in the full applicant pool (test publishers typically provide this; alternatively, if you have historical applicant data, use that). Even a modest ratio of SD_u / SD_r = 1.4 (the restricted sample is about 30% narrower than the applicant pool — a conservative estimate for many hiring contexts) transforms r = .19 into approximately r_corrected ≈ .26. Ratios of 1.6–2.0, which are common for cognitive tests in selective hiring, push the corrected coefficient into the .30–.40 range — squarely in line with the well-established meta-analytic validity of cognitive ability tests for job performance (Schmidt & Hunter, 1998, report operational validities around .51 after full correction for range restriction and criterion unreliability).

**What about the "only 3.6% of variance" framing?**

This is a common and misleading rhetorical frame. The R² = .036 for r = .19 sounds tiny partly because bivariate R² in applied prediction is always small — virtually every real-world predictor of job performance explains a small share of variance individually. The practical value of a selection tool is better indexed by its utility (the gain in mean criterion performance from selecting the top X% of applicants), which depends on the validity coefficient, the selection ratio, and the base rate of success. A validity of r = .19 on range-restricted data, when corrected to r = .35 in the applicant population, has meaningful utility — especially if the test is part of a battery.

**What to actually do**

1. **Do not drop the test based on this coefficient alone.** The r = .19 estimate has a known downward bias whose magnitude you haven't yet quantified.
2. **Obtain the test publisher's applicant-pool SD** (or normative unrestricted SD) for the ability measure, and compute SD_r from your 180 employees.
3. **Apply Thorndike's correction** and report the corrected coefficient alongside the observed one.
4. **Also correct for criterion unreliability**: supervisor ratings are typically unreliable (interrater reliability for performance ratings often runs .50–.60). The correction for attenuation due to criterion unreliability is: r_true = r_obs / √ρ_yy. Correcting both range restriction and criterion unreliability compounds the upward adjustment.
5. **If you have historical applicant data**, a concurrent or predictive validity study on the full applicant distribution would be more defensible than the incumbent study design you've run.

**Bottom line**: The finding r = .19, p = .012, on a sample of retained employees using a test that was part of prior screening is exactly what range restriction theory predicts you would find even if the test is genuinely valid. Before recommending any change to your hiring process, you need the corrected validity estimate. Dropping a cognitively predictive test based on an attenuated coefficient from an incumbent sample is a common and costly mistake in applied selection.
