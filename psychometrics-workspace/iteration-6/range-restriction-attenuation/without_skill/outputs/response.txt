Your HR director raises a valid concern, and the statistics support her instinct.

**What r = .19 means**

A correlation of r = .19 is considered weak to modest by conventional standards. Squaring it gives R² = .036, meaning your cognitive ability test accounts for about 3.6% of the variance in supervisor performance ratings. That leaves 96.4% of the variance in job performance unexplained by the test — which is a reasonable basis for questioning whether the test is doing useful work in your selection process.

**Statistical significance vs. practical significance**

Your p = .012 tells you the result is statistically significant with N = 180, but statistical significance is not the same as practical significance. With a sample of that size, even a correlation of r = .15 would reach significance. The effect is real, but it is small.

**Is r = .19 good enough to justify a selection test?**

In selection research, the standards for "useful" validity are somewhat context-dependent, but many I/O psychologists would want to see validity coefficients in the range of r = .25–.35 or higher to justify the costs and administrative burden of testing — particularly if the test creates disparate impact concerns, which cognitive ability tests frequently do. At r = .19, the practical utility gain from using the test (improvement in mean performance of selected candidates over random selection) would be limited.

**Alternatives to consider**

If you decide to drop or supplement the cognitive ability test, alternatives with stronger validity evidence in the selection literature include:
- Structured behavioral interviews (typical validity around r = .35–.50)
- Work sample tests or job simulations (r ≈ .33–.54)
- Situational judgment tests (r ≈ .25–.43)
- Combinations of predictors using a multiple regression or composite scoring approach

**Recommendation**

Based on the data as reported, your HR director's concern appears justified. An observed validity of r = .19 is below what most selection professionals would consider a strong justification for continued use of a standalone cognitive test, especially weighed against the costs of administration, potential legal exposure, and candidate experience implications. I would recommend either replacing the test with a more valid alternative or investigating whether the test performs better in combination with other predictors in a battery.

If you want to make a stronger case for retaining the test, you would need to demonstrate better predictive validity — either by revising how you score or norm the test, or by collecting additional criterion data over a longer follow-up period.
