Your colleague is wrong, and this is one of the most consequential measurement mistakes in intervention research. Let me explain exactly why.

**The core problem: reliability is not inherited from components**

When you subtract pre from post to create a change score, the reliability of that difference is *not* the reliability of either component score. It follows a specific formula:

  ρ(X−Y) = (ρ_x·σ²_x + ρ_y·σ²_y − 2r_xy·σ_x·σ_y) / (σ²_x + σ²_y − 2r_xy·σ_x·σ_y)

Assuming equal variances at both time points (which is a reasonable first approximation for most intervention studies), this simplifies to:

  ρ(diff) ≈ (ρ_component − r_xy) / (1 − r_xy)

The variable that does all the damage is r_xy — the pre-post correlation. Here is why that matters: in a mindfulness intervention study, participants are assessed twice on the same construct using the same instrument. Pre-post correlations of .60–.80 are typical, and often higher when the interval is short or the construct is trait-like. As r_xy rises, the denominator of the formula shrinks dramatically while the numerator remains roughly constant, collapsing the reliability of the difference.

**A concrete number for your situation**

With alpha = .83 at both time points and a pre-post correlation of r = .70 (a conservative estimate for this kind of design):

  ρ(diff) ≈ (.83 − .70) / (1 − .70) = .13 / .30 ≈ **0.43**

That is not a rounding issue. Your DV — the quantity you are actually using in your analysis — has reliability of approximately .43, which is well below the .70 threshold for early-stage research and far below the .80+ standard for any applied or clinical decision. The good component alpha does not rescue this.

**The direction of the effect is counterintuitive but reproducible**

Your colleague's intuition assumes higher pre-post correlation is a sign the measure is working consistently, which would be good news. The opposite is true for difference scores: the more stable and consistently measured the construct is across time (higher r_xy), the *worse* your change score reliability becomes. This is the fundamental pathology of difference score designs.

**What to do instead**

Two alternatives that avoid this problem:

1. **ANCOVA on post-scores** (recommended first option). Use post-intervention wellbeing as the outcome, with pre-intervention wellbeing as a covariate and condition as a predictor. This statistically controls for baseline without ever subtracting it, preserving the full reliability of post-scores. ANCOVA on post with pre as covariate is mathematically more powerful than ANOVA on change scores and does not inherit the reliability collapse. This is the standard recommendation in Lord (1967) and remains best practice.

2. **Latent change score model in SEM** (recommended if you want to model change explicitly). A latent change score model (McArdle & Hamagami framework) in lavaan partitions change at the latent level, where measurement error has been removed. The latent difference is free of the attenuation problem that plagues observed difference scores. It requires lavaan or Mplus and more setup, but gives you genuine inference about change with appropriate standard errors.

**What to tell your colleague**

"Alpha = .83 at both time points" is correct and reassuring about the component scores. The error is the inference that this reliability transfers to the difference. It does not. Difference score reliability is governed by the formula above, and with typical pre-post correlations in intervention studies, the result is almost always disappointing regardless of how good the component reliability is. This is documented clearly in Cronbach & Furby (1970) and has not changed since.

Fix the analysis at the design stage (ANCOVA) rather than trying to salvage the change score. It is a cleaner solution and will receive less pushback from reviewers who know this literature.
