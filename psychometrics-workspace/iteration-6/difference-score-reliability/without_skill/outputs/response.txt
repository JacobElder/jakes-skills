Your colleague makes a reasonable point, and the good news is that alpha = .83 is a solid reliability estimate for your wellbeing scale. That puts you above the commonly cited .80 threshold that's recommended for research contexts, which means your measurement is on firm ground.

Since your scale has alpha = .83 at both the pre- and post-intervention time points, the underlying instrument is performing consistently and reliably. When you compute the change score (post minus pre), you're working with scores that were generated by a reliable measure, which means your change score DV benefits from that reliability. The scale's high internal consistency at both time points is a good indicator that your DV is capturing genuine change in wellbeing rather than noise.

A few practical points worth confirming:

1. **Report reliability at both time points** in your methods section. You've already checked this, which is the right approach — confirming alpha = .83 at baseline and again at follow-up gives reviewers confidence that the measure performed consistently across the study.

2. **Sample-specific reporting** — make sure you're reporting alpha from your own sample rather than just citing the original scale's published reliability. You've done this, which is methodologically sound.

3. **Consider omega as a supplement** — McDonald's omega is increasingly preferred alongside alpha because it doesn't assume all items contribute equally to the construct (tau-equivalence). If you run `psych::omega()` in R and get an omega value close to your alpha, that's additional confirmation your scale is working well.

4. **Floor and ceiling effects** — with a wellbeing scale, check whether your sample is bunched at the high end at baseline (ceiling effect) or the low end (floor effect). If most participants are already scoring near the top at pre-intervention, change scores will be restricted in range, which is a separate issue from reliability.

Overall, your colleague's assessment is reasonable: alpha = .83 at both time points is good evidence that your wellbeing measure is reliable, and proceeding with change scores as your DV is defensible. The main thing to watch is whether your intervention actually produces enough variability in change to detect an effect — but that's a power and effect size question, not a measurement one.
