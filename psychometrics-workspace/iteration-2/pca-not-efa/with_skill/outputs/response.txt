Not quite — there are three substantive problems with this approach, and I'd push back on your advisor's recommendation as stated.

---

## 1. PCA is not EFA, and that distinction matters here

The most important issue: **PCA and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) are fundamentally different analyses**, and conflating them is one of the most common mistakes in applied measurement work.

- **PCA** models *total* variance. Each component is a weighted linear combination of all observed items, and the components account for all the variance in the item set — including item-specific variance and measurement error. Components are determinate: you compute them directly from the data. PCA is the right tool for dimension reduction when you want to summarize a set of variables efficiently.

- **EFA** (common factor analysis) models *common* variance only. It separates variance that is shared among items (attributable to latent factors) from variance that is unique to each item (specific + error). Item variance decomposes as σ² = h² (communality) + u² (uniqueness). Factors are latent causes of item responses — the thing you're trying to measure.

If your goal is to claim that your three dimensions *are* latent constructs underlying "social connection" — i.e., that there's something you can't observe directly that is causing people to endorse items — you need the common factor model. PCA doesn't make that commitment. It's agnostic about causation; it's just a re-expression of the observed correlation matrix.

In practice, with many items and high communalities, PCA and EFA can yield similar-looking loading patterns. But with 15 items and moderate communalities (typical for this kind of scale), the two can diverge. PCA loadings tend to be inflated relative to factor loadings because they're accounting for unique variance too. More importantly: interpreting PCA components as subscales for a latent-variable measurement model is a category error. Your subscales represent something specific about a theory of social connection, and that calls for EFA.

**Bottom line:** Use EFA. Run PCA for data reduction if you want — it's not wrong as a technique — but do not present its components as subscales in a measurement model without flagging this loudly.

---

## 2. Varimax rotation is wrong for this construct

Varimax is an *orthogonal* rotation: it mathematically constrains the resulting factors/components to be uncorrelated with each other. That is almost never the right assumption for psychological constructs.

Ask yourself: would you expect the three dimensions of social connection to be completely unrelated to each other? Almost certainly not. If people score high on emotional closeness, they're probably somewhat more likely to score high on sense of belonging than people who score low. That's what it means to be constructs within the same broad domain.

Imposing orthogonality when the factors are actually correlated distorts your factor solution — it forces variance shared between correlated factors to be distributed across both in a way that can misassign items, inflate some loadings, and suppress others.

**Use an oblique rotation instead** — promax or oblimin. These allow factors to correlate. Then:
- Report the **pattern matrix** (standardized regression coefficients) for interpretation of which items belong to which factor.
- Report the **factor correlation matrix** — this tells you how correlated your subscales are, which is itself substantively meaningful.

If after running oblique rotation the factor correlations turn out to be near zero, then varimax and oblimin give approximately the same answer — no harm done by using oblique. But if they're correlated (likely), oblique will give you a more accurate picture. Oblique is strictly more general; there is no cost to using it.

---

## 3. The eigenvalue > 1 rule is unreliable

Kaiser's eigenvalue-greater-than-1 criterion is a convenient heuristic from 1960 that is known to systematically *overestimate* the number of factors/components, often badly. It has no principled statistical justification. SPSS defaults to this rule, which is why a whole generation of researchers treats it as authoritative — but it is not. With 15 items, eigenvalue > 1 will often suggest more factors than actually exist in the data.

The gold-standard approach for deciding how many factors to retain:

1. **Parallel analysis** (`psych::fa.parallel(data, fa = "fa")` in R): compare your observed eigenvalues to eigenvalues generated from random data of the same dimensions. Retain only factors whose observed eigenvalue exceeds the random-data eigenvalue. This is the method with the strongest empirical support.

2. **Velicer's MAP (Minimum Average Partial)**: minimizes average squared partial correlation after sequentially removing each factor. `psych::nfactors()` reports this.

3. **Scree plot**: look for the "elbow." Subjective, but useful alongside parallel analysis.

4. **Theoretical interpretability**: if your third factor has 3 items and you can't give it a coherent name, that's evidence it isn't real. Factor count is a substantive decision, not a mechanical one.

You may well end up with 3 factors — that's plausible — but you should verify that with parallel analysis rather than eigenvalue > 1.

---

## 4. Polychoric correlations (while we're here)

One more thing: since your items are Likert-scale, using Pearson correlations (which SPSS's PCA does by default) underestimates the true correlation between the underlying traits. This biases loadings downward and can produce a spurious "difficulty factor" that separates items by endorsement frequency rather than content. The appropriate input for EFA with Likert items is a **polychoric correlation matrix**, which treats item responses as discretized versions of underlying continuous variables.

In R: `psych::fa(..., cor = "poly")` handles this directly.

---

## What you should actually do

Replace the PCA with an EFA:

```r
library(psych)

# Step 1: Determine number of factors using parallel analysis
fa.parallel(your_data, fa = "fa", cor = "poly")
# cor = "poly" because your items are Likert (ordinal)

# Step 2: Run EFA with oblique rotation and polychoric correlations
efa_result <- fa(your_data,
                 nfactors = 3,        # or whatever parallel analysis suggests
                 fm = "minres",       # minimum residual; robust, doesn't require normality
                 rotate = "oblimin",  # oblique rotation — allows factors to correlate
                 cor = "poly")        # polychoric correlations for Likert items

# Step 3: Inspect results
print(efa_result, cut = .30, sort = TRUE)  # pattern matrix
efa_result$Phi  # factor correlation matrix
```

If parallel analysis agrees with your advisor that 3 factors is the right number, that's reassuring — but now you're getting there through a method that justifies a latent-variable subscale interpretation, uses an appropriate correlation matrix for ordinal data, and allows subscales to correlate as they likely do.

Then, if you want to do this properly: treat this EFA as exploratory. On an independent sample or a random holdout split, run a **CFA** to confirm the structure before writing up the three subscales as established. Running EFA and CFA on the same data is double-dipping. And report **omega** (not just alpha) for each subscale — alpha assumes tau-equivalence (equal loadings) which is almost never true; `psych::omega()` or `semTools::compRelSEM()` after a lavaan CFA gives you the right estimate.

## Summary

| Issue | Current approach | Better approach |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Analysis type | PCA (total variance) | EFA (common/latent variance) |
| Rotation | Varimax (orthogonal, forces zero factor correlation) | Oblimin/Promax (oblique, allows correlation) |
| Factor count criterion | Eigenvalue > 1 (overextracts) | Parallel analysis + MAP + interpretability |
| Correlation input | Pearson (biases loadings on Likert) | Polychoric |
| Confirmation | None | CFA on independent sample |

Your advisor's recommendation reflects a workflow that was standard in the 1980s and is still unfortunately common. The items above aren't pedantry — each one affects whether the subscales you report actually measure what you claim they measure.
