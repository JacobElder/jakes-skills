It sounds like you're on the right track! PCA with varimax rotation and the eigenvalue > 1 rule is a very common and widely accepted approach for identifying underlying structure in survey data, and getting 3 components from 15 items is a reasonable outcome. That said, there are a few things to double-check before finalizing your subscales.

## What you've done looks reasonable

PCA with varimax rotation is a standard exploratory technique in social science research, and using eigenvalue > 1 (the Kaiser criterion) is the most common method for deciding how many components to retain. SPSS makes this easy to do and it's what most applied researchers use. Getting 3 interpretable components from 15 items measuring a single broad construct like social connection is quite plausible.

## Things to verify

**Check that the components make sense substantively.** Look at the items with the highest loadings on each component. Do they cluster around distinct themes? For example, one component might capture emotional closeness, another might capture frequency of social contact, and a third might capture sense of belonging. If the components are interpretable and make theoretical sense, that's a good sign.

**Check for cross-loadings.** Items that load strongly on more than one component (e.g., loading ≥ .40 on two components) can be problematic. You may want to consider dropping those items or using only the items that cleanly define each subscale.

**Look at the variance explained.** Check how much total variance your 3 components explain. If the three components together explain, say, 55–65% of the total variance, that's reasonable. Very low percentages might suggest the solution isn't capturing the main structure well.

**Consider reliability.** Once you've defined your subscales, compute Cronbach's alpha for each one. A common rule of thumb is α ≥ .70 for acceptable reliability. If any subscale falls below this, you might need to revise the item composition.

## A potential caveat about varimax

One thing worth noting is that varimax rotation assumes your components are uncorrelated (orthogonal) with each other. If your three aspects of social connection are actually related to each other (which intuitively they might be — people with more emotional closeness might also feel a stronger sense of belonging), then an oblique rotation method (like promax or oblimin) might actually fit better. Oblique rotation allows components to correlate. In practice, for many psychological constructs, oblique and orthogonal rotation give similar results, but it's worth trying both and seeing if the solutions differ.

## Computing subscale scores

If your components look good and your items are clean, computing subscale scores as simple sum scores (or averages) of the items with the highest loadings on each component is a very common and defensible approach. Just be sure to reverse-score any negatively-worded items before summing.

## Bottom line

Your approach is solid and follows standard practice. The main things to verify are that the components are interpretable, items load cleanly, and reliability is acceptable for each subscale. If you want to be extra thorough, try an oblique rotation to see if you get a meaningfully different (or similar) solution.
