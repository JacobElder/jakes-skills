Short answer: not yet — and the reason isn't statistical power, it's measurement.

Before you interpret that M = 3.9 → 4.4 shift as evidence of improved psychological safety, you need to establish **longitudinal measurement invariance**. This step is almost universally skipped in pre-post intervention work, and skipping it can make a measurement artifact look like a program effect (or, less commonly, hide a real effect).

---

## The core problem: did the training change how people use the scale?

A 6-week mindfulness program doesn't just potentially change psychological safety — it changes people. Specifically, it can change how employees interpret survey items and calibrate their responses. This phenomenon goes by several names: **response shift**, **referent recalibration**, **reconceptualization**. After a training that explicitly focuses attention on psychological safety norms, employees may:

- Understand "psychological safety" differently at T2 (reconceptualization)
- Apply a different internal standard when rating how "safe" they feel (recalibration)
- Have a genuinely different threshold for what counts as a 4 vs. a 5 on the scale

When any of those happen, an item's intercept shifts between occasions. The item no longer has the same expected score at the same underlying level of psychological safety. Your observed mean difference (Δ = 0.5) then conflates **real construct change** with **scale recalibration**, and you can't tell which it is — or in what proportion — without testing.

---

## What you need to test: longitudinal measurement invariance

The same configural → metric → scalar sequence that applies to cross-group comparisons applies to cross-time comparisons. You're treating "time" (pre vs. post) as the grouping factor.

**Configural invariance**: Same items load on the same factor at T1 and T2. This tests whether the basic structure of psychological safety is the same across occasions. If it fails, you have a more serious problem than a mean shift.

**Metric (weak) invariance**: Factor loadings are equal across time. Items relate to the latent factor with the same sensitivity at T1 and T2. If this fails, even comparing the variances or the factor structure across time is problematic.

**Scalar (strong) invariance**: Item intercepts are equal across time. This is the level you need to compare latent means. If scalar invariance holds, a shift in the latent mean can be interpreted as a genuine change in psychological safety. If it fails, the observed mean shift is contaminated by intercept drift — the scale has recalibrated, not (or not only) the construct.

---

## Why this is especially likely to matter for intervention research

Standard cross-group invariance (e.g., gender, country) tests whether the scale means the same thing to different kinds of people. Longitudinal invariance after an intervention tests something more pointed: whether the intervention itself altered the measurement instrument.

Mindfulness and psychological safety training are precisely the kinds of interventions that can cause response shift. Teaching employees to think carefully about their team's communication norms — which is roughly what a psychological safety program does — changes the referent they use when answering questions like "I feel safe speaking up." A pre-trained employee might interpret "safe" relative to a baseline they've never examined. A post-trained employee may have a sharpened, more demanding internal standard. They score higher on the scale not because things objectively improved, but because their threshold for a high score dropped, or vice versa.

Golembiewski, Billingsley & Yeager (1976) gave the canonical taxonomy of this: alpha change (true change on a stable scale), beta change (rescaling of the metric), and gamma change (reconceptualization of the construct). Your paired t-test can only detect the combination of all three; it cannot decompose them.

---

## How to test it

With lavaan and semTools, the longitudinal case is handled by using time as the grouping factor, with persons appearing in both groups (same N, matched). Because the items are Likert (7-point), use WLSMV or MLR with polychoric correlations:

```r
library(lavaan)
library(semTools)

# Model: single-factor, 7 items
model <- 'psych_safety =~ ps1 + ps2 + ps3 + ps4 + ps5 + ps6 + ps7'

# Reshape data to long if needed, or specify as two-group with person as cluster
# For longitudinal, this is often modeled as a two-wave model

# Option A: Treat time as a grouping variable (wide-to-long, group = "wave")
fit_config <- cfa(model, data = d_long, group = "wave",
                  ordered = paste0("ps", 1:7))
fit_metric <- cfa(model, data = d_long, group = "wave",
                  group.equal = "loadings",
                  ordered = paste0("ps", 1:7))
fit_scalar <- cfa(model, data = d_long, group = "wave",
                  group.equal = c("loadings", "intercepts"),
                  ordered = paste0("ps", 1:7))

lavTestLRT(fit_config, fit_metric, fit_scalar)
```

For ordinal items, the threshold parameterization matters; use `semTools::measEq.syntax()` with `parameterization = "theta"` and Wu & Estabrook (2016) identification constraints if you want to do this rigorously.

Also examine **ΔCFI** and **ΔRMSEA** alongside the chi-square difference test. With a reasonably large N, Δχ² will often reject scalar invariance even when the intercept differences are trivial in practice. Chen (2007) guidelines: ΔCFI ≤ −.010, ΔRMSEA ≥ .015 as practical thresholds.

---

## If invariance holds at scalar level

Then you're in good shape to compare latent means. At that point, you can run the latent mean comparison (or equivalently, the observed-score paired t-test as an approximation) and a statistically significant difference with Δ = 0.5, SD ≈ 0.8 is meaningful — Cohen's d ≈ 0.625, which is a moderate-to-large effect.

But you'd also want to address: is there a control group? Pre-post in a single group conflates training effects with regression to the mean, history effects, demand characteristics (employees wanting to appear more positive after a company-sponsored program), and natural maturation. A waitlist control or matched comparison group would strengthen causal claims considerably.

---

## If scalar invariance fails

You're not stuck — partial scalar invariance may be defensible. Identify which items show intercept drift (the largest modification indices), free those intercepts, and confirm at least 2 items per factor remain invariant (Byrne, Shavelson & Muthén, 1989; Steenkamp & Baumgartner, 1998). If only 1-2 items are driving the failure, you can partially anchor the comparison and interpret the latent mean difference cautiously, with appropriate caveats.

If most items show drift, that's substantively interesting in its own right: the training may have fundamentally shifted how employees conceptualize psychological safety, which is arguably a program goal — but it makes the pre-post comparison uninterpretable on the original metric.

---

## Bottom line

The M = 3.9 → 4.4 shift is suggestive, but interpreting it as evidence of improved psychological safety requires first establishing scalar invariance across occasions. Without it, you don't know whether you're seeing a real construct change, a recalibration of how employees use the scale, or some mixture of the two. Run the invariance sequence before you touch a paired t-test.
