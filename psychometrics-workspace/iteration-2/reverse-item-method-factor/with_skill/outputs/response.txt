The 10/10 positive/negative split is standard practice in the sense that you'll see it in most scale-development textbooks and a large fraction of published personality measures — but "standard" and "good" are two different things here, and this particular standard has a well-documented empirical problem you need to plan around before you collect a single response.

**The method factor problem**

When you put 10 negatively-worded items in an EFA or CFA alongside 10 positively-worded items, what you very often get is not one clean personality factor. You get two factors: the substantive construct you were trying to measure, and a method factor defined almost entirely by item wording direction. Positively-worded items load together; negatively-worded items load together — even when they're supposed to be measuring the same thing. This has been replicated across constructs including self-esteem (the Rosenberg scale is the classic case), neuroticism, life satisfaction, and others.

That second factor isn't your personality dimension. It's an artifact of how respondents process negative syntax. It will appear as a real dimension in your EFA. If you then use parallel analysis or an eigenvalue criterion and extract two factors, you'll conclude you measured a two-dimensional construct when you set out to measure one. Your factor count is now wrong, and any downstream analyses (subscale scoring, structural models) will be built on that error.

In CFA, you'll find that a single-factor model fits very poorly, while a two-factor model — or a one-factor model with a correlated method factor for negatively-worded items — fits substantially better. Modification indices will be screaming at you to correlate residuals among your reverse-coded items. That's not model refinement; that's the method factor showing up.

**The acquiescence problem, honestly**

The reason for including reverse-keyed items in the first place is acquiescence bias — the tendency of some respondents to agree regardless of item content. The theory is that if half the items are reverse-worded, acquiescent responding inflates scores on positively-worded items but deflates them on negatively-worded items, and these two effects cancel out in the sum score.

The problem is that this works as a cancellation strategy only if the negatively-worded items function symmetrically with the positively-worded ones on the underlying construct. In practice they often don't. Respondents interpret negatively-worded items differently — they require additional cognitive processing ("Not being anxious" is not simply the opposite of "Being anxious" for every respondent), and they activate a response set that's somewhat distinct from the set triggered by straightforward positive items. The result is that your reverse-coded items may have lower factor loadings, lower corrected item-total correlations, and higher residual variances than your positive items. They're doing worse measurement work, not better.

Blanton and Jaccard (2006) and Rodebaugh et al. (2007) lay this out clearly. Kam and Meyer (2015) showed that the acquiescence-control benefit of reverse-keyed items is much smaller than traditionally assumed, while the method-factor cost is real and often substantial.

**What to do instead**

You have a few options, depending on your goals:

1. **Reduce or eliminate negatively-worded items and write all-positive items thoughtfully.** This sounds heretical but works. For many personality constructs, a unidimensional scale of carefully written positive items will outperform a mixed-wording scale that has to contend with method-factor contamination. You lose the acquiescence "correction" but gain clean measurement.

2. **If you must use reverse-worded items, pilot them rigorously.** Cognitive interviews will tell you whether respondents are processing "I rarely feel sad" as the functional opposite of "I feel sad" or as a different kind of item entirely. Items that survive cognitive interviews and show good item statistics in pilot data are candidates to keep; those that don't should be dropped regardless of your 10/10 plan.

3. **Plan to test and model the method factor explicitly.** In your CFA, specify a priori that negatively-worded items may share residual covariance (a method factor), and compare: (a) one-factor model, (b) one-factor model with a method factor for negatively-worded items, (c) two-factor model. This is the Marsh (1996) approach for self-concept scales, and it's the honest way to handle the situation. If you find the method factor is substantial, you can partial it out analytically — but now you have a more complex model that requires justification.

4. **Consider balanced item construction as an alternative to wording-direction balance.** You can write items that tap opposite poles of a trait without using negative syntax — "I feel calm in stressful situations" vs. "I feel overwhelmed easily" — where both are grammatically positive but substantively tap different regions of the trait continuum.

**For your 20-item scale specifically**

I would not write 10 negatively-worded items with the expectation that they'll function symmetrically with your positive items. The more defensible plan: write 20–25 positive items, run a pilot, and prune to the best 15–20 based on item-total correlations, distributional properties, and cognitive interview findings. Then at EFA/CFA, you have a clean unidimensional model to test. You can still examine inter-item correlation patterns for signs of acquiescence clustering if you're worried about that response style.

If you do use negatively-worded items, treat the 10/10 split as a hypothesis to test, not a design feature that controls for something. Build the method-factor CFA before you finalize your scale structure. And report both the clean-one-factor and the method-factor models so reviewers can see what you found.

The short version: the 10/10 split is a reflex from an older literature. The empirical record since the mid-1990s says it often creates more measurement problems than it solves.
