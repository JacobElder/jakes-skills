Short answer: no, ICC = 0.72 over 4 weeks is not good reliability for a state anxiety scale — it's actually a red flag.

Here's the core problem. Test-retest correlation (and ICC) conflates two distinct things: the stability of the construct and the reliability of measurement. For a trait construct — something like Big Five Neuroticism or dispositional affect — you want high test-retest stability over weeks or months, because that's what "trait" means. For a state measure, the construct is explicitly defined as transient, fluctuating with current circumstances. If your state anxiety scale yields ICC = 0.72 over 4 weeks, the most parsimonious interpretation is not "great reliability" — it's that your scale is functioning more like a trait measure than a state measure.

Put differently: genuine state anxiety should look quite different in the same person at two points 4 weeks apart, especially in the absence of any particular anxiety-inducing manipulation. If scores correlate at r ≈ .72 (roughly what ICC ≈ .72 implies), the scale is picking up stable between-person variance — individual differences in baseline anxiety proneness, i.e., trait anxiety — rather than within-person fluctuation in current state. The Spielberger STAI distinction between State (Form Y-1) and Trait (Form Y-2) exists precisely because this conflation is a known measurement problem.

What's the appropriate test-retest interval for a state measure? Short — very short. Same session (immediate retest), 24–48 hours at most, and ideally with an active mood induction or situational manipulation so you can test whether scores track the manipulation. At 4 weeks, you are not measuring equivalence of the measurement procedure; you are measuring stability of a construct that, by definition, should not be stable. The high ICC you obtained is not evidence of reliability — it's evidence of construct contamination.

The more granular question: what exactly does ICC = 0.72 mean here? You'd want to know which ICC model you used (Shrout & Fleiss ICC(2,1) absolute agreement is typical for test-retest; ICC(3,1) consistency is sometimes used but gives an inflated estimate when systematic session drift is present). Either way, a value of .72 over 4 weeks should trigger concern, not satisfaction.

What you should actually do:

1. Examine within-person change scores across the two sessions. If the variance of change scores is small, that is additional evidence that scores are not behaving as state measures should.

2. Check whether your scale correlates more strongly with established trait anxiety measures (e.g., STAI-Trait, IPIP Neuroticism) than with validated state measures administered concurrently — that pattern would confirm the trait-contamination hypothesis.

3. Consider whether items have been written in a way that pulls for dispositional interpretations ("I tend to feel anxious" rather than "I feel anxious right now"). State items should use present-tense, momentary language and ideally reference the current situation explicitly.

4. If you want to assess internal consistency reliability (a different question from stability), use McDonald's omega from a factor model on the item-level data from a single time point. That gives you reliability as a property of the measurement process, unconfounded by construct stability.

Bottom line: reframe your concern. The problem isn't that ICC = 0.72 is "too high" in some abstract sense — it's that a high ICC at a 4-week interval is theoretically inconsistent with what state anxiety is supposed to be. Treating this as acceptable reliability would be a mistake; a reviewer who understands the state-trait distinction will flag it immediately.
