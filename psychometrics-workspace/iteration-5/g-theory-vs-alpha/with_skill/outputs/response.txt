Your advisor is not right — and the problem is not the value of alpha (.81 is fine on its face), it's that alpha is the wrong statistic entirely for the design you ran.

Here is the core issue.

**Why Cronbach's alpha is inappropriate here**

Cronbach's alpha is designed for a single-facet design: persons × items only. In that setup, all variance that isn't attributable to persons is treated as "error" and goes into the denominator. That's fine when items are the only source of measurement variability.

Your design is person × item × rater × session — a fully crossed four-facet design. When you compute alpha on item scores averaged across raters and sessions, you are collapsing rater variance, session variance, the rater × item interaction, the rater × session interaction, the session × item interaction, and their higher-order interactions all into a single undifferentiated error term. Alpha cannot distinguish these sources from each other, and it cannot distinguish them from true person variance. The result is a number that is not interpretable as reliability for this design. Alpha may be too high (rater variance is large, but averaging across raters suppresses it) or too low (session instability inflates apparent error), but either way you don't know which, and you can't act on the result.

**The correct framework: Generalizability Theory**

Generalizability theory (G theory; Cronbach, Gleser, Nanda & Rajaratnam, 1972) was built precisely for this situation. It treats raters, items, and sessions as facets of measurement — each a random sample from a universe of admissible observations — and uses variance components (from a random-effects ANOVA or mixed model) to decompose observed-score variance into its sources.

The analysis proceeds in two stages:

**G study.** Fit a fully crossed random-effects model to estimate variance components for each main effect and interaction: σ²(person), σ²(item), σ²(rater), σ²(session), σ²(person×item), σ²(person×rater), σ²(person×session), σ²(item×rater), σ²(item×session), σ²(rater×session), and σ²(person×item×rater×session, e) — the residual. This tells you how much variance each source contributes to observed scores.

**D study.** The decision study uses the G-study variance components to predict reliability-like coefficients under different design configurations — any combination of n_i items, n_r raters, and n_s sessions you might consider deploying. The D study produces:

- **G coefficient (Eρ²)** — analogous to reliability for *relative* decisions (ranking people)
- **Phi coefficient (Φ)** — analogous to reliability for *absolute* decisions (is this person above a cut score?)

The practical value of the D study is decisive for design optimization. You can answer: "Does my G coefficient improve more if I go from 3 to 5 raters, or from 2 to 3 sessions?" This is a question alpha cannot answer, because alpha has already collapsed rater and session variance into a single error residual. G theory keeps them separated so you can price the tradeoff.

For example: if σ²(person×rater) is large and σ²(person×session) is small, adding raters buys you more reliability per measurement unit than adding sessions. If item variance is the dominant error source, neither strategy helps much and you need more items.

**Your specific design**

With 45 persons, 20 behaviors (items), 3 raters, and 2 sessions fully crossed, you have the data to run a complete G study. The variance components for all main effects and interactions are estimable. Run the G study first; examine which facet interactions with persons are largest — those are your biggest error sources. Then use the D study to find the most cost-efficient path to the G coefficient you need.

**R implementation**

Two approaches:

```r
# Option 1: gtheory package (purpose-built for G theory)
library(gtheory)

# Data should be in long format: one row per person × item × rater × session combination
# Columns: person, item, rater, session, score

gstudy_result <- gstudy(
  data = long_data,
  formula = score ~ (1|person) + (1|item) + (1|rater) + (1|session) +
            (1|person:item) + (1|person:rater) + (1|person:session) +
            (1|item:rater) + (1|item:session) + (1|rater:session)
)

# D study: predict reliability under different n_raters, n_items, n_sessions
dstudy_result <- dstudy(
  gstudy_result,
  colname.objects = "person",
  data = long_data,
  colname.scores = "score"
)

# Option 2: lme4 for variance components, then compute G and Phi by hand
library(lme4)

fit <- lmer(score ~ 1 + (1|person) + (1|item) + (1|rater) + (1|session) +
```

The `gtheory` package automates the D-study projections; `lme4` gives you more control and handles edge cases (negative variance estimates, unbalanced designs) better.

**Bottom line**

Alpha .81 tells you that if you averaged scores across your 3 raters and 2 sessions, the resulting 20-item composite has decent internal consistency within that averaged data. It tells you nothing about whether your raters are a meaningful source of variance, whether sessions differ, or whether you could achieve the same reliability with fewer raters and more sessions. For a behavioral observation coding scheme with this design, G theory is not optional — it is the appropriate reliability framework, and the D study will give you the actionable design guidance that alpha cannot.
