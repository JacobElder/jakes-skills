Your advisor is right — an alpha of .81 is generally considered good reliability. Here's why that conclusion holds up, along with a few nuances worth knowing.

**Alpha .81 clears the standard thresholds**

The most widely cited benchmark for reliability in applied research is Nunnally's (1978) .80 criterion. Your alpha of .81 meets that standard. For research contexts requiring individual-level decisions, .90 is the typical target, but for group-level research and instrument evaluation, .80+ is well-regarded. You are above that line.

**Your design adds some complexity worth noting**

You mentioned a fully crossed person × item × rater × session design — this is more elaborate than a typical survey where only items vary. A few things are worth considering:

First, by averaging item scores across raters and sessions before computing alpha, you are summarizing the items at a somewhat processed level. The alpha you computed reflects the internal consistency of the 20 averaged item scores, which is a reasonable way to assess whether the items are measuring a common construct.

Second, with 3 raters and 2 sessions, there is some degree of rater and session variance built into those averaged scores. The averaging process stabilizes the scores across raters and occasions, which is part of why your alpha is as high as it is. This is actually a strength — when averaged across multiple raters and sessions, the scores tend to be more reliable than any single rater's or single session's scores would be.

**Interrater reliability is a separate question**

Alpha addresses item-level internal consistency. Since you have multiple raters, it is also worth separately computing interrater reliability, typically using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). This would tell you how consistently your 3 raters agreed in their behavioral codings, independent of item-level consistency. If ICC is also strong (say, above .75), that is further convergent evidence for the reliability of your coding scheme.

**Summary**

An alpha of .81 from a 20-item behavioral observation scheme is solid. Your advisor's assessment is sound. The main follow-up I'd recommend is reporting the ICC for interrater agreement separately so reviewers can see both the item-level consistency and the rater-level consistency of your measurement approach.
