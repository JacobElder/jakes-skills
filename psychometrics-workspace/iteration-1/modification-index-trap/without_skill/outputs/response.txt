No — you shouldn't just add those modifications and report the improved model without careful caveats. Here's why:

## The "MI-chasing" problem

Modification indices (MIs) show you which parameter additions would most improve model fit in your current sample. The critical word is "in your current sample." Adding parameters because they improve fit — without an independent theoretical or substantive reason — is essentially mining your data for post-hoc adjustments. This is sometimes called "capitalizing on chance": the modifications you're adding may reflect idiosyncratic features of your particular sample (sampling error, specific population characteristics) rather than anything real about the underlying construct structure.

When you start from a confirmatory model and then freely modify it based on empirical output, you've shifted from confirmatory to exploratory mode. The resulting model needs to be treated accordingly.

## What you should do instead

**1. Examine each proposed modification on its merits**

For each modification indices suggestion, ask: Is there a plausible substantive reason this parameter should exist, *independent* of the fact that it improves fit?

- Correlated residuals: These are often defensible if items share wording, administration method, or content beyond the factor (e.g., two items that both use negative framing, or two items asking about the same behavior in different time frames). They're not defensible just because they're suggested by the MI.
- Cross-loadings: Does it make theoretical sense that an item taps two constructs? Is there something about the item content that would predict this?

If you can't articulate a reason before looking at the MI value, the modification is empirically driven.

**2. Look at standardized residuals, not just global fit**

Global fit indices (CFI, RMSEA) tell you the model misfits — they don't tell you *where* or *why*. Examine the matrix of standardized residual covariances (sometimes called normalized residuals). This will show you which specific item pairs are being poorly reproduced. That diagnostic step can reveal whether the misfit is localized (a few problematic items) or distributed, and can point to theoretically meaningful patterns.

**3. Replicate before claiming the modifications are real**

If you do make modifications with substantive justification, the resulting model should be validated in an independent sample or via cross-validation before it's presented as a confirmed structure. CFI = .95 in one sample may not replicate. Overfitted models almost always show worse fit when applied to new data.

**4. Be transparent about what kind of modeling you've done**

A model that started as CFA but incorporated data-driven modifications is no longer a confirmatory model — it's an exploratory respecification. This should be clearly stated in your paper. Presenting it as confirmatory overstates what you've established. The appropriate framing is something like: "Based on initial poor fit and subsequent exploratory respecification, we identified a modified model. This modified model requires replication in an independent sample."

## Bottom line

Your current fit (CFI = .89, RMSEA = .08) is poor by conventional standards, but the solution is not to add modifications to reach an arbitrary threshold. Before modifying:

1. Ask whether the original theoretical model was correctly specified to begin with
2. Inspect standardized residuals to understand the nature of the misfit
3. Only add modifications you can justify substantively
4. Report any modified model as exploratory and replicate it

Adding four modifications (three correlated residuals + one cross-loading) and reporting CFI = .95 without addressing these issues would produce a model that fits your data better but may not generalize — and would misrepresent your research as confirmatory when it has become exploratory.
