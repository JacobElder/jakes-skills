For your scenario — two clinicians rating 50 patients on a 5-point severity scale — you should use ICC (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient), not Cohen's kappa.

## Why not Cohen's kappa?

Cohen's kappa is designed for categorical agreement on nominal (or at best ordinal) data where you want to assess whether two raters place observations into the same discrete categories. It treats each category as a separate bin and ignores the magnitude of disagreement — a clinician rating a patient "1" vs. "2" counts the same as rating "1" vs. "5." For a severity scale, that's clearly wrong.

Even weighted kappa (which does account for degree of disagreement on ordinal scales) has been shown to be a special case of ICC under certain conditions — and if you're going to that effort, you may as well use ICC directly with full flexibility.

## Why ICC?

ICC treats your 5-point ratings as an interval-level (or at least continuous-ish) measure and quantifies the proportion of total variance attributable to between-patient differences. It directly models clinician-level variance as a nuisance source. This is the right frame: you want to know whether the relative ordering of patients is reproducible across clinicians, and optionally, whether the absolute level of ratings agrees.

## Which ICC variant to use?

ICC is not a single statistic — there are multiple forms depending on your design assumptions. The two most common classification systems are Shrout & Fleiss (1979) and McGraw & Wong (1996). The `psych::ICC()` function in R reports all major variants with both sets of labels.

The key decisions are:

**1. Model (1, 2, or 3 in Shrout & Fleiss notation):**
- **ICC(1)**: Each patient is rated by a different random pair of clinicians drawn from a larger pool. Rarely applicable.
- **ICC(2)**: The same set of clinicians rates all patients, AND those clinicians are treated as a random sample from a larger population of clinicians. Appropriate if you want results to generalize to other clinicians.
- **ICC(3)**: The same clinicians rate all patients, AND those specific clinicians are the only ones of interest (fixed effects). More appropriate if your two clinicians are the definitive raters (e.g., scoring a fixed protocol).

For your scenario — two specific clinicians who happen to be representative of a broader clinical context — **ICC(2,1)** is usually the defensible choice. It allows you to generalize your conclusions beyond these two raters.

**2. Single vs. average (the ",1" or ",k" suffix):**
- **Single-rater ICC (k=1)**: Reliability of one rater's ratings. This is what you want when you'll use a single clinician's rating in practice.
- **Average-rater ICC (k=2 here)**: Reliability of the *mean* of both clinicians' ratings. Use this only if the two clinicians will always rate together and you'll average their scores.

Given you have two clinicians and want to understand their individual reliability: **ICC(2,1)** with absolute agreement is most appropriate.

**3. Absolute agreement vs. consistency:**
- **Absolute agreement** penalizes systematic differences in rater level (one clinician consistently rates 1 point higher). This is usually what clinicians care about.
- **Consistency** only assesses whether raters track each other across patients (relative ordering), ignoring level differences.

Use absolute agreement unless you specifically plan to statistically adjust for rater-level differences.

## What to report

Report the ICC estimate, its 95% confidence interval, and specify which ICC type you used and why. A value ≥ .75 is commonly considered good interrater reliability; ≥ .90 is excellent — though these are rough guidelines and should be interpreted in clinical context.

## When weighted kappa is a reasonable alternative

If a reviewer or clinical context specifically demands kappa-family statistics, **quadratic weighted kappa** is the most defensible choice for an ordinal scale — it weights disagreements by the square of the distance between categories. Under certain conditions it is numerically equivalent to ICC(3,1). But ICC is more directly interpretable as a variance-partitioning statistic and is generally preferred in psychometric and clinical measurement contexts.
