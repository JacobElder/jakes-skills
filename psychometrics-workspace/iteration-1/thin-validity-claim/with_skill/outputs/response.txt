No, that is not adequate evidence for construct validity. It is one data point of one type, and the framing "construct validity was established" reflects a misunderstanding of what validity is and how it is argued.

---

**Validity is not a box you check off**

The foundational problem here is linguistic and conceptual. Messick (1989, 1995) and the 2014 *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* (AERA/APA/NCME) are unambiguous: validity is a property of **score interpretations and uses**, not of a test or scale. The correct framing is not "the scale is valid" or "construct validity was established." The correct framing is something like: "scores from this scale have convergent evidence supporting their interpretation as a measure of X in population Y for use Z."

When a paper says "construct validity was established," it is treating validity as a status to be achieved rather than an ongoing argument to be constructed. That framing was retired decades ago when the old "three Cs" (content, criterion, construct) taxonomy was replaced by the unified view.

---

**What they actually have: partial convergent evidence**

A correlation of r = 0.55 with another measure of the same construct is convergent evidence — specifically, it falls under what the 2014 *Standards* call "evidence based on relations to other variables." This is one of five sources of validity evidence, and even within this source, a single correlation is the weakest possible form of the argument.

Convergent evidence answers the question: do scores move together with other measures of the same thing? An r of 0.55 says yes, moderately. But:

1. That's the only claim it supports — and only partially.
2. It says nothing about content representativeness, response processes, internal structure, discriminant validity, or consequences of use.

---

**The common-method variance problem**

There is an additional specific problem with this particular correlation: both measures are self-report. When two self-report scales are administered in the same session, the observed correlation contains not just trait overlap but **common-method variance** — shared variance attributable to the measurement method rather than the constructs. Respondents who tend to agree with positive items on Scale A will tend to agree with positive items on Scale B; social desirability, acquiescence, response sets, mood at time of testing, and item wording similarity all inflate the correlation above what would be found between the construct and a behaviorally-indexed or rater-assessed measure.

This is exactly what the **multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) framework** (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) was designed to detect. In an MTMM matrix, you cross multiple traits × multiple methods. Convergent validity requires that the correlation between the same construct measured by *different methods* (monotrait-heteromethod) is high. Discriminant validity requires that this correlation exceed (a) correlations between different traits measured by the same method (heterotrait-monomethod) and (b) correlations between different traits measured by different methods (heterotrait-heteromethod).

A single correlation between two self-report measures of the same construct is a monotrait-monomethod correlation — the weakest cell in the MTMM design. It cannot separate trait variance from method variance. You cannot claim convergent validity from this alone.

---

**What would adequate evidence look like?**

The paper would need to build a validity argument drawing on multiple sources. At minimum for a new scale paper:

1. **Evidence based on content**: Expert review of items for domain representativeness. A content validity ratio (Lawshe, 1975) or structured expert ratings. Do the items actually cover the construct domain? This is prior to data collection and cannot be replaced by any correlation.

2. **Evidence based on response processes**: Cognitive interviews or think-aloud protocols to verify that respondents are interpreting items as intended. A scale that looks face-valid but is interpreted idiosyncratically has a response process validity problem no correlation will reveal.

3. **Evidence based on internal structure**: Factor analysis to test whether items cohere into the structure the theory predicts. If the scale is claimed to be unidimensional, does CFA support that? Reliability (preferably McDonald's omega from the factor model) establishes internal consistency as a lower bound on construct homogeneity.

4. **Evidence based on relations to other variables — properly done**: This is where the r = 0.55 lives, but it needs company:
   - **Discriminant evidence**: Does the scale NOT correlate (or correlate much less) with measures of related-but-distinct constructs? A scale claiming to measure anxiety should correlate less with depression (related) than with another anxiety measure. Without discriminant evidence, the scale might be measuring general distress or negative affect rather than the specific construct.
   - **Heteromethod convergent evidence**: Does the scale correlate with a non-self-report index of the same construct — behavioral observation, physiological measure, informant rating, or archival record? A heteromethod correlation of even r = 0.35 is stronger convergent evidence than a monomethod correlation of r = 0.70.
   - **Predictive validity**: Does the score predict future outcomes the theory says it should?

5. **Structural equation modeling MTMM**: Formally model trait and method variance using CFA-MTMM (correlated traits–correlated methods or CT-CU models). This directly partitions the variance in scores into trait and method components.

---

**The short version**

r = 0.55 with another self-report measure tells you the two scales share roughly 30% of their variance. That is consistent with measuring the same construct, but it is equally consistent with both measures sharing a common method factor (self-report bias), having overlapping but non-identical constructs, or simply sharing item wording conventions. "Construct validity was established" is an overclaim. What they have is one piece of convergent evidence, confounded by method, with no discriminant evidence, no content review, no internal structure evidence, and no response process evidence. The claim should be "preliminary convergent evidence was obtained; further validation work is needed."
