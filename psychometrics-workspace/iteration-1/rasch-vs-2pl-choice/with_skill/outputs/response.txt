The Rasch vs. 2PL decision is one of the genuinely interesting methodological forks in psychometrics because it's not primarily a statistical question — it's a measurement philosophy question. Let me break down what's actually different, then help you think through which makes sense for your test.

## What's different between Rasch and 2PL

Both are unidimensional IRT models for dichotomous items. The math is almost identical — the 2PL just adds one parameter:

**Rasch (1PL):**
P(Xᵢ = 1 | θ) = exp(θ − bᵢ) / [1 + exp(θ − bᵢ)]

**2PL:**
P(Xᵢ = 1 | θ) = exp(aᵢ(θ − bᵢ)) / [1 + exp(aᵢ(θ − bᵢ))]

The single difference: the 2PL has a discrimination parameter **aᵢ** for each item; Rasch constrains all discriminations to be equal (effectively fixed to 1 on the logit scale).

**Discrimination** is the slope of the item characteristic curve — how sharply the item differentiates between students just above and just below its difficulty level. A highly discriminating item (high a) has a steep S-curve; a weakly discriminating item (low a) has a shallow, spread-out S-curve. Rasch says: treat all items as equally discriminating. 2PL says: items differ in how well they differentiate ability, and we should model that.

## The deeper difference: a measurement philosophy commitment

Here's where the choice matters beyond just model fit: **the Rasch model has a property called specific objectivity (sometimes called sample independence)**.

Specific objectivity means:
- **Item parameters are independent of the sample of persons** used to estimate them (up to a linear transformation).
- **Person parameters (θ) are independent of the items used to estimate them.**

This is a strong, useful property. If you're building a test bank, creating alternate forms, running adaptive testing, or comparing students who saw different subsets of items, Rasch's specific objectivity makes that principled and clean. With 2PL, you still have parameter invariance in a weaker sense (parameters are estimable from any representative sample), but specific objectivity in the strict Rasch sense requires the equal-discrimination constraint.

The Rasch tradition (Andrich, Wright) treats this as a *prescriptive* standard: items that don't conform to equal discrimination are the problem items — revise them or drop them until your item pool fits the Rasch model. The philosophy is that Rasch defines what good measurement looks like, not just what your data look like.

The 2PL tradition (Birnbaum, Lord) takes the opposite stance: items naturally vary in discrimination, modeling that variation gives better fit and more accurate θ estimates, so let the data dictate. By fixing discriminations to be equal in Rasch, you're imposing a constraint the data may not support — and if it doesn't hold, your θ estimates are less accurate.

**This is why you shouldn't choose based on AIC/BIC alone.** If you've committed to Rasch for measurement-theoretic reasons (specific objectivity, future test equating, adaptive testing), then finding that the 2PL fits better by AIC is interesting information about item quality, not a reason to abandon Rasch. Fix the misfitting items instead.

## For your 30-item knowledge test, N = 800

Your sample size is solidly adequate. Rasch needs roughly 250+ for stable estimation; 2PL needs ~500. At N = 800 with 30 items, both models will give you stable, reliable parameter estimates. This is not the limiting factor for your decision.

## Which should you use?

A few questions that actually drive the decision:

**1. Do you plan to equate or link this test to future forms?**
If yes, and you want the strong form of parameter invariance that enables principled linking without reference populations, Rasch is compelling. Its specific objectivity is the theoretical basis for clean equating.

**2. Do you expect your items to vary substantially in quality?**
A 30-item knowledge test developed carefully with domain experts probably has reasonably similar discriminations. A hastily assembled item set, or one mixing short recall items with complex application items, may have genuinely heterogeneous discriminations. If you expect real variation, forcing equal discrimination (Rasch) will give you worse θ estimates for the students.

**3. Are you diagnosing item quality?**
If you're doing item analysis to revise and improve the test, the 2PL's discrimination parameters give you direct, interpretable evidence about which items are doing the most measurement work. That's practically useful.

My default for a knowledge test in this situation: **start with 2PL**, examine the discrimination estimates, and if most items cluster near similar discrimination values, consider whether Rasch would be a justifiable simplification. If several items have notably lower or higher discrimination (say, a < 0.5 or a > 2.5), those items merit inspection regardless of model choice.

## R code

```r
library(mirt)

# Fit 2PL
fit_2pl <- mirt(data, 1, itemtype = "2PL", SE = TRUE)
coef(fit_2pl, IRTpars = TRUE, simplify = TRUE)  # a (discrimination) and b (difficulty) for each item

# Fit Rasch
fit_rasch <- mirt(data, 1, itemtype = "Rasch")
coef(fit_rasch, IRTpars = TRUE, simplify = TRUE)  # only b varies; a is fixed

# Formal comparison (LRT, AIC, BIC)
# This tells you about data fit, not which is philosophically right
anova(fit_rasch, fit_2pl)

# Item fit — check which items misfit under your chosen model
itemfit(fit_2pl, fit_stats = "S_X2")   # S-chi-square; standard item-level fit stat

# Model-level fit
M2(fit_2pl)   # RMSEA, CFI, TLI analogues for IRT

# IRFs — visual check that curves look plausible
plot(fit_2pl, type = "trace")

# Item Information Functions — which items contribute most precision, and where
plot(fit_2pl, type = "infotrace")   # per-item IIF
plot(fit_2pl, type = "info")        # Test Information Function (TIF)
plot(fit_2pl, type = "infoSE")      # conditional SE across theta

# Person scores
theta <- fscores(fit_2pl, method = "EAP", full.scores.SE = TRUE)
```

## The item information and test information functions are essential

Don't stop at coefficient tables. The item information function (IIF) for a 2PL item peaks at θ = b (item difficulty) and is taller for items with higher a. The test information function (TIF) is simply the sum of all IIFs across your 30 items — it tells you **where on the ability continuum your test is most precise**.

For a knowledge test used for pass/fail decisions at a cut score, you want the TIF to peak near the cut score, not necessarily at the mean of the distribution. If your TIF peaks well above or below where you're making decisions, your test is less precise where it counts. This is the single most practical output of IRT for test development, and it's something CTT sum scores can't give you.

## What about 3PL?

For a 30-item knowledge test, I'd be cautious about 3PL. The guessing parameter (c) is substantively relevant only if students at very low ability genuinely guess randomly — typical for multiple-choice items on a test that's long enough that running out of time causes guessing, or where test-takers have zero relevant knowledge and just mark answers. It requires ~1000 respondents for stable estimation of c, and it's easy to overfit. Unless there's clear evidence of a guessing floor in your data (IRF lower asymptotes clearly above zero), stick with 2PL. The question to ask is substantive: is guessing meaningfully happening in your test context? Not: does 3PL fit better by AIC?

## Practical bottom line

For a 30-item knowledge test with N = 800:
- **If measurement equating, test banking, or CAT are in your future**: start with Rasch, use it as a quality standard for your items, revise misfitters.
- **If this is a one-time test and you want the best θ estimates**: use 2PL, examine item discriminations, inspect the TIF relative to your decision cut points.
- **Either way**: plot the test information function and item information functions — they'll tell you more about your test's strengths and weaknesses than any single reliability coefficient.
