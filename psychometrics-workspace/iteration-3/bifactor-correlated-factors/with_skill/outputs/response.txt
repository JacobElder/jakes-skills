Your advisor is right that a correlation of r = .71 between hedonic and eudaimonic wellbeing is expected — these dimensions of wellbeing are genuinely related, and most theory predicts they should be. But "factors are correlated, therefore a total score is fine" is not a complete argument. The correlation tells you the factors share variance; it doesn't tell you how much of the total-score variance is driven by a single general wellbeing dimension versus the two group factors separately. That distinction is exactly what determines whether a total score is defensible, and you need a bifactor model to answer it.

**Why r = .71 isn't sufficient justification on its own**

r = .71 means roughly 50% of the variance in one factor is shared with the other. That's high enough that a general "wellbeing" factor almost certainly underlies both subscales. But high factor correlation in a two-factor CFA is precisely the signature of a bifactor structure hiding beneath: one general factor loading on all 16 items, plus two residual group factors capturing what's unique to hedonic and eudaimonic items respectively. Your current two-factor model isn't wrong — it fits the data — but it doesn't decompose the variance in a way that answers the total-score question.

**What to actually do: bifactor model + omega**

Run a bifactor model and compare omega-hierarchical (ω_h) with omega-total (ω_t). These are the two statistics that settle the total-score question:

- **ω_t** (omega-total): the proportion of total-score variance attributable to *all* common factors — general + both group factors. This is the ceiling for how reliable your total score can be.
- **ω_h** (omega-hierarchical): the proportion of total-score variance attributable to the *general factor alone*, after partialling out the group factors. This is the key statistic. It tells you whether a total score primarily reflects "wellbeing in general" or is contaminated by the specific hedonic vs. eudaimonic variance.

**Decision rule:**

- If ω_h is high (typically > .80) and close to ω_t (gap < .10–.15), the general factor dominates, the scale is functionally unidimensional, and a total score is defensible. Your advisor's intuition would be confirmed.
- If ω_h is substantially lower than ω_t (e.g., ω_h = .55, ω_t = .90), a large chunk of total-score variance is coming from the group factors. Your total score is a blend of general wellbeing + hedonic-specific variance + eudaimonic-specific variance in unclear proportions, and interpreting it as a pure wellbeing index is not defensible. In that case, subscale scores (or a bifactor-scored general factor) are more interpretable.

**R code:**

```r
library(psych)

# omega() fits a bifactor model and reports ω_h and ω_t directly
om <- omega(wellbeing_data, nfactors = 2, fm = "ml", poly = TRUE)
# poly = TRUE because you likely have Likert items

om$omega_h    # omega-hierarchical — the key number
om$omega.tot  # omega-total

# Alternatively, fit the bifactor model in lavaan explicitly:
library(lavaan)
library(semTools)

bifactor_model <- '
  # General factor loads on all 16 items
  G =~ item1 + item2 + ... + item16
  # Group factors (orthogonal to G in bifactor parameterization)
  Hedonic =~ item1 + item2 + ... + item8
  Eudaimonic =~ item9 + item10 + ... + item16
'

fit <- cfa(bifactor_model, data = wellbeing_data,
           ordered = TRUE,           # WLSMV for ordinal Likert
           estimator = "WLSMV",
           orthogonal.g = TRUE)      # General factor orthogonal to group factors

compRelSEM(fit)  # Returns ω_h and ω_t; preferred for lavaan-fitted models
```

**Bottom line**

Don't skip this step. The fact that your factors correlate at r = .71 is evidence that a bifactor structure *exists* — it's not evidence that the total score is uncontaminated. Run `psych::omega()`, look at ω_h vs. ω_t, and then you can give your advisor a defensible answer either way. If ω_h comes back high (> .80), you can sum all 16 items with confidence. If it doesn't, you have a more interesting problem and subscale-level reporting (or reporting the bifactor general-factor score) is the honest move.
