This is an important finding that you need to take seriously. Scalar invariance is the prerequisite for comparing latent means across groups, and your results indicate it does not hold for your scale.

**What the results mean**

A ΔCFI of −.024 exceeds the commonly used threshold of −.010 (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002), and the significant Δχ² confirms that the fit difference is not attributable to chance. Together, these indicate that at least some item intercepts are not equivalent across groups — meaning people at the same true depression level in the two groups respond differently to those items on average.

**Can you compare means?**

Strictly speaking, no — not under full scalar invariance, which you have not achieved. The whole point of scalar invariance is to ensure that the scale is measuring depression on the same metric with the same item baselines in both groups. Without it, a difference in observed or latent means could reflect genuine group differences in depression OR differences in how items function across groups — and you cannot tell which.

**What you could consider**

Some researchers invoke "partial scalar invariance" (Byrne et al., 1989) as a workaround: you identify the items driving the misfit using modification indices, free those intercepts, and argue the remaining constrained items are sufficient to anchor a meaningful comparison. In your case, since two items are flagged, you could try freeing those two intercepts and testing whether the partially constrained model fits adequately.

However, this approach has limitations you should be aware of. Freeing intercepts post-hoc based on modification indices is a form of model fitting that can capitalize on chance, especially if you don't have a strong theoretical reason why those specific items would behave differently. It also raises questions about whether your scale is really measuring the same construct comparably across groups.

**My recommendation**

Given that scalar invariance failed, I would be cautious about proceeding with latent mean comparisons. If you do choose to use partial invariance, report it clearly and note that interpretations should be made with caution. You may also want to consider whether the scale is really appropriate for use across these two groups, or whether a different instrument with better cross-group measurement properties would be more suitable for your research question.

At minimum, you should consult with a measurement specialist before drawing strong conclusions from group mean comparisons in this context.
