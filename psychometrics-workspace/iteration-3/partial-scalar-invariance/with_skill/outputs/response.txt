Yes, you can still compare latent means — but only under partial scalar invariance, and only if three conditions are met. Let me walk through each.

---

**What happened and why it matters**

Configural invariance confirmed that the same 9-item factor structure holds in both groups. Metric invariance confirmed that loadings are equal — items relate to the depression construct with the same strength across groups. That's good: you're already on a common metric.

Scalar invariance requires equal intercepts, meaning that people at the same true depression level should endorse each item at the same expected rate regardless of group. Your ΔCFI of −.024 and a significant Δχ² tell you this doesn't hold globally. The modification indices are pointing at two specific items.

Full scalar invariance is what's needed for clean latent mean comparison. But "partial scalar invariance" — proposed by Byrne, Shavelson & Muthén (1989) — is a defensible fallback when only a subset of items drive the failure.

---

**Condition 1: Are you freeing too many intercepts?**

The rule of thumb is no more than ~20% of intercepts released. You have 9 items, so 20% = 1.8 items. Two items is 22% — this is right at the edge, and you should acknowledge that explicitly when you report this. One freed intercept would be clearly defensible; two is borderline but generally accepted in practice for a 9-item scale, especially if both items are on the same factor and the other factor retains more invariant anchors. Do not present this as comfortably within bounds — it is borderline and worth flagging as a limitation.

---

**Condition 2: At least 2 invariant intercepts per factor must remain constrained**

This is the identification requirement (Steenkamp & Baumgartner, 1998). For the latent mean difference to be estimable and interpretable, each factor needs at least two intercepts that remain fixed across groups — they serve as the anchor for placing the latent means on a common scale. Check that your two non-invariant items don't both land on the same factor and aren't the majority of indicators on that factor. If both non-invariant items come from a 3-item factor, for example, you now have 1 invariant intercept left on that factor, which fails this requirement and makes the partial invariance approach untenable for that factor.

---

**Condition 3: Substantive justification for the freed items — not just MI-chasing**

This is the most important condition and the one most often skipped. Freeing intercepts purely because modification indices are large is exploratory post-hoc fitting. To defend partial scalar invariance in a manuscript, you need a theoretically coherent reason why those two specific items would have different baseline endorsement rates across groups at the same true depression level.

Ask: Is there anything about these item wordings that could function differently across your two clinical groups? For example:
- Does one item reference somatic symptoms that might be more normatively endorsed (or suppressed) in one clinical context?
- Does one item have a cultural or situational referent that's more salient for one group?
- Were the groups recruited via different diagnostic criteria, and could one item be a criterion symptom for one group but not the other (which would inflate endorsement in one group regardless of trait level)?

If you can construct a coherent theoretical account — not just "MIs told us to free them" — partial invariance is defensible and should be reported as such. If you cannot identify a substantive reason, you should be more cautious about interpreting the freed model as psychometrically justified rather than just better-fitting.

---

**The procedure**

In lavaan, the partial scalar invariance model frees the intercepts for those two items in one group while constraining all other intercepts:

```r
library(lavaan)
library(semTools)

# Start from metric-invariant model, then free the two problematic intercepts
# Suppose items 4 and 7 are the non-invariant ones
model_partial <- cfa(model, data = d, group = "group_var",
                     group.equal = c("loadings", "intercepts"),
                     group.partial = c("item4~1", "item7~1"))

# Compare to metric model — should be an improvement;
# compare to full scalar — the chi-square difference should be non-significant
# (or ΔCFI should come back within -.010) if those two items account for the misfit
lavTestLRT(fit_metric, model_partial, fit_scalar)
```

The partial model should fit significantly better than full scalar (confirming those items drive the failure) but not significantly worse than metric (confirming the rest of the intercepts hold).

To estimate and compare latent means, add `meanstructure = TRUE` and free latent means in one group:

```r
model_partial_means <- cfa(model, data = d, group = "group_var",
                           group.equal = c("loadings", "intercepts"),
                           group.partial = c("item4~1", "item7~1"),
                           meanstructure = TRUE)
# Latent means are constrained to 0 in reference group by default
# and estimated in the comparison group — those are your latent mean differences
summary(model_partial_means, fit.measures = TRUE)
```

---

**What to report**

Don't present this as full scalar invariance. In your methods section:
1. Report the full invariance sequence (configural → metric → scalar) with fit statistics and ΔCFI/Δχ² at each step.
2. Report that scalar invariance failed (ΔCFI = −.024, Δχ² significant).
3. Identify which items were freed based on modification indices, and provide the substantive justification.
4. Report fit of the partial scalar model and confirm it recovers acceptable fit relative to metric.
5. State explicitly that latent mean comparisons are conducted under partial scalar invariance with 7/9 intercepts constrained, that this is at the boundary of the ~20% threshold (2/9 = 22%), and that interpretations of the mean difference should be made with appropriate caution.

The mean comparison is defensible — but the caveats are load-bearing, not boilerplate.
