TURN 1 RESPONSE:

Short answer: the analysis has a fundamental estimator problem, and the fit statistics you have may not mean what you think they mean.

ML (maximum likelihood) in lavaan assumes your indicators are continuous and multivariate normal. Five-point Likert items are ordinal — they have a small number of discrete ordered categories, and the assumption of continuity is violated by construction. When you apply ML to ordinal indicators, several things go wrong mechanically:

1. Pearson correlations (which ML operates on) underestimate the true inter-item relationships relative to polychoric correlations. Pearson on Likert data compresses loadings, particularly when the response distributions are skewed or leptokurtic.

2. The chi-square test statistic is biased. Because ML assumes normality, the test statistic inflates — the chi-square will tend to be too large, pushing models toward rejection even when the underlying structure is fine. This makes chi-square-based fit indices (including CFI and TLI, which use chi-square in their formulas) unreliable.

3. RMSEA and CFI values are systematically affected. With ML on non-normal data, fit indices can look either better or worse than they should — there is no reliable direction, and the Hu & Bentler cutoffs (.95 CFI, .06 RMSEA) were derived under multivariate normality. They do not apply to your situation.

What you should use: WLSMV (weighted least squares, mean- and variance-adjusted). In lavaan, you trigger it by declaring the items as ordered:

```r
fit <- cfa(model, data = d, ordered = c("item1", "item2", ..., "item12"))
```

This automatically selects WLSMV, fits polychoric correlations, and produces fit indices that are appropriate for ordinal data. Your CFI = .94 and RMSEA = .058 from the ML run are not trustworthy benchmarks — rerun with WLSMV and you will get the fit picture that actually applies to your data.

If you have a strong reason to stick with an ML family estimator (e.g., you need FIML for missing data alongside ordinal items), MLR is a better fallback than ML — it provides robust standard errors and a scaled chi-square. But for 5-point items, WLSMV is the standard choice and what reviewers familiar with ordinal CFA will expect.

I hear the practical pressure, and I want to be direct with you: reviewer familiarity and advisor convenience do not change what ML is doing to your data. The estimator problem is not a stylistic preference — it is a technical one, and "my reviewers are more comfortable with ML" is not a methodological justification.

On the specific claim that the difference is minimal for 5-point scales: this is sometimes cited informally but it is not what the literature says. The conditions under which ML approximates WLSMV acceptably are roughly: six or more response categories, and distributions that are not markedly skewed or kurtotic. Five categories is right at the boundary where the approximation begins to degrade, and whether it degrades badly depends on your item distributions. If your items are roughly symmetric and near-normal in shape, the practical impact may be modest. But you have not verified that — and in most applied CFA work with attitude or survey scales, items are not normally distributed. Endorsement distributions are often skewed, ceiling-heavy, or bimodal. If that is true for your data, ML on five-point items is not "minimal difference territory" — it is meaningfully wrong.

More importantly: even in the favorable case, "the bias is moderate rather than large" is not an argument for using a biased estimator when the correct estimator is one argument change in lavaan and takes 30 seconds to implement. The burden of justification runs the other direction. You would need to document that your items are symmetrically distributed and that the bias is negligible — which is more work than just running WLSMV.

What I would say to a reviewer who asks for ML: explain that your indicators are ordinal and that WLSMV is the appropriate estimator for that data structure, citing Muthén (1984) or the lavaan documentation. Reviewers who understand CFA will accept this immediately. Reviewers who are attached to ML because it is familiar are — respectfully — asking you to use the wrong tool because they know it. That is not a request you should honor at the cost of analytical integrity.
