The 10:1 rule is too crude to use as your primary planning criterion — and leaning on it could lead you to collect exactly the wrong amount of data.

Here's the core problem: "10 participants per item" treats all EFA studies as equivalent. They aren't. The stability of a factor solution depends primarily on two things that the N-per-item rule ignores entirely:

**1. Communality (h²) — this is the real driver**

Communality is the proportion of each item's variance explained by the common factors. MacCallum, Widaman, Zhang & Hong (1999) — the landmark paper on this — showed that:

- When average communalities are **high (h² > .6)**, N ≈ 100 can yield stable, replicable factor solutions.
- When average communalities are **low (h² ≈ .3)** — meaning items are weakly determined by the underlying factors — even N = 500 may not be enough to get stable rotations.

Your 20 items and N = 200 (the 10:1 result) could be completely adequate or badly underpowered, depending on how well your items actually reflect the constructs you're measuring.

**2. Factor overdetermination (≥ 4 items per factor)**

The second key variable is how many items you have per factor. Factors with 4 or more strong-loading items are "overdetermined" — they'll recover reliably even with modest N. A factor with only 2–3 items is much more fragile. If your 20 items are spread across many factors, or if some factors are underrepresented, stability requires larger N regardless of communality.

**What 10:1 actually gives you**

The 10:1 heuristic (and the related "N ≥ 200" rule) come from an era before this communality-focused work was well understood. They're not wrong in the sense that 200 is often a reasonable place to start — but they provide no diagnostic leverage. You can't use them to know whether 200 is enough *for your specific scale*, and you certainly can't use them to know whether 200 is overkill or dangerously insufficient.

**Practical guidance for your 20-item questionnaire**

1. **Anticipate your communalities before data collection.** If this scale is adapted from existing work, look at the communality estimates reported in prior EFA studies. If items have h² > .6 and you expect 4+ items per factor, N = 100–150 is often defensible. If items are novel or you expect weak-to-moderate communalities, push well above 200.

2. **Think about your factor structure.** 20 items — how many factors are you expecting? 2 factors with 10 items each is very different from 5 factors with 4 items each. More factors with fewer items per factor demands larger N.

3. **Plan stability checks.** Split-half replication (`psych::fa.bootstrap()` or a literal sample split) is more informative than N rules. A factor solution that replicates in both halves is stable; one that doesn't needs a larger sample or better items, regardless of whether you hit some ratio.

4. **Consider improving items first.** Higher-loading items (better items) mean higher communalities, which directly reduces the N you need for a stable solution. Investing in item quality pays off in sample-size requirements.

**Bottom line:** Your advisor's 200 target isn't necessarily wrong, but the justification is. The 10:1 rule can't tell you whether 200 is adequate for *your* study. What matters is communality magnitude and factor overdetermination — and you should be assessing both, ideally using pilot data or published estimates from related scales, rather than relying on a headcount heuristic.
