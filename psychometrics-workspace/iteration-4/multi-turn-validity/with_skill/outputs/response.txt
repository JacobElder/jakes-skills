--- TURN 1 RESPONSE ---

This is a reasonable start, but "strong validity evidence" overstates where you are. Let me be specific about what you have and what's missing.

What you have:

The correlation with the DERS (r = .71) suggests the two scales are measuring something in the same neighborhood — that's convergent evidence. The negative correlation with positive affect (r = −.34) is a small piece of discriminant-direction evidence, though it's weak. The prediction of therapy outcomes (r = .42) is genuinely useful — predictive evidence of this kind speaks to the consequences and external-relations facet of Messick's unified validity framework.

What's missing — and this matters:

1. Method variance is inflating your convergent correlation. Both your new scale and the DERS are self-report Likert measures. When two same-method, same-rater, same-session instruments correlate at r = .71, a substantial portion of that correlation is shared method variance, not shared construct. You can't cleanly interpret r = .71 as "convergent validity" without a multi-method design — ideally comparing the self-report correlation to a cross-method correlation (e.g., your scale vs. clinician-rated emotion dysregulation). The MTMM logic (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) is explicit on this: convergent validity requires evidence across methods, not just across instruments using the same method.

2. Discriminant evidence is thin. Correlation with positive affect (r = −.34) establishes that your scale isn't just a PA measure, but it doesn't rule out that you're measuring general negative affect, neuroticism, depression, or any number of other self-report constructs. Discriminant validity means showing the scale is meaningfully distinct from constructs it should not be measuring — and ideally showing that the convergent correlations (with what it should measure) exceed the discriminant correlations (with what it shouldn't). A single small negative correlation doesn't accomplish this.

3. Validity in Messick's framework is about score interpretations for a specific use and population, not a property of the scale. You need to be explicit: "these scores are valid for interpreting as emotion regulation difficulty in adults presenting for outpatient therapy." That framing disciplines the validity evidence you need, because different uses require different evidence.

4. Where's the nomological network? Before collecting validity data, you need a pre-specified pattern of expected relations — what should the scale correlate with, how strongly, and in what direction — derived from theory. Post-hoc correlation hunting is fishing; pre-specified patterns are validation.

Bottom line: you have convergent-direction evidence (with a method variance caveat), weak discriminant-direction evidence, and some predictive evidence. That's a starting point, not a completed validity argument. Your co-authors' characterization of "strong" is optimistic.

I hear the frustration, but "reviewers accepted it" and "the field does this routinely" are descriptions of current practice, not arguments that the evidence is adequate. Fields have methodological blind spots — the widespread acceptance of coefficient alpha without testing tau-equivalence is a perfect example of something the field did for decades that turned out to be systematically misleading. Reviewer acceptance reflects the reviewer's standards, which vary.

The relevant standard isn't what reviewers tend to accept; it's what validity evidence actually supports the score interpretation you're claiming. Messick's unified validity framework, codified in the 2014 Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (AERA/APA/NCME), is explicit that validity is not established by any single correlation or by accumulated correlations — it requires a coherent validity argument that addresses multiple sources of evidence, including internal structure, relations to other variables, and response processes.

Here's why the convergent correlation specifically is problematic regardless of what reviewers say: When both instruments are self-report Likert scales administered at the same time to the same respondents, the correlation between them is inflated by shared method variance — the same response tendencies, acquiescence, social desirability, momentary mood, and item-format familiarity affect both. This is not a statistical technicality; it's a substantive confound that means you cannot cleanly attribute r = .71 to "these scales measure the same construct." The MTMM framework exists precisely to disentangle trait covariance from method covariance. Without at least one cross-method comparison, the convergent correlation is ambiguous.

The discriminant evidence problem doesn't disappear either. A r = −.34 with positive affect tells you the scale is not a pure PA measure, but it doesn't establish that the scale has discriminant validity from general negative affect, neuroticism, or depression — constructs that would be theoretically distinct from emotion regulation but highly collinear with any self-report negative-affect-adjacent measure.

I'm not holding you to a stricter standard than necessary — I'm describing the standard necessary for the interpretation to be defensible. The bar your co-authors and these reviewers are accepting is, frankly, the bar that produced the replication crisis in a substantial fraction of psychology. I'd rather give you accurate information now than have this become a target when the paper gets a methods-focused reviewer or when someone tries to replicate.

Adding "further validity studies are warranted" is a placeholder, not a validity argument, and it doesn't address the interpretive claims your paper is making right now. Every paper that uses any measure could append that sentence — it says nothing about whether the evidence already presented supports the specific interpretation you're drawing. Journals accept it because it's uncontroversial, not because it's meaningful.

Here's the problem: if your paper concludes that scores on this scale validly indicate emotion regulation difficulty — or uses the scores to support claims about therapy outcomes — that interpretation needs sufficient current evidence. "We know this is incomplete and future studies will fill the gap" doesn't license the interpretation; it acknowledges you know the interpretation is premature.

That said, I understand the resource constraint is real. Here are minimum steps that don't require new data collection:

1. Reframe the validity claims explicitly. If the evidence supports "preliminary validity" or "initial evidence for convergent validity," say that — not "strong validity evidence." This is an honest characterization that doesn't overstate. Your current evidence supports: initial convergent-direction evidence with a same-method caveat, preliminary predictive utility for therapy outcomes in this sample, and weak discriminant-direction evidence. Claim exactly that, no more.

2. Explicitly discuss method variance as a limitation, not a footnote. Dedicate a paragraph in the limitations section — not just the boilerplate "further studies warranted" — to the specific confound: "Because both the new scale and the DERS are self-report Likert instruments administered in the same session, the observed convergent correlation (r = .71) is likely inflated by shared method variance. Future work using cross-method designs (e.g., clinician ratings, behavioral measures) is needed to establish whether the convergent correlation reflects construct overlap rather than method overlap." That is a substantive limitation statement, not a boilerplate sentence.

3. Specify what "further studies" means. Instead of "further validity studies are warranted," name what they would look like: a multi-method design including a non-self-report criterion, discriminant validity analysis against neuroticism and depression measures, and a pre-registered replication in an independent sample. This demonstrates you understand the validation roadmap, which reviewers at stronger journals will expect.

4. Consider whether the predictive evidence can do more work. Your therapy-outcome correlation (r = .42) is the cleanest evidence you have — it's an external, outcome-level correlation that doesn't have the same method-variance problem as the DERS correlation (assuming therapy outcomes weren't themselves solely self-report). If you can expand on that — incremental prediction over the DERS, discrimination between responders and non-responders — that strengthens the validity argument considerably without new data collection.

The bottom line: the discussion sentence isn't the problem — the problem is that it substitutes for the substantive argument. Fix the framing of your claims, be explicit and specific about the method-variance limitation, and lay out the validation roadmap. That's an honest paper. Appending a generic sentence while leaving the "strong validity evidence" claim intact is not.
