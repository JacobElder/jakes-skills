## Interpreting kappa = .62

The most widely cited benchmarks for interpreting kappa come from Landis and Koch (1977):

- < 0: less than chance agreement
- .01–.20: slight
- .21–.40: fair
- .41–.60: moderate
- .61–.80: substantial
- .81–1.00: almost perfect

At .62, you're at the lower end of the "substantial" range, which supports your supervisor's conclusion. For clinical rating scales, this level of interrater reliability is commonly reported in the literature and generally considered workable, though higher is always better.

## Weighted vs. unweighted kappa

One thing worth noting: you mentioned you computed weighted kappa, which is the right choice here. Weighted kappa is better suited to ordinal rating scales (like your 1-7 hostility scale) than unweighted kappa, because it accounts for the degree of disagreement — a 1-vs-7 disagreement is penalized more heavily than a 1-vs-2 disagreement. So you've already taken an appropriate step by using the weighted version.

## What .62 means practically

A kappa of .62 suggests your raters agree more often than would be expected by chance, but there is still meaningful disagreement. In clinical research, this typically means:

- The rating system is capturing something real and the raters share a common understanding of the construct
- There is room for improvement through additional rater training, clearer anchor definitions for each point on the scale, or practice rating sessions with calibration
