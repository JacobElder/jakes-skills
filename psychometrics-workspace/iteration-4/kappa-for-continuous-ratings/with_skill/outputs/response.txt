Your supervisor is wrong, and the error here is in the statistic chosen, not the value obtained. Cohen's kappa — even weighted kappa — is not appropriate for a 1-7 hostility scale. You should be computing an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), not kappa.

## Why kappa is the wrong statistic

Kappa was designed for nominal (categorical) data — categories that have no inherent order, like diagnostic categories ("anxiety disorder," "mood disorder," "no diagnosis"). When you apply kappa to an ordered scale, you throw away the ordinal information entirely.

Here's the specific problem: kappa treats a disagreement of 1 vs. 7 identically to a disagreement of 3 vs. 4. Both are "disagreements," so both penalize the kappa coefficient equally. That's absurd for a scale where the whole point is that the numbers represent graded levels of severity. A rater who scores 3 and a rater who scores 4 are showing near-perfect agreement in any meaningful clinical sense; kappa doesn't see it that way.

Weighted kappa partially addresses this by applying penalty weights to disagreements based on distance — but the choice of weights is arbitrary (linear vs. quadratic), and more fundamentally, the entire framing is still categorical. You're still asking "did they agree on the category?", just with graduated penalties.

There's also the kappa paradox to know about: kappa is mathematically sensitive to the marginal distributions of the ratings. When raters both tend to use one end of the scale (say, both tend to rate most patients as low-hostility, which is realistic in a clinical sample), kappa can be paradoxically low — even when rater agreement is actually very high — because of the base-rate correction built into kappa's formula. You can have ICC > .90 and kappa < .40 in the same dataset. If your ratings are skewed toward the low end of the hostility scale, kappa = .62 could be substantially understating the real agreement.

## What you should use: ICC

For continuous or ordinal ratings like a 1-7 scale, the correct statistic is the intraclass correlation coefficient. ICC is appropriate here because it treats the scores as ordered quantities on a continuum, accounts for both the level and the rank of the scores, and has interpretive benchmarks specifically developed for clinical research.

The choice of ICC model is not automatic — you need to decide deliberately:

**ICC(2,1) — Two-way random effects, single rater, absolute agreement**
Use this when your two raters are a random sample from a larger pool of raters you'd want to generalize to (e.g., any two trained clinical raters from your clinic could have been selected). "Absolute agreement" means you care whether the two raters give the same number, not just whether they rank patients similarly.

**ICC(3,1) — Two-way mixed effects, single rater, consistency**
Use this when your two specific raters are fixed (these are the raters, period — you're not generalizing to other raters) and you care only about rank-order consistency, not whether they're using the same absolute score level. This is the more lenient model; it ignores systematic additive differences between raters.

For most clinical interrater work, ICC(2,1) absolute agreement is the right choice, because you want the raters to agree on the actual severity score, not just whether one patient is more hostile than another. If rater A consistently scores everyone 1 point higher than rater B, ICC(3,1) would call that "perfect consistency," which is not clinically useful.

## Benchmarks for ICC

The commonly cited thresholds (Koo & Mae, 2016, *Journal of Chiropractic Medicine*) for clinical ratings:
- < .50: poor
- .50–.75: moderate
- .75–.90: good
- > .90: excellent
