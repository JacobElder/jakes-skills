# Prioritizing Investments: The Funding Waterfall

The canonical Boglehead order for funding accounts, while working and accumulating assets. Source: bogleheads.org/wiki/Prioritizing_investments. This is *the* answer to "where should I put my next dollar?"

The order isn't dogma — it's a starting point. People legitimately deviate based on tax situation, available accounts, and personal goals. Walk through it and adjust.

## The order (US-centric)

### 0. Foundations (before investing at all)

- **Minimum payments on all debts.** Don't default; that wrecks credit and snowballs.
- **Starter emergency fund of ~$1,000 to 1 month of expenses** in a high-yield savings account (Ally, Marcus, Wealthfront Cash, T-bills, money market funds — currently yielding ~4–5%). Avoid stocks for this purpose.
- **Adequate insurance:** health, term life if dependents, disability if working, auto, renter's/homeowner's, umbrella if you have meaningful assets.

### 1. Capture the full 401(k) (or 403(b) / 457) employer match

This is the highest-priority *investment*. An employer match is an immediate 50–100% return — there is literally no other investment option that competes. If you contribute 6% and your employer matches 50% of that (so 3%), you just got a 50% return before the money is even invested.

**Even with credit card debt at 25% APR, take the match first** — match is typically a >50% return; debt is "only" 25%.

### 2. Pay off high-interest debt

Anything above ~6–7% APR. Credit cards (15–25% typical), personal loans, some private student loans. Paying off debt is a guaranteed, risk-free, tax-free return at the loan's interest rate. A 20% credit card payoff is a 20% return; nothing in the stock market reliably beats that.

**Soft cutoff:** debate the threshold between 5–7%. Below ~4%, most Bogleheads invest instead. Mortgages and federal student loans usually fall below the cutoff and stay until much later.

### 3. Max the HSA (if eligible)

Health Savings Account requirements: enrolled in a qualifying high-deductible health plan (HDHP). 2024 limits: $4,150 individual / $8,300 family + $1,000 catch-up at 55+.

The HSA is the most tax-advantaged account that exists. **Triple-tax-advantaged:**
- Pre-tax contribution (or above-the-line deduction if outside payroll)
- Tax-free growth
- Tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses (which everyone has eventually)

**The Boglehead HSA hack:** pay current medical expenses out of pocket (if you can afford to), let the HSA invest and grow for decades, and reimburse yourself for those old expenses tax-free whenever you want (no time limit — keep the receipts). Or save them for retirement healthcare costs.

After age 65, HSA withdrawals for non-medical purposes are taxed as ordinary income (like a Traditional IRA). So worst case it acts like a Traditional IRA; best case it's tax-free forever.

**Place the HSA above IRA in priority** because it has the match-equivalent property of being literally tax-free coming and going.

### 4. Max IRA contributions

2024 limit: $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+). 2025: $7,000 ($8,000).

**Roth vs Traditional IRA:**
- **Roth** — contribute after-tax, withdraw tax-free. Best when your current marginal tax rate is lower than expected retirement rate (e.g., young, low income, or in a low-tax year).
- **Traditional** — deduct contribution now, taxed on withdrawal. Best when current rate is higher than expected retirement rate.
- **Uncertain?** Split contributions, or default to Roth when young.

**Income limits for direct Roth contributions** (2024 phaseout): single $146k–$161k MAGI; MFJ $230k–$240k. Above that → **Backdoor Roth** (contribute non-deductible to Traditional IRA, immediately convert to Roth). Requires no pre-tax IRA balances or you'll pay the pro-rata tax — be careful.

**Traditional IRA deductibility** phases out at much lower incomes if you have a workplace plan (2024: $77k–$87k single / $123k–$143k MFJ). Above that, you're stuck with non-deductible Traditional (which is rarely worth it — backdoor it).

### 5. Max the 401(k) up to the annual limit

2024 limit: $23,000 employee contribution ($30,500 with catch-up at 50+). 2025: $23,500. Plus employer match (already captured in step 1).

**Roth 401(k) vs Traditional 401(k):** same logic as IRA, with one wrinkle — Roth 401(k) has the same contribution limit as Traditional, so a Roth 401(k) effectively shelters more dollars (you've paid the tax already). Generally good for younger/lower-income savers; Traditional better for higher earners now.

### 6. Mega Backdoor Roth (if your plan supports it)

Some 401(k) plans allow **after-tax** contributions beyond the $23,000 employee limit, up to the total annual addition limit ($69,000 in 2024 including employer match). If the plan also allows **in-plan Roth conversions** or **in-service distributions to a Roth IRA**, these after-tax contributions can be converted to Roth, sheltering huge amounts.

Check the plan's Summary Plan Description (SPD) for both features. Not all plans offer this; many tech companies do.

### 7. Complete the full emergency fund

3–6 months of essential expenses (more for single-earner households, variable income, or specialized careers with longer job search times). High-yield savings, money market, or short-term Treasuries. Not stocks.

Some Bogleheads place this earlier (right after step 1) if they don't have any cash cushion at all. Move it up if the starter $1,000–$2,000 from step 0 feels insufficient.

### 8. 529 plans (if saving for kids' education)

Tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals for qualified education expenses (K–12 up to $10k/year, college, some apprenticeships). Many states give a state-tax deduction for contributions to their plan.

**Tip:** the SECURE 2.0 Act now allows unused 529 funds to be rolled into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary (subject to limits and a 15-year-old account requirement). Reduces the "what if my kid doesn't go to college" risk.

### 9. Taxable brokerage account

After all tax-advantaged options are maxed (or unavailable), invest in a regular brokerage account. Use tax-efficient broad-market index funds — VTI/VXUS or equivalents. Avoid:
- High-distribution funds (REITs, high-yield bonds)
- High-turnover active funds (capital gains drag)
- Target-date funds (forced distributions)

Use ETFs over mutual funds where possible (ETFs rarely distribute capital gains).

### 10. Pay down low-interest debt

Mortgage, student loans below ~4–5%. Optional and last. Many Bogleheads keep low-rate mortgages forever because tax-advantaged investing has higher expected return, but the psychological pull of being debt-free is real and legitimate.

## Common edge cases

### Employee Stock Purchase Plan (ESPP)

If your employer offers an ESPP with a 5–15% discount and no holding period, it's effectively free money — buy and immediately sell. Slot it after the 401(k) match but before high-interest debt if it's truly free money (no concentration risk because you sell immediately).

If the ESPP requires a holding period (e.g., 12 months) before you can sell, treat it more cautiously — concentration in employer stock is a known risk.

### Non-governmental 457(b)

Available to some non-profit and government employees. Limit similar to 401(k), and **stacks** with 401(k)/403(b) limits (you can max both). For governmental 457(b), there's no early withdrawal penalty — useful for early retirees. For *non*-governmental 457(b), money technically belongs to the employer until paid out, which is a meaningful credit risk — use only after maxing other accounts, and only if the employer is financially strong.

### Pension contributions (mandatory)

If you have a defined-benefit pension with mandatory contributions, that's effectively forced retirement savings — count it toward your retirement picture, but it doesn't fit the funding waterfall.

### Self-employed / sole proprietor

Replace the 401(k) with a **Solo 401(k)** (highest limits, allows employee + employer contributions) or **SEP-IRA** (simpler, employer contributions only, lower effective limit for self-employed). Solo 401(k) is preferred for high earners.

### Highly compensated employee (HCE) with 401(k) contribution limits

Some employers have plans that fail nondiscrimination testing, capping highly-paid employees below the IRS limit. If this hits you, lean harder on taxable and any backdoor Roth options.

## What this list does NOT prescribe

- **Asset allocation within accounts.** Separate question.
- **Fund selection within accounts.** Separate question.
- **Roth vs Traditional** at each step. You have to think about that yourself.
- **Tax-efficient fund placement** across accounts. See `tax_placement.md`.
