REJECTING THE "STRONG TRACK RECORD" ARGUMENT

This is the most seductive argument in active fund marketing, and it's also the most dangerous one to fall for. Here's why it doesn't hold up.

1. Past performance does not predict future outperformance.

   The S&P SPIVA U.S. Scorecard — the most rigorous, independent benchmark comparison run — consistently shows that over 15-year periods, more than 80% of active US large-cap mutual funds underperform the S&P 500 index. That's not 51% — it's over 80%. This holds across virtually every category and every measured time period.

   AGTHX had a strong track record in the past. So did hundreds of other actively managed funds that have since underperformed, closed, or been merged away. The survivorship of a track record is itself a selection effect.

2. The funds that do outperform rarely sustain it.

   SPIVA's "persistence" scorecard tracks whether funds that outperform in one period continue to outperform in the next. They don't, at any statistically meaningful rate. An active manager with a 10-year strong record is not more likely to outperform over the next 10 years — if anything, their size and institutional constraints work against them.

3. AGTHX is a large-cap blend fund competing directly against the S&P 500.

   American Funds Growth Fund of America holds many of the same stocks as the Vanguard 500 Index. Any outperformance it produces has to overcome: (a) the 0.63% expense ratio drag, (b) the management fees embedded in that ratio, (c) transaction costs from portfolio turnover, and (d) the drag of holding cash for redemptions. That's a steep uphill climb every single year.

4. You cannot know in advance which funds will outperform.

   If you could identify future outperformers reliably, you'd already be rich. The 20% of active funds that beat the index over 15 years were not identifiable beforehand. "Strong track record" is the most common way past performance gets dressed up as a predictor — but it isn't one.

Bogle's framing: "Owning the stock market over the long term is a winner's game; attempting to beat the market is a loser's game."
