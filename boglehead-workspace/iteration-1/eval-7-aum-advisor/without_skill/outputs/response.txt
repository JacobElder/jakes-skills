QUESTION: I've been with a financial advisor for 6 years. They charge 1% of assets under management. I have $350,000 with them. They've done a solid job — my portfolio is in low-cost Vanguard index funds. Is this a good deal?

RESPONSE:

Short answer: No, it's probably not a good deal — and understanding why requires looking at what you're actually paying for versus what you're getting.

---

WHAT YOU'RE ACTUALLY PAYING

At $350,000 with a 1% AUM fee, you're paying $3,500 per year right now. But that number will grow as your portfolio grows — which is the key issue with AUM fees.

If your portfolio grows to $700,000, you'll be paying $7,000/year.
If it reaches $1,000,000, you'll be paying $10,000/year.
Over a 20–30 year retirement horizon, the cumulative cost is enormous.

The compounding effect cuts both ways: your investments compound upward, but so do the fees dragging on that growth. A commonly cited estimate is that a 1% annual fee can reduce your ending portfolio value by 20–25% over 30 years, compared to a fee-free equivalent.

---

THE CORE PROBLEM: WHAT ARE YOU PAYING FOR?

This is the critical question. You said your portfolio is in low-cost Vanguard index funds. That's actually a red flag for value — not because index funds are bad (they're excellent), but because:

1. Index funds are designed to be self-managing. They don't require active stock-picking, market timing, or ongoing research. A three-fund portfolio of Vanguard index funds is something you could set up yourself in an afternoon.

2. The advisor's core job — selecting investments — is already outsourced to Vanguard. The advisor is essentially getting paid 1% to hold a portfolio that Vanguard manages for 0.03–0.15% in fund expense ratios.

3. Rebalancing is straightforward. With index funds, annual rebalancing takes less than an hour and can be done by nearly anyone following a simple rule (e.g., return to target allocation).

If your advisor is primarily providing portfolio management via index funds, you are significantly overpaying.

---

WHAT A 1% ADVISOR MIGHT LEGITIMATELY PROVIDE

There are situations where a financial advisor earns their fee. Ask yourself honestly whether your advisor does any of the following:

- Comprehensive financial planning: retirement projections, Social Security optimization, withdrawal sequencing in retirement, estate planning coordination
- Tax optimization: tax-loss harvesting, Roth conversion strategies, asset location (placing bonds in tax-advantaged accounts, stocks in taxable)
- Behavioral coaching: keeping you invested during market crashes when you'd otherwise panic-sell (this has real, documented value)
- Major life event guidance: divorce, inheritance, business sale, disability, death of a spouse
- Insurance and estate review: evaluating life insurance, long-term care, beneficiary designations

If your advisor provides robust, ongoing service in several of these areas, the 1% may be more defensible — though still worth shopping.

If your interactions are mostly quarterly statements and an annual phone call, you're overpaying substantially.

---

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO INSTEAD (LIKELY)

Option 1: Do it yourself
A simple three-fund portfolio (US total market, international, bonds) with Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab costs almost nothing and can be managed in a few hours per year. If you're comfortable with this, you could save $3,500+ annually — money that stays invested and compounds.

Option 2: Fee-only financial planner (one-time or hourly)
Services like the Garrett Planning Network or NAPFA-registered advisors offer one-time or hourly financial planning. You pay $2,000–$5,000 for a comprehensive financial plan, then implement it yourself. Repeat every few years or at major life events. This gives you professional guidance without the ongoing 1% drag.

Option 3: Lower-cost ongoing advisor
Some advisors charge flat retainer fees ($2,000–$6,000/year regardless of assets) or lower AUM percentages (0.25–0.5%). As your portfolio grows, these structures become far more cost-effective than 1% AUM.

Option 4: Robo-advisors
Betterment, Wealthfront, and Vanguard Digital Advisor offer automated portfolio management at 0.15–0.35% annually — a fraction of 1%. These are appropriate if you want some automation but don't need comprehensive planning.

---

THE LOYALTY FACTOR

Six years is a long time, and there's a natural reluctance to change. But sunk costs shouldn't drive future decisions. The question is: going forward, is this the best use of $3,500+ per year?

Also worth noting: the fact that you're asking this question suggests you may already sense the answer. Trust that instinct.

---

BOTTOM LINE

Your advisor put you in good investments — that's genuinely positive. But good investments don't justify a 1% ongoing fee when you're in passive index funds. You're essentially paying for execution of a strategy you could run yourself, or hire someone for a one-time flat fee to set up.

Before making a change, have an honest conversation with your advisor: ask them to clearly articulate what ongoing value they're providing beyond investment selection. If the answer is compelling, stay. If it's vague, it may be time to move on.

For most people with a straightforward financial situation and a comfortable relationship with basic financial concepts, the DIY or fee-only planner route will leave them significantly wealthier over time.
