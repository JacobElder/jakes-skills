Good setup — three funds, three accounts, each with a different tax character. Here is the canonical Boglehead placement:

---

BND (bonds) → Traditional 401(k)
VXUS (international stocks) → Taxable brokerage
VTI (US stocks) → Roth IRA

---

The reasoning for each:

**BND in the Traditional 401(k)**

Bond interest is taxed as ordinary income — at your full marginal rate, which could be 22%, 24%, 32%, or higher. If you hold BND in a taxable account, every interest payment you receive gets taxed that year at that rate, with no deferral.

By contrast, stock returns — qualified dividends and long-term capital gains — are taxed at preferential rates (0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your bracket), and most of the return from a stock index fund comes as unrealized appreciation that isn't taxed at all until you sell (or never, if passed to heirs with a step-up in basis).

The math makes this clear: a bond fund yielding 4% held in taxable at a 32% federal + 6% state combined rate loses roughly 1.52% of fund value annually to taxes. A stock fund with a 2% qualified dividend yield loses only about 0.30%, and the bulk of its return is sheltered until you choose to realize it.

The Traditional 401(k) defers all taxes until withdrawal, so it is the ideal shelter for the asset class most punished by current taxation. Put the inefficient asset in the tax-deferred account.

**VXUS in the taxable brokerage**

International stock funds pay foreign withholding taxes on dividends — taxes levied by the countries where the underlying companies operate. If VXUS is held in a taxable account, the IRS lets you claim a foreign tax credit (FTC) to recover most of that withholding, dollar-for-dollar against your US tax bill.

If VXUS is held inside a Traditional 401(k) or Roth IRA, that foreign tax credit is permanently lost. The IRA or 401(k) wrapper cannot pass the FTC through to you. The foreign government took the money, and you get nothing back.

The estimated benefit is roughly 0.20% per year of the international fund's value — not enormous, but real and permanently compounding. There is genuine debate in the Boglehead community about whether this benefit is large enough to matter relative to other considerations (if your 401(k) has a cheaper international option, for instance, it may be worth putting VXUS there and foregoing the FTC). But with three equally good options across three accounts, taking the FTC in taxable is the right default.

International stocks are also reasonably tax-efficient in taxable accounts: their dividends are largely qualified, and the returns are driven by long-term price appreciation just like domestic stocks.

**VTI in the Roth IRA**

The Roth IRA is your most valuable tax shelter. Contributions go in after-tax, and everything that grows inside — dividends, capital gains, decades of compounding — comes out tax-free. No RMDs. No tax bill at withdrawal.

The fundamental rule of Roth placement: put your highest-expected-return asset here, because you want maximum growth in the account where growth is never taxed. Stocks have higher expected returns than bonds over the long run, so stocks belong in Roth.

Between VTI and VXUS, either works in Roth. VTI goes here because VXUS is doing specific work in taxable (the FTC). If your Roth were larger and could hold both, that would be fine too. The point is not to put bonds in your Roth — that is the canonical mistake: wasting Roth space on a low-growth asset whose returns would have been sheltered anyway in the 401(k).

---

**One important reminder before you act on this:**

Asset location is a second-order optimization. Get your asset *allocation* right first — the right stock/bond ratio, the right amount of international — and then use placement to extract a bit more efficiency. Don't let the tax-placement tail wag the asset-allocation dog. If the amounts in each account don't match up neatly to hold exactly your target allocation, the right response is to think of the three accounts as one unified portfolio and place assets where they fit best, not to change your allocation to fit a placement scheme.

Also: this is general information, not personalized tax or financial advice. If you are in an unusual bracket, have a pension, are near retirement, or have a very large taxable account relative to your tax-advantaged accounts, it is worth a conversation with a fee-only fiduciary advisor (or posting on bogleheads.org, which is free and has genuinely expert volunteers).
