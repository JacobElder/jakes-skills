QUESTION: I just inherited $175,000. I've been investing for a few years and have a simple three-fund portfolio. I'm terrified of putting it all in at once — what if the market crashes right after? My plan is to spread this out over 3 years, investing about $5,000 per month. Is that a good approach?

RESPONSE:

Your concern is completely understandable — fear of putting a large sum in at exactly the wrong moment is one of the most emotionally powerful forces in investing. But let's be direct about what the data says and what's actually going on here.

---

THE CORE FINDING: LUMP SUM USUALLY WINS

Vanguard conducted a well-known study on this exact question using historical US, UK, and Australian market data. Their finding: lump sum investing (LSI) outperforms dollar-cost averaging (DCA) about two-thirds of the time — by an average of roughly 2-3% over a 12-month horizon.

The reason is simple. Markets spend more time going up than going down. Every day cash sits on the sidelines waiting to be invested, it's earning a lower expected return than it would in equities. With 36 months of DCA, you'd have money sitting uninvested for an average of 18 months — that's a long time to forgo equity market exposure in a portfolio designed for long-term growth.

---

YOUR SPECIFIC PLAN HAS MEANINGFUL DRAWBACKS

Spreading $175,000 over 36 months at $5,000/month is unusually extended. Here's what that actually means:

1. Prolonged cash drag: After month 1, you've only deployed $5K out of $175K — less than 3% of your windfall is working for you. After 12 months, you've deployed only $60K. For 24 months, the majority of your inheritance sits in cash (or a money market fund).

2. You're not actually reducing your risk much: Market downturns can happen at any time — including in month 31 or 35. DCA doesn't eliminate the risk of buying before a crash; it just spreads it out without fundamentally changing the odds you'll encounter one.

3. The emotional trade-off has a real cost: DCA does reduce regret if the market falls immediately after you invest. But it increases regret if the market rises — which is the more likely scenario. The protection you're buying is real, but it's expensive.

---

WHAT ACTUALLY MAKES SENSE

If you genuinely can't stomach lump sum, a middle path is more reasonable than 36 months:

Option A — True Lump Sum (Recommended by Data)
Invest all $175,000 immediately according to your target asset allocation. History says this is the highest-expected-value choice. Accept that there will be volatility and that your balance may drop — but your time horizon is long enough to recover.

Option B — Compressed DCA (6-12 Months)
If the fear is real and would cause you to panic-sell during a drawdown, a 6-12 month schedule is a reasonable behavioral compromise. Something like $14,500/month for 12 months captures most of the benefit of being invested while giving you psychological ramp-up time. The cost is modest relative to 36 months.

Option C — Tiered Deployment
Invest 50% immediately ($87,500), then deploy the remaining 50% over 6-12 months. This is a clean split that gets a significant portion working right away.

What is not recommended is 36 months. The drag is substantial, and the emotional protection it offers is more likely to cause FOMO regret (markets rise) than to save you from a crash (markets fall).

---

THE BEHAVIORAL REALITY

The most important factor isn't which approach maximizes expected return — it's which approach you'll actually stick with. If a sudden 30% market drop in month 2 would cause you to stop investing or sell what you've put in, then lump sum isn't the right choice for you right now, regardless of what the data says.

Ask yourself honestly: "If I invest $175,000 today and it becomes $122,500 in 6 months, will I stay the course?" If the answer is yes, invest the lump sum. If the answer is genuinely no, use a compressed DCA schedule — but make it 6-12 months, not 36.

---

PRACTICAL HOUSEKEEPING

A few things to address regardless of which approach you choose:

- Keep your emergency fund fully funded before deploying anything into the market.
- Check whether any of this can go into tax-advantaged accounts (maxing a Roth IRA or a backdoor Roth if eligible, or increasing 401(k) contributions to free up salary for windfall deployment).
- If the inheritance came from a taxable account with embedded gains, make sure you understand the step-up in basis rules — you likely owe nothing on gains that accrued before you inherited.
- Whichever schedule you choose, write it down and automate it. The plan you stick to is more important than the theoretically optimal one.

---

BOTTOM LINE

Your instinct toward caution is emotionally reasonable, but 36 months of DCA is excessive. The data favors lump sum investing. If you need behavioral protection, compress your timeline to 6-12 months. Do not leave the majority of a $175,000 windfall in cash for two-plus years while markets are likely trending upward. Your three-fund portfolio is already a great structure — the main job now is getting this money into it efficiently.
