The Boglehead view here is clear, and it's worth separating two questions: **past** (was holding for 2 years the right call?) and **future** (what should you do now and with future purchase periods?).

---

## The tax math is real but smaller than it sounds

With a lookback ESPP, the gain at purchase can be substantial. Example: if the stock rose 30% during the 6-month offering period and you buy at 85% of the opening price, your gain at purchase is ~53% of the purchase price, not just 15%.

**Qualifying disposition benefit:** The ordinary income inclusion is capped at the *lesser* of 15% of the offering-date price or your actual gain. Everything above that is LTCG. So if the stock rose a lot, you convert a chunk of what would otherwise be ordinary income into LTCG.

**The realistic tax delta:** In a high bracket (37% ordinary vs. 20% LTCG), the qualifying disposition treatment might save you 10–17% on a portion of the gain — roughly 3–5% of the total stock value in typical scenarios. Real money. But not "hold a single stock for two years" money.

---

## The problem with the hold-for-qualifying-disposition strategy

After the ESPP purchase closes, you own shares in your employer. That's it — that's the decision you're making every day you don't sell. The framing "I'm holding for qualifying disposition" is actually: **"I am choosing to hold a concentrated single-stock position in my employer in exchange for a modest tax benefit."**

Three things compound the risk:

1. **Employment concentration.** Your salary, bonus, and career trajectory are already tied to this company. A bad quarter, a sector downturn, or a layoff hits both your income and your stock simultaneously — the worst possible correlation.

2. **Single-stock volatility.** Individual stocks routinely drop 40–70% on earnings misses, competitive disruptions, or macro events. The two-year qualifying window exposes you to that full drawdown risk on top of your already-paid ordinary income tax at purchase.

3. **"I believe in the company" is priced in.** Every investor in the market has also evaluated your company and arrived at today's price. Your belief isn't an edge — the gain from the ESPP came from the guaranteed discount, not from the stock itself.

---

## What you should do now

If you've met the qualifying disposition requirements (2 years from offering date, 1 year from purchase date), **sell now** — or sell strategically if you have other capital gains/losses to offset. You've already paid the concentration-risk price of two years; at least capture the qualifying treatment now that you've earned it.

Going forward on future ESPP purchase periods: **sell shortly after each purchase closes.** You capture the 15% discount (plus any lookback gain) as ordinary income — the guaranteed return that justified the program in the first place — and immediately redeploy into a total-market index fund. The qualifying disposition benefit doesn't justify the next six months of single-stock risk, let alone two years.

---

## The numbers in plain terms

Say you're earning $150k and contributing 10% ($15k/year), with $7,500 deployed per 6-month period. At a 15% discount, the guaranteed spread might be $1,500–$3,000 per period, depending on the lookback. That's a 20–40% annualized return *on the cash deployed* — but only on the discount. Once you own the shares, that guaranteed return is gone. What remains is an unhedged bet on one stock.

Sell the shares. Redeploy into VTI. Keep participating in the ESPP — it's one of the best guaranteed returns available — but treat each purchase date as the endpoint, not a starting point.
