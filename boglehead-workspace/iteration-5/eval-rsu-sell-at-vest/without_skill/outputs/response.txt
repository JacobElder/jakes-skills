Your coworker is technically correct but is only telling you half the story.

**The tax mechanics are right**

At vesting, the $10,000 was already taxed as ordinary income (shows up on your W-2 at 32%). Your cost basis is now $50/share. If you sell immediately: no additional tax. If you hold >1 year and the stock rises, those *gains* get long-term capital gains rates (~15-20% for your bracket) instead of 32%. That's a real savings — but only on the gain portion, not the $10K you already paid tax on.

**What your coworker isn't telling you**

The tax benefit is real but modest compared to the risk you're taking on. Consider:

- You already have massive exposure to your employer: your salary, your health insurance, your career trajectory — all depend on this one company.
- RSUs are a way to force employees into concentrated positions. The smart move is generally to unwind that concentration, not double down on it.
- If the company struggles, you could lose your job *and* watch the shares crater simultaneously. That correlation is exactly what diversification is supposed to protect against.
- To save ~$1,200–$1,700 in taxes on a $10K position (the LTCG advantage on a hypothetical 100% gain), you're accepting unlimited downside risk on a concentrated single-stock position.

**The framing trap**

"I believe in my company" is the same reasoning people at Enron, Lehman, and Theranos had. Your positive view of the company is already priced in by the market — you don't have an informational edge over it.

**The clear-headed answer**

Sell the shares, pay no additional tax (basis = $50), invest the proceeds in a diversified low-cost index fund. Let the tax tail wag the investment dog only when the tax savings are large relative to the risk — a $1,500 potential savings on a $10K position doesn't meet that bar.

The year-wait strategy makes more sense for large, highly appreciated positions where the tax savings are substantial. For a freshly vested $10K lot, the risk you're bearing isn't worth it.
