No, break-even is the wrong framework — and this is one of the most important cases where the intuitive answer leads people astray.

## The break-even framing misses the point

Break-even analysis treats Social Security like an investment you're trying to maximize in expected value. But SS isn't an investment — it's **longevity insurance**. Its purpose isn't to maximize your cumulative payout if you die at the median age; it's to protect you financially if you *don't*.

Insurance isn't judged by whether you "break even." You don't regret homeowner's insurance if your house doesn't burn down.

## The actual risk you're insuring against

Claiming at 62 locks in roughly **43% less per month** than waiting until 70, permanently — and those inflation adjustments compound on a smaller base for the rest of your life.

The question isn't: "Will I live past 82?"

The question is: **"What happens to me financially if I'm 88 or 92?"**

- At 88, your ability to earn income is essentially zero.
- Your care costs are likely rising.
- Every month, you're receiving a benefit that is 43% smaller than it could have been — permanently.

If that scenario happens, you will have locked in a lower income floor during the years you are most financially vulnerable and least able to recover.

## About your family history

Your parents' ages are real data, but with limitations:

1. **Family history shifts the distribution, it doesn't determine your outcome.** Your parents dying at 74 and 78 raises your actuarial risk. It doesn't mean you die at 76. People with similar family histories routinely live into their late 80s — and if you do, you'll have locked in a 43% reduction for 20+ years.

2. **Medical advances compound over time.** Your parents' health outcomes reflected their era. Longevity is increasing; the tail is getting longer.

3. **You are not your parents.** Their causes of death, lifestyle, and health history matter more than their ages.

## The honest tradeoff

Claiming early *does* beat waiting if you die young — no honest person will tell you otherwise. If you die at 74 like your father, early claiming wins.

But the cost of being wrong is asymmetric:

- If you claim at 62 and die at 74: you made the right call. You got 12 years of smaller checks and a little extra.
- If you claim at 62 and live to 88: you made the wrong call. You got 26 years of checks that were 43% smaller, during a period when you couldn't earn more.

**The downside of being wrong is much larger in one direction than the other.** That's exactly when you want insurance protection — optimize for the tail, not the median.

## One additional factor worth knowing

If you're married, the calculus shifts further toward waiting. Your benefit becomes the **survivor benefit** for a spouse — whoever lives longer receives the higher of the two. Claiming early reduces that survivor benefit permanently as well.

## What Bogleheads typically say

The community consensus is: don't use break-even as your frame. Reframe it as: "What is my income floor if I live to 90?" Then design around protecting that floor.

If cash flow is the real constraint at 62 (you need the income now to cover expenses), that's a legitimate reason to claim earlier — but it's a different argument than "the math says claim early based on family history."

This isn't personalized financial advice — your specific health, marital status, other income sources, and tax situation all matter here. An advice-only fee-only financial planner or a Social Security-specific calculator (OpenSocialSecurity.org is free and well-regarded by Bogleheads) is worth the time for a decision this consequential and irreversible.
