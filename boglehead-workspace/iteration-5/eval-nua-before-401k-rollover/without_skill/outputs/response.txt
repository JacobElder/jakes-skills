Before you do a full rollover, there's a critical tax strategy your advisor may not have mentioned: **Net Unrealized Appreciation (NUA)**.

## The NUA Opportunity

Your company stock has $202,000 in unrealized gain ($240,000 value − $38,000 basis). How you handle that stock at separation determines whether that gain is taxed as **ordinary income** or **long-term capital gains**.

| Scenario | Tax treatment on the $202,000 NUA |
|---|---|
| Full IRA rollover | Ordinary income when withdrawn (22–24%+ federal) |
| NUA distribution | Long-term capital gains when sold (0–20% federal) |

## How NUA Works

Instead of rolling the company stock to an IRA, you take a **lump-sum in-kind distribution** of just the stock to a taxable brokerage account. You pay ordinary income tax now on the **cost basis only** (~$38,000). The remaining $380,000 rolls to the Vanguard IRA normally.

When you eventually sell the stock, the $202,000 appreciation is taxed at LTCG rates — not ordinary income rates.

## Rough Tax Comparison

Assuming 22% federal ordinary income, 15% LTCG:

- **Full rollover:** $202,000 × 22% = ~**$44,440** in future taxes on that gain
- **NUA strategy:** $202,000 × 15% = ~**$30,300** on that gain
- **Estimated federal savings: ~$14,000+** (state taxes may add more)

This grows if you're in a higher bracket, or shrinks if your retirement income is low enough to qualify for the 0% LTCG rate.

## When NUA Makes Sense

- Low cost basis relative to current value (yours is 16% — that's very favorable)
- You expect to be in a meaningful LTCG bracket
- You don't need to sell the stock immediately (you can hold it in taxable)
- You can afford the ordinary income tax hit on $38,000 in the distribution year

## What to Do Before Deciding

1. **Ask your advisor specifically:** "Have you modeled the NUA strategy for my company stock?" If they haven't, that's a meaningful gap.
2. **Get a CPA involved** — the lump-sum distribution must meet IRS requirements (it has to happen in a single tax year after a qualifying event like retirement).
3. **Model your 2026 income** — the NUA distribution year will include $38,000 of ordinary income, so timing matters.

Your advisor isn't wrong that the IRA is simpler — but "simple" here could cost you $14,000+ in unnecessary taxes. Worth a 30-minute conversation with a tax professional before you sign anything.
