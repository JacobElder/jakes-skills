Great question — and I mean that genuinely, because it's exactly the right question to ask. The 5-year outperformance of AGTHX looks compelling on paper. Let me work through why the Boglehead answer is still "switch," and why the "diversify management styles" logic doesn't hold up.

---

**The Expense Ratio Math Is Working Against You — Compoundingly**

AGTHX charges 0.63%. The Vanguard 500 Index charges 0.03%. That's a 0.60% annual drag on every dollar in AGTHX, every year, forever.

On your $120,000 over the next 20 years (assuming 7% nominal growth before fees):

- Vanguard 500 Index at 0.03% → roughly $455,000
- AGTHX at 0.63% → roughly $420,000

That 0.60% difference compounds into roughly $35,000 in lost wealth — before we even discuss whether AGTHX will continue to outperform. To break even, AGTHX has to beat the index by at least 0.60% per year, net. It has to beat it by *more* than that to be worth the risk.

John Bogle put it plainly: "In investing, you get what you don't pay for."

---

**The 5-Year Outperformance Is Real — and Almost Certainly Misleading**

You said AGTHX has beaten the S&P 500 by about 1.5%/year over the past 5 years. That's true. Here's why it doesn't tell you what you think it does:

**1. Five years is an extremely short window for evaluating active management.**

SPIVA (S&P's semi-annual scorecard, the most rigorous analysis of active fund performance) consistently shows that roughly 80–90% of active managers underperform their benchmark over 15-year periods. The key word is *15 years*. Over 5-year windows, a meaningful minority of funds do outperform — by luck, by style tilt, by sector concentration. The 5-year number is basically noise from a statistical standpoint.

**2. Past performance does not predict future performance. Empirically.**

This is not a cliche — it's the finding from essentially every serious academic study. The funds that top the 5-year rankings are not disproportionately represented in the next 5-year rankings. SPIVA even measures "persistence" directly and finds near-zero persistence of outperformance beyond chance. The fund that beat the market by 1.5%/year last 5 years is not more likely than average to beat it the next 5 years.

Bogle called it: "Buying funds based purely on past performance is one of the stupidest things an investor can do." Harsh, but the evidence supports it.

**3. American Funds specifically: understand what you own.**

AGTHX is one of the better-known American Funds products, and it's genuinely one of the better-run active funds out there — low turnover for an active fund, experienced managers, below-average fees *for an active fund*. That said, "better than most active funds" is a low bar when the comparison is a 0.03% index fund. American Funds products are also typically sold by commissioned advisors (the share class you're in matters — the R6 share class available in 401(k)s is better than the retail class). In a 401(k) context, you're probably in a clean institutional share class, which is the best-case version of AGTHX.

**4. The recent outperformance has a likely explanation.**

AGTHX is a growth-tilted large-cap fund. Growth stocks — especially US large-cap tech — massively outperformed value and international stocks from roughly 2017 through 2024. If AGTHX is tilted toward large-cap growth (which it is), it would naturally outperform a broad S&P 500 index during that specific window. That's a style tailwind, not manager alpha. You can't count on that tailwind continuing.

---

**"Shouldn't I Keep Half to Diversify Management Styles?"**

This is a common intuition, and I want to take it seriously before explaining why it doesn't work the way you're thinking.

"Diversifying management styles" is not a recognized source of portfolio diversification in the way that, say, adding bonds or international stocks is. Here's why:

- **The S&P 500 index and AGTHX are not diversifiers of each other.** AGTHX holds a concentrated set of US large-cap stocks. The S&P 500 index holds a broad set of US large-cap stocks. Their correlation is extremely high — probably 0.90+. Adding AGTHX to a Vanguard 500 Index position doesn't reduce your risk in any meaningful way.

- **"Active management" is not an asset class.** Active management is a *cost* layered on top of an asset class. You're not getting exposure to something new; you're getting the same stocks, selected by a human, at higher cost.

- **The correct diversifiers are actual different assets:** international stocks, bonds, maybe small-cap value if you want to tilt. Not "also an active fund."

If you genuinely want more diversification than a plain S&P 500 fund, the Boglehead answer is to add a total international index fund (e.g., Vanguard Total International), not AGTHX. That gives you exposure to ~7,000 stocks in 40+ countries that you don't own yet.

---

**What I'd Actually Suggest**

In your 401(k), check what other funds are available. Ideally you want:

1. A US broad market index or S&P 500 index (the Vanguard 500 Index you mentioned — great, use this)
2. A total international index fund (if available)
3. A bond index fund (if you want/need fixed income based on your time horizon)

If the Vanguard 500 Index is the only low-cost option, a 100% allocation there is completely fine for the equity portion of your retirement savings. You can add international exposure through an IRA if it's not available in the 401(k).

The switch from AGTHX to Vanguard 500 Index inside a 401(k) is a tax-free exchange — no capital gains, no tax consequence. This is actually the ideal situation to make the switch; you'd pay capital gains taxes to do this in a taxable account.

---

**Bottom Line**

The 5-year outperformance feels like a good reason to stay put. The math and the evidence say otherwise:

- 0.60%/year in fees is a guaranteed headwind AGTHX has to overcome every single year
- The 5-year window is too short to distinguish skill from luck or style tailwinds
- 15-year persistence of active outperformance is essentially zero across the industry
- "Diversifying management styles" doesn't accomplish what diversification is supposed to accomplish

Switch to the Vanguard 500 Index. If you want real diversification, find out if your 401(k) has a total international index option and add that.

This isn't personalized financial advice for your specific tax situation — if you have a complex situation or are near retirement, a fee-only fiduciary advisor (or the bogleheads.org forum, which is free and excellent) is worth a conversation.
