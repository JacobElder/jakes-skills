TASK: "I have $200,000 in Tesla stock in my taxable brokerage account. I bought it 3 years ago for $65,000, so I'm sitting on a huge gain. It's now about 60% of my total portfolio. My financial advisor says 'let your winners run' and warns me that the capital gains tax would be enormous if I sell. Should I keep holding?"

---

Let's be direct: "let your winners run" is a psychological bias dressed up as investment advice, not a strategy. And the capital gains tax argument, while real, is almost certainly backwards here. Let's work through this carefully.

---

THE CORE PROBLEM: CONCENTRATION RISK

You have 60% of your total portfolio in a single stock. That is a dangerously concentrated position by any standard, and the Boglehead view is unambiguous on this: individual stock concentration at that scale is not an investment strategy — it's speculation, with your financial future at stake.

Tesla is a particularly volatile name. It has historically swung 50-70% in a single year, in both directions. It fell roughly 65% in 2022 alone. A 60% drop in Tesla from today's price would vaporize roughly 36% of your entire portfolio's value. That is not a tail risk — it is a well-documented, already-happened risk.

Meanwhile, your upside is capped by the specific fortunes of one company. A total-market index fund (VTI) already holds Tesla at its market-cap weight. If Tesla continues to outperform, you would capture that proportionally in a diversified portfolio. What you're getting by holding 60% in Tesla is pure idiosyncratic risk — the kind the market does not reward you for taking, because it's unnecessary and avoidable.

---

THE ADVISOR'S ARGUMENT: NAMED AND REFUTED

"Let your winners run."

This is not an investment principle. It is a behavioral bias. The fact that Tesla has gone up a lot tells you nothing about what it will do next. Past appreciation is not a signal of future return; it is already priced into today's share price. Every buyer of Tesla at today's price is making the same forward-looking bet you are — but without an embedded gain complicating their judgment.

The Boglehead test is simple: if you had $200,000 in cash today, would you put 60% of your total portfolio into a single stock? If the answer is no — and for almost anyone, it should be — then the only reason you're holding it is because you already own it. That's inertia, not a strategy.

"The capital gains tax would be enormous."

This is the more serious argument, and it deserves a real answer rather than dismissal.

Your gain is $135,000 ($200,000 - $65,000 cost basis). Since you've held more than one year, this qualifies as a long-term capital gain. The federal rate is 15% or 20% depending on your income, plus a 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) if your income exceeds $200K single / $250K married. At the worst-case federal rate of 23.8%, you'd owe roughly $32,000 in federal taxes on the full gain. Add state taxes if you live somewhere like California (up to 13.3%), and yes, a full-year sale could run $40,000-$50,000 or more in total taxes.

That is a real number. Now here's the reframe: you are currently carrying $200,000 in a stock that has demonstrated it can fall 50-65% in a bad year. A 60% drawdown in Tesla would cost you roughly $120,000 in portfolio value. The tax bill you're trying to avoid is smaller than the loss you'd absorb in a single bad year for this stock. The tax is certain and bounded. The downside is uncertain and potentially much larger.

Capital gains tax is the cost of having gains. It is not a reason to hold an undiversified position indefinitely.

---

THE MATH IN ONE PLACE

Scenario A — Sell now, pay taxes, diversify:
  - Gross proceeds: $200,000
  - Federal LTCG tax on $135,000 gain (worst-case 23.8%): ~$32,000
  - You keep: ~$168,000, immediately reinvested in a broadly diversified portfolio
  - Your downside: proportional to the broad market (a 50% market crash = ~$84,000 loss from here)

Scenario B — Hold indefinitely:
  - Current value: $200,000
  - Tesla falls 60% (which it has done): you lose $120,000
  - You keep: $80,000
  - Your downside avoided the tax bill and lost $120,000 anyway

The tax you're trying to avoid ($32,000-$50,000) is the exact cost of buying yourself out of $120,000+ in potential downside. That is a reasonable trade.

---

WHAT BOGLEHEADS ACTUALLY DO IN YOUR SITUATION

The Boglehead answer for a large concentrated taxable position is not "sell everything tomorrow." It is: diversify systematically over 2-3 tax years to manage the tax hit.

Step 1: Spread sales over multiple tax years.
Sell roughly one-third of your Tesla position each year for the next three years. This spreads the gains across three tax returns, may keep you from being pushed into a higher income bracket, and makes each year's tax bill much more manageable. Start this year or next, depending on which year is better for your income picture.

Step 2: Harvest any available losses to offset.
If you have any other positions sitting at a loss, sell those in the same tax year you sell Tesla. Capital losses offset capital gains dollar-for-dollar. This is worth doing before each sale.

Step 3: Consider which year is lowest income.
If you have any upcoming years with lower income — a career break, retirement, reduced work — the 0% federal long-term capital gains rate applies at the federal level for income up to ~$47,000 single / ~$94,000 married (2025 figures). If your taxable income will be low enough, you may pay no federal tax on some of this gain. That's worth knowing.

Step 4: If you are charitably inclined, consider donating shares directly.
Donating appreciated Tesla shares directly to a donor-advised fund (DAF) lets you deduct the full fair market value and pay zero capital gains tax on the donated shares. If you give to charity anyway, this is one of the most efficient moves available.

Step 5: Reinvest proceeds into a total-market portfolio.
In a taxable account, the Boglehead default is:
  - VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF, 0.03% ER) for US equities
  - VXUS (Vanguard Total International ETF, 0.07% ER) for international
  - Or VT (Vanguard Total World ETF, 0.07%) if you want a one-fund solution

Holding international (VXUS) in taxable is particularly useful because it gives you the foreign tax credit — a real annual benefit that disappears if international is held in a 401(k) or IRA.

The target: get Tesla below 5-10% of your total portfolio over 2-3 years. 60% is genuinely dangerous, regardless of how good the company is.

---

ON YOUR FINANCIAL ADVISOR

A few things worth naming directly.

"Let your winners run" combined with tax-focused hesitation to sell is an argument that results in you doing nothing — which means you continue holding the position, and your advisor continues managing it. If your advisor is compensated on AUM (a percentage of assets managed), they have no financial incentive to recommend you simplify into a low-cost index portfolio. That does not make them a bad person, but it is a real incentive worth recognizing.

The question to ask them: "If I didn't already own this stock and had $200,000 cash today, would you recommend I put 60% of my portfolio into Tesla?" If the answer is no, then the only thing keeping you here is the tax argument. And we've established that the tax math doesn't support holding indefinitely.

A fee-only fiduciary advisor — someone who charges a flat fee for their time rather than a commission or AUM percentage — has no financial stake in your staying concentrated. NAPFA.org and the Garrett Planning Network both maintain directories. A one-time consultation to map out a multi-year unwinding strategy is money well spent given the dollar amounts involved.

---

THE BOTTOM LINE

The Boglehead answer is clear: sell Tesla and diversify. Do it systematically over 2-3 years to manage the tax bite. Reinvest in a total-market portfolio at minimal cost. Don't let fear of taxes keep you permanently concentrated in a single volatile stock.

"Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack." — John Bogle

You found a needle. It appreciated enormously. Now buy the haystack.
