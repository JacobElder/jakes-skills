There are a few things happening here that deserve to be addressed separately, because your advisor is bundling together two different arguments — and one of them is genuinely true while the other is being used to justify something that probably isn't in your interest.

---

THE LEGITIMATE PART OF THE TAX ARGUMENT

Your gain is real and the tax math is real. You bought at $65,000 and it's now worth $200,000, so your unrealized long-term capital gain is $135,000. If you sold the entire position today, at the 15% federal LTCG rate you'd owe roughly $20,250 in federal taxes (higher if your income puts you in the 20% bracket, plus the 3.8% NIIT if your AGI exceeds the threshold, plus any state capital gains tax). That's a material hit, and your advisor isn't wrong to flag it.

Taxes are a real cost. The question is whether the tax tail should wag the risk management dog — and it usually shouldn't.

---

WHERE THE ADVISOR GOES WRONG

"Let your winners run" is a Wall Street phrase that sounds wise but papers over a serious problem: concentration risk. Having 60% of your total portfolio in a single stock is not a portfolio strategy — it's a bet. A big one. On one company.

Here's the thing Bogleheads say clearly: individual stocks are not appropriate as a core portfolio strategy, and the evidence behind that isn't opinion. Bessembinder's research on US stocks from 1926 to 2016 found that 58% of individual stocks underperformed Treasury bills over their lifetime, and essentially all the net wealth creation in US markets came from about 4% of stocks. Even professional fund managers — with research teams, Bloomberg terminals, and decades of experience — underperform simple index funds 80%+ of the time over 15 years. A retail investor's odds are worse, not better.

The fact that Tesla has been one of the 4% of stocks that performed spectacularly does not make Tesla a reliable investment going forward. It makes you lucky — and that luck is already sitting in your brokerage account. The question is what to do now, not what happened.

"Let your winners run" also commits a second error: it conflates past performance with future expectation. A stock that has tripled is not more likely to triple again because it tripled before. The price already reflects everything the market knows about Tesla's future. You are not holding a winner — you are holding a position in a company at its current market price, which may or may not reflect fair value.

---

THE REAL CALCULUS: PAYING TAX TO BUY DIVERSIFICATION

Here's a reframe that Bogleheads use in exactly this situation:

Selling your Tesla position and reinvesting in a broad index fund is not "losing money to taxes." It is paying a one-time fee to buy permanent diversification. The question is whether that fee is worth it.

Let's run rough numbers:
- You sell $200,000 of Tesla. You owe, say, $25,000-$30,000 in combined federal and state taxes (depending on your state — this could be more or less).
- You now have approximately $170,000-$175,000 in a total market index fund (VTI, VTSAX, or equivalent).
- That $170,000 now participates in the broad US (and international, if you add VXUS) market. It will never go to zero because a single company had a bad earnings quarter, ran into regulatory trouble, or saw a leadership change.

What's the cost of not diversifying? If Tesla drops 50% — not a hypothetical; it dropped roughly 65% from late 2021 to early 2023 — your $200,000 position becomes $100,000. You would have needed that position to fall by roughly 13-15% to "break even" with the after-tax diversified outcome. A 13-15% drop is a completely ordinary single-stock correction. A 50-70% drop is something Tesla itself has already done.

The risk you are holding is not symmetric. The upside is Tesla continues to outperform an already-high valuation. The downside is a company-specific event — a product recall, regulatory action, CEO distraction, competitive disruption, accounting irregularity — that would take your $200,000 down by a large percentage while the total market index barely registers it.

---

THE STEP-UP IN BASIS ARGUMENT

Your advisor may be implicitly pointing toward a "hold until death" strategy, which is a real thing. Under current tax law, heirs inherit assets at the fair market value on the date of death — the embedded gain disappears permanently. No tax ever paid.

This is a legitimate reason to hold a highly appreciated position in taxable — but only if all of the following are true:
1. You can genuinely stomach a 50-80% drawdown in this position and hold through it without selling (which would give you the worst of both worlds: the volatility and the taxes).
2. The position does not represent a risk to your retirement income or financial security.
3. You are not planning to spend this money within the next 5-15 years.
4. Estate planning is actually a priority in your situation.

If Tesla drops 70% and you're watching $200,000 become $60,000, would you hold without flinching? If the honest answer is anything other than a clear yes, the step-up strategy isn't really available to you — it requires iron discipline over potentially decades.

---

WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO

You don't have to make this a binary decision. A staged approach is both tax-efficient and rational:

1. Decide what a tolerable single-stock weighting is. Bogleheads generally treat individual stocks as satellite or entertainment money — 5% of net worth or less. You're at 60%. The target is probably somewhere in the 0-10% range, depending on your conviction and risk tolerance.

2. Sell a portion now. You don't have to sell all of it. Selling $75,000-$100,000 worth and redirecting it to a three-fund portfolio cuts your concentration significantly while spreading the tax hit across this tax year. You could sell more over the next 1-2 years to continue reducing the concentration gradually, managing tax bracket exposure year by year.

3. Stop adding new money to Tesla. Whatever new savings you have — 401(k) contributions, IRA contributions, future taxable contributions — none of it goes into Tesla. Let time and new contributions do some of the rebalancing work passively.

4. Donate appreciated shares to charity if giving is part of your life. Donating Tesla shares directly to a donor-advised fund (DAF) gives you a fair-market-value deduction and avoids capital gains entirely. This is the most tax-efficient way to get shares out of the position without writing a check to the IRS.

5. Use tax-loss harvesting elsewhere if you have any losing positions in the taxable account. Losses can offset some of the Tesla gain dollar-for-dollar.

---

ON YOUR ADVISOR

"Let your winners run" and "the tax would be enormous" are both arguments for doing nothing — which means no transaction, no scrutiny, no change. When advice consistently points toward inaction, it's worth understanding the advisor's compensation structure. Is your advisor a fiduciary? Are they fee-only or commission-based? A 1% AUM fee on your portfolio creates zero incentive to rock the boat.

A fee-only or advice-only fiduciary advisor (NAPFA.org maintains a directory) would charge you a flat fee or hourly rate, model the actual after-tax scenarios for your specific income and state, and give you a recommendation with no financial stake in the outcome.

Alternatively, the bogleheads.org forum ("Investing - Help with Personal Investments") is free. Post your full situation using their portfolio review template and you'll get detailed, experienced feedback from people who have seen this exact situation dozens of times.

---

THE BOTTOM LINE

Your advisor is correct that the tax bill is real. Your advisor is not serving you well by using that tax bill as a reason to hold 60% of your portfolio in a single stock indefinitely. The tax cost is a one-time friction. The concentration risk is ongoing, every single day you hold.

Bogle: "The two greatest enemies of the equity fund investor are expenses and emotions." Attachment to a winning position because it has won is an emotion, not a strategy. You are not holding a winner — you are holding a large single-stock bet at today's price, whatever that price reflects.

You don't need to sell everything immediately. But you do need a deliberate plan to reach a sensible allocation — and "let your winners run" is not a plan. It's a feeling dressed up as advice.

This isn't personalized financial or tax advice. Your specific income, tax bracket, state, and overall financial picture matter significantly here. A fee-only fiduciary or CPA who works with investors can model the actual numbers for your situation.
