You're raising a fair challenge, and the honest answer is: you're partly right. Whole life insurance does have legitimate uses in specific situations. The Boglehead position isn't "whole life is never defensible, full stop." It's "whole life is almost never the right answer for most people, and the sales pitch is routinely misleading." Let me work through what's actually going on here.

---

THE LEGITIMATE CASES EXIST — BUT THEY'RE NARROW

The Boglehead community explicitly acknowledges real exceptions: estate planning for wealthy ($10M+ estates) needing liquidity for estate taxes via ILITs, funding buy-sell agreements, supporting a disabled dependent, and certain asset-protection purposes in specific states. If you have a genuine estate tax exposure and your CFP structured an ILIT for liquidity, that's a defensible move — it's not the typical salesperson pitch.

If that's genuinely your situation, fair enough. But let's stress-test the reasoning, because "high income + estate planning" is also the exact framing used in virtually every whole life sales pitch to high earners, legitimate or not.

---

THE QUESTIONS THAT ACTUALLY MATTER

Before accepting "my situation is different," a Boglehead would want to know:

1. What is your estate's size relative to the federal exemption? As of 2025, the federal estate tax exemption is roughly $13.6M per individual, $27.2M for a married couple. Unless your estate is approaching or exceeding those numbers, estate taxes are not a problem you have. High income doesn't automatically equal estate tax exposure. If you're a high-earning professional with $1–3M in assets, you don't need an estate planning vehicle for tax purposes.

2. Have you fully funded your tax-advantaged accounts first? The Boglehead funding waterfall puts 401(k) match, HSA, IRA, maxed 401(k), and mega backdoor Roth all before any insurance-as-investment product. For a high earner in 2025, that's potentially $70,000–$85,000+ per year in tax-advantaged space. If any of those buckets are unfilled, whole life is jumping the queue.

3. What's the internal rate of return on your specific policy? "It's been performing well" after 5 years can be misleading. The first several years of a whole life policy largely go to commissions, fees, and insurance costs. The internal rate of return (IRR) on cash value — relative to what you've paid in premiums — is almost always 0–2% for the first 10–15 years, even on a "well-performing" policy. You'd need an in-force illustration to know where yours actually stands. Compare that number against what the same dollars would have returned in a total-market index fund at comparable risk.

4. What's your uncle's compensation arrangement? This isn't an accusation — it's a structural question. A fee-only fiduciary CFP who charges a flat fee or hourly rate has no incentive to sell you a product. A CFP who also holds an insurance license and earns commissions has a compensation motive worth acknowledging. The disclosure should be explicit: "I was paid a commission of X on this policy" or "I was not compensated beyond my fee." Many excellent CFPs hold insurance licenses and make honest recommendations; the question is whether this specific recommendation came with a clear conflict-of-interest disclosure.

---

ON BEING "TOO DOGMATIC"

The Boglehead pushback on whole life isn't dogmatic in the way that "never eat carbs" is dogmatic. It's grounded in three concrete things:

First, the math is usually bad. The IRR on whole life cash value, compared to term + invest the difference in a low-cost index fund, underperforms for most of the policy's life — often by a wide margin. Illustrations shown at sale assume dividends that historically underperform their projections. This is not ideology; it's the documented quantitative track record of the product.

Second, the sales incentives are misaligned. Whole life commissions are commonly 50–100% of the first year's premium. The structural incentive for the person recommending it is significant, and "this is right for your situation" has been used to sell unsuitable policies to high earners for decades. That's not conspiracy — it's how the compensation structure works.

Third, the bundling destroys clarity. You can't easily see what you're paying for the insurance component vs. the savings component vs. the advisor's compensation. That opacity reliably favors the seller.

None of that makes whole life categorically wrong in all cases. It makes it a product that requires exceptional scrutiny and a verified legitimate use case to justify — which is why the default is healthy skepticism, not blind acceptance.

---

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO GIVEN THAT YOU OWN THE POLICY

Since you're 5 years in, the relevant question isn't whether you should have bought it — it's what to do now. Here's the Boglehead framework for your position:

- Under 5 years: the conventional advice is to surrender. You've lost the most to upfront fees, but haven't put so much in that the sunk cost makes exit irrational.
- 5–15 years (where you are now): this is the gray zone. Request an in-force illustration from the insurer showing projected cash value over the next 10, 20, and 30 years. Calculate the IRR compared to what you've paid in. Compare that to what the same dollars would return in a taxable index fund at 7% real return. If the IRR is competitive and you have genuine estate tax exposure, keeping it is defensible. If not, a 1035 exchange to a low-cost deferred variable annuity (Fidelity Personal Retirement at ~0.25% ER) can preserve the tax-deferred status and let you exit the insurance drag.
- Over 15–20 years: the worst fees are typically already paid; the policy often becomes more competitive. Usually the right call is to keep it.

You are right at the boundary of the "evaluate carefully" zone. Get the in-force illustration before making any decision either way.

---

THE BOTTOM LINE

"Can whole life make sense for some people in some situations?" — yes, genuinely. The Boglehead position isn't that it's never appropriate; it's that it's appropriate in a small fraction of situations, the pitch for it routinely overstates its value, and the burden of proof should be high.

The specific tests worth running on your situation:
- Do you have actual estate tax exposure (net worth approaching estate tax exemption thresholds)?
- Have you fully funded all available tax-advantaged accounts first?
- Have you calculated the actual IRR on your policy, not just accepted "performing well" at face value?
- Did your CFP disclose any commission income from this recommendation, and are you confident the recommendation is fee-only?

If those answers all check out, your uncle may well be right. If they don't, you're in the much more common situation of a high earner who was sold a product on the "estate planning" framing when the underlying math didn't support it.

One note: this isn't personalized financial or tax advice. For a situation involving large policy values, actual estate tax exposure, and specific state laws, a fee-only fiduciary advisor independent of the original sale — NAPFA.org has a directory — is worth a one-time flat-fee consultation to pressure-test the recommendation with fresh eyes.
