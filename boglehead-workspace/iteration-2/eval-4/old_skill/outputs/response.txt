Great question, and your situation is actually pretty clear-cut once you run it through the Boglehead funding waterfall. Let me walk through it step by step.

---

## Your Situation at a Glance

- $1,200/month to deploy
- No 401(k) employer match
- $18,000 in credit card debt at 24% APR
- $3,000 in savings
- Enrolled in an HDHP (HSA-eligible)

The absence of an employer match changes the calculus significantly. Normally, capturing the match is the single highest-priority step — an immediate 50–100% return that beats everything, including high-interest debt. Since there's no match here, we skip straight past that step.

---

## Step 1: Starter Emergency Fund — You're Already There (Mostly)

The waterfall starts with a starter emergency fund of roughly $1,000–1 month of expenses before doing anything else. Your $3,000 in savings likely covers this. Keep it in a high-yield savings account (Ally, Marcus, SoFi, etc.) or a money market fund earning ~4–5%. Don't touch it.

If your monthly expenses run more than $3,000, bump up your savings cushion slightly before going all-in on debt payoff — but for most people, $3,000 is sufficient as a starter fund.

---

## Step 2: Attack the Credit Card Debt — This Is the Priority

24% APR is the equivalent of a guaranteed, risk-free, tax-free 24% return. There is no investment in the world that reliably delivers 24% returns. The stock market, long-term, averages something like 7% real after inflation. Paying off that debt is literally twice as good as any reasonable expected investment return.

With no employer match to chase first, this is your #1 priority.

**The math:** $18,000 at 24% APR accrues roughly $360/month in interest. Every month you carry this balance, you're burning $360. Putting $1,200/month toward this — after covering your minimum payments — means you'd pay it off in about 16–18 months depending on your minimum payment structure.

The Boglehead position here is unambiguous: pay off credit cards before investing in taxable accounts. Full stop.

---

## Step 3: Max the HSA — But Only Once You've Cleared the Debt (or Alongside It, With Caveats)

Here's where it gets slightly interesting. You're enrolled in an HDHP, which makes you eligible for an HSA — and the HSA is arguably the most tax-advantaged account that exists. It's triple-tax-advantaged:

1. Contributions go in pre-tax (or are deductible above-the-line)
2. Growth is tax-free
3. Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free

The 2025 HSA contribution limit is $4,300 for individual coverage (or $8,550 for family coverage).

**The Boglehead HSA hack:** if you can pay current medical expenses out of pocket, let the HSA invest and grow for decades, then reimburse yourself for those old expenses later — tax-free, with no time limit. Keep your receipts. This turns the HSA into a stealth retirement account.

**Should you contribute to the HSA while still carrying the credit card debt?**

This is a legitimate judgment call. The strict waterfall says to knock out the 24% debt first. But there's a reasonable counterargument: HSA contributions reduce your taxable income now, which effectively lowers the cost of contributing. If you're in the 22% federal bracket, for example, a $4,300 HSA contribution costs you only about $3,354 out of pocket after the tax benefit — which somewhat narrows the gap between the guaranteed 24% return (debt payoff) and the tax benefit of the HSA.

My lean: given how brutal 24% is, prioritize the debt first. Once it's gone (roughly 16–18 months at your pace), immediately redirect toward maxing the HSA.

---

## Step 4: After the Debt Is Gone — The Full Waterfall

Once you've cleared the $18,000 in credit card debt (call it ~18 months from now), your $1,200/month opens up considerably. Here's what the waterfall looks like at that point:

1. **Max the HSA first** (~$358/month for individual, or $712/month for family). This beats the IRA in priority because of the triple tax advantage.
2. **Max a Roth IRA** — $7,000/year in 2025 (~$583/month). Given that you're presumably not at the income phaseout yet (Roth phaseout begins at $150k MAGI for single filers in 2025), go Roth. Young savers with lower current income benefit from paying tax now and letting the money grow tax-free forever.
3. **Max the 401(k)** — $23,500/year in 2025. No match doesn't mean it's not worth contributing; the tax deferral (or Roth conversion, if offered) is still valuable. At $1,200/month, you likely can't max this and the above simultaneously until your income grows, but contribute what you can after HSA and IRA.

A reasonable post-debt allocation at $1,200/month might look like:
- $358/month → HSA (individual) or as much as you can toward the full limit
- $583/month → Roth IRA
- Remainder → 401(k) or taxable brokerage

---

## Step 5: Build Out Your Full Emergency Fund

Once the credit card debt is gone and you're funding tax-advantaged accounts, fill your emergency fund to 3–6 months of essential expenses. Your current $3,000 is a good starter, but you'll want $9,000–$18,000 (depending on your monthly expenses and income stability). High-yield savings or short-term Treasuries — not stocks.

---

## What to Invest In (Once You Get There)

When you do start investing inside the HSA, Roth IRA, or 401(k), keep it simple. The three-fund portfolio is the Boglehead default:

- **US Total Stock Market:** VTI (Vanguard ETF), FSKAX or FZROX (Fidelity), SWTSX (Schwab)
- **Total International Stock:** VXUS (Vanguard), FTIHX (Fidelity), IXUS (iShares)
- **US Total Bond Market:** BND (Vanguard), FXNAX (Fidelity), AGG (iShares)

Or just pick a target-date index fund (Vanguard's are excellent; Fidelity's Freedom Index funds work too) and call it done. It'll automatically rebalance and shift toward bonds as you near retirement. That's a fully defensible, fully Boglehead answer.

**Watch expense ratios.** Anything above 0.20% deserves scrutiny. Vanguard, Fidelity, and Schwab offer the core index funds at 0.03%–0.06%. That's the universe you want to be in.

---

## The Summary in Plain English

Right now: put your $1,200/month toward the credit card debt. Pay it off as fast as you can — in roughly 16–18 months. Keep your $3,000 emergency fund untouched in a high-yield savings account.

Once the debt is gone: contribute to the HSA first, then max a Roth IRA, then put the rest in your 401(k). Invest in low-cost total market index funds.

The credit card debt is the enemy right now. At 24%, it's eating your wealth faster than the market can build it. Eliminate it, then build.
