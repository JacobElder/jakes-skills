Short answer: no, it's not a good deal — and the reason is specific and worth understanding.

You're paying 1% AUM for someone to hold Vanguard index funds on your behalf. Those funds themselves are excellent choices — low-cost, broadly diversified, exactly what a Boglehead would recommend. The problem isn't the portfolio; it's the price you're paying for someone else to maintain it.

**What 1% AUM actually costs you**

On $350,000, 1% is $3,500 per year. That sounds reasonable in isolation, but the real cost is the compounding drag over time.

Assume your portfolio grows at 7% annually before the advisory fee. With the 1% fee, your effective return is ~6%. Over 20 years:

- $350,000 at 7% → ~$1,354,000
- $350,000 at 6% → ~$1,123,000

That difference is roughly $231,000 — or about 17% of your ending wealth gone to fees. Over 30 years the gap is even larger. Bogle called this "the tyranny of compounding costs," and this is exactly what he meant.

**The real question: what are you getting for $3,500/year?**

If your advisor is actively managing your Vanguard index fund positions — which in a Boglehead portfolio means: set the allocation once, rebalance once or twice a year, don't touch anything else — then the honest answer is that you're paying $3,500/year for something you could do yourself in about two hours a year.

The value proposition for AUM-based advisors typically rests on:

1. Behavioral coaching (stopping you from panic-selling in a downturn)
2. Comprehensive financial planning (tax strategy, estate planning, insurance review, Social Security optimization)
3. Rebalancing and fund selection

The first is real but hard to value. If you've been with this advisor for 6 years and you've stayed the course through market volatility partly because of their guidance, that's worth something — just probably not $3,500/year worth. The second and third, for a straightforward index fund portfolio, are largely something you can handle yourself or get done for a flat fee.

**What the alternatives look like**

Option 1: Full DIY. A three-fund portfolio at Vanguard (VTI + VXUS + BND, or a single target-date fund) costs roughly 0.03–0.15% in fund expenses — call it $105–$525/year on your balance versus $3,500. Set it once, rebalance annually, stay the course. The annual time commitment is real but small.

Option 2: Advice-only or fee-only fiduciary advisor. You pay a flat fee ($1,500–$5,000) for a comprehensive financial plan, or hourly ($250–$400/hr) for specific questions. You get professional-grade advice without an ongoing 1% drag on your wealth. NAPFA (napfa.org) and the Garrett Planning Network maintain searchable directories. This is often a better deal than AUM for someone with a clean, uncomplicated index-fund portfolio.

Option 3: Vanguard Personal Advisor Services (~0.30% AUM). If you want a real human advisor on call and prefer not to DIY, Vanguard's hybrid robo+human service is a reasonable middle ground. The funds they use are Vanguard index funds — same as you have now — but the cost is about 70% lower than what you're paying.

**The advisor is not wrong about the funds**

To be clear: your advisor has put you in an excellent portfolio. A lot of advisors at this price point put clients into expensive actively managed funds or proprietary products that benefit the advisor's commission rather than your returns. Yours didn't do that, and that's meaningful.

But here's the catch: the fact that the portfolio is good is also the argument for leaving. The reason you might need an advisor to actively manage a portfolio is if it's complex, requires ongoing tax-loss harvesting, has significant estate planning dimensions, or involves instruments that are hard to understand. A portfolio of low-cost Vanguard index funds doesn't require active management. It requires mostly nothing.

**What to consider before making a move**

A few questions worth sitting with:

- Is your advisor providing services beyond portfolio management — tax strategy, estate planning input, insurance review, cash-flow planning? If yes, that changes the calculus somewhat.
- Do you have a taxable account with large unrealized gains? If so, moving might trigger a capital gains event — worth modeling before you act.
- Are you genuinely confident you'd stay the course through a 40% drawdown without someone to talk you off the ledge? Be honest. That's worth something.

If the answer to those is largely "no / not really / yes I could manage it," then you're paying roughly $3,500/year for a service that costs less than $500/year to run yourself, and you've been doing so for six years.

That's about $21,000 in total fees over the relationship — money that, invested alongside the rest of your portfolio at 7%, would be worth considerably more by the time you retire.
