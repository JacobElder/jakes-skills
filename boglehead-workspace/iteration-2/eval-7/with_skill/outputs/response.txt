Short answer: no, it's not a good deal — and the fact that the portfolio is in low-cost Vanguard index funds actually makes it a worse deal, not a better one. Here's why.

---

THE MATH FIRST

You have $350,000 with an advisor charging 1% AUM. That's $3,500 per year you're paying right now. At your current portfolio size, assume 7% annual growth before fees. Over the next 20 years:

- At 7% gross, $350k grows to roughly $1,354,000
- At 6% net (after the 1% fee), $350k grows to roughly $1,123,000

That's a $231,000 difference — on a $350,000 starting balance. The compounding effect of a persistent 1% drag is brutal. Bogle called it the "tyranny of compounding costs," and he was right. "In investing, you get what you don't pay for."

---

WHY THE VANGUARD FUNDS MAKE IT WORSE, NOT BETTER

This is the key thing to understand, and it's counterintuitive: if your portfolio were in high-cost active funds, you might at least be getting something in exchange for the fee — an attempt at outperformance (usually failed, but an attempt). But you're in low-cost Vanguard index funds. Those funds cost roughly 0.03–0.07% per year on their own. You can hold them yourself, at a Vanguard or Fidelity or Schwab brokerage account, for free.

So what exactly are you paying $3,500 a year for?

The advisor isn't selecting superior funds — Vanguard index funds are the obvious Boglehead-approved choice, available to anyone. The advisor isn't adding alpha by picking stocks. The advisor isn't protecting you from high-cost active funds — you're already in index funds. You're paying $3,500 annually for someone to do something you could do yourself in an afternoon.

This is the irony: a portfolio in terrible high-ER active funds at least has a theoretical (even if statistically unlikely) claim on outperformance. A portfolio in index funds with a 1% AUM fee on top is paying 1% for nothing.

---

WHAT YOU ARE PAYING FOR (AND WHETHER IT'S WORTH IT)

To be fair, let's steelman the case for keeping the advisor:

- Behavioral coaching: Advisors can talk clients off the ledge during a market crash. If your advisor has kept you from selling during 2020 or other corrections, that has real dollar value. Studies (Vanguard's "Advisor's Alpha" research) estimate behavioral coaching is worth up to 1.5% in prevented mistakes — but only if you'd actually make those mistakes without them.
- Tax and planning advice: Did they help you figure out Roth vs. Traditional contributions? Walk you through a backdoor Roth? Optimize tax placement across accounts? Plan for Social Security? These are legitimate services.
- Financial plan: Did they help you build and maintain an actual financial plan with savings targets and retirement projections?

Honest question: over 6 years, has your advisor done any of those things? Or have they mostly maintained a portfolio of Vanguard funds that doesn't really need maintaining?

If the honest answer is mostly the latter, you're overpaying significantly.

---

THE ALTERNATIVE

You don't need to choose between "1% AUM advisor forever" and "complete DIY with no guidance." There's a middle path that Bogleheads consistently recommend:

**Option 1: Full DIY**
Open a brokerage account at Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab. Mirror your current Vanguard fund allocation. Rebalance once a year. Total cost: ~0.05% or less. Takes maybe 2-3 hours per year. The internet (bogleheads.org is genuinely excellent and free) can answer nearly any question that comes up.

**Option 2: Fee-only, advice-only advisor**
If you genuinely want a professional second opinion or help with a specific question (Social Security timing, Roth conversion strategy, estate planning), hire an advisor who charges a flat fee or hourly rate — not a percentage of your assets. NAPFA (napfa.org) and the Garrett Planning Network (garrettplanningnetwork.com) maintain directories. A one-time plan might cost $1,500–$5,000. You pay once, implement it yourself, and revisit as needed.

**Option 3: Vanguard Personal Advisor Services**
If you want ongoing human guidance but want to reduce costs, Vanguard's own advisory service charges ~0.30% AUM. It's still a fee, but it's less than a third of what you're paying now, and they won't steer you into unsuitable products because they use their own index funds.

---

BOTTOM LINE

You've made the hardest decisions correctly: you're in index funds, you've stayed invested for 6 years, you're not chasing performance. The advisor may have helped you build those habits — that's real. But going forward, the question is whether $3,500/year (rising as your portfolio grows) is worth continuing to pay for a service you may no longer need.

Run the math on what 0.95% less in annual fees does to your balance over 20 years. For most people, the answer makes the decision obvious.
