Your advisor has the asset location mostly backwards, and the reasoning she gave for both decisions is a common misconception that ends up costing real money over time. Let me walk through each account.

---

**Roth IRA: Bonds (BND) — this is the main problem**

The logic "safe assets belong in your retirement security account" sounds intuitive but gets the tax math exactly backwards. Here's the correct framing:

Roth IRA is the most valuable account you have — withdrawals are completely tax-free, including all the growth. That means you want the highest-expected-return asset sitting there, so the maximum amount of future growth never gets taxed. Stocks, not bonds.

Bonds belong in tax-deferred accounts (Traditional 401(k) or Traditional IRA) because bond interest is taxed as ordinary income at your full marginal rate. Shielding it in a tax-deferred account defers that tax. By contrast, stocks held in tax-advantaged accounts — and especially in Roth — grow without generating annual taxable income, and qualified dividends / long-term capital gains already receive preferential rates in taxable accounts anyway.

Putting BND in your Roth is the equivalent of filling a Ferrari with regular unleaded: you're wasting your best asset (tax-free compounding forever) on your lowest-expected-return holding. A 4% yielding bond fund in Roth grows tax-free at 4%. That same slot could be growing at 7–10% (historical equity average) tax-free.

The Bogleheads wiki flags this directly as one of the most common asset-location mistakes: "Bonds in Roth IRA while stocks sit in Traditional 401(k). Almost always backwards."

---

**Traditional 401(k): International Stocks (VXUS) — also suboptimal, for a different reason**

"That's where you have the most money" is not a tax-efficiency rationale — it's just a size observation. The asset location question is: what tax treatment does this account provide, and which fund benefits most from it?

Traditional 401(k) is tax-deferred, meaning you'll pay ordinary income tax on every dollar you pull out in retirement. Bonds are ideal here precisely because their interest would otherwise be taxed at ordinary rates anyway — you get a clean deferral of tax on income that was going to be taxed ordinarily either way.

International stocks have an additional consideration: the foreign tax credit (FTC). International equity funds pay foreign withholding tax on their dividends. If you hold VXUS in a taxable account, you can claim those foreign taxes as a credit on your US return — effectively recovering ~0.20% of the fund's value annually. If you hold VXUS in any tax-advantaged account (IRA, 401k, Roth), that credit is permanently lost. Not a disaster, but it's a real cost.

The standard Boglehead recommendation: bonds in tax-deferred (your Traditional 401k), international in taxable where you can capture the FTC, highest-growth assets (US stocks) in Roth.

---

**Taxable Brokerage: VTI — this part is correct**

VTI in taxable is exactly right. Total US stock market is among the most tax-efficient assets you can hold: low turnover means minimal capital gains distributions, most of the return is unrealized appreciation (not taxed until you sell), and qualified dividends receive preferential rates. This is the Boglehead-standard holding for taxable accounts.

---

**What the corrected placement looks like**

| Account | Current | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Roth IRA | BND (bonds) | VTI or US stocks — let equities compound tax-free |
| Traditional 401(k) | VXUS (international) | BND or bonds — shelter ordinary income |
| Taxable | VTI | VTI + potentially VXUS here for the foreign tax credit |

If you don't have enough taxable space to hold meaningful VXUS there, international in the Roth is the next-best placement (still tax-free growth). The worst location for VXUS is the Traditional 401(k), where all future growth — including the portion attributable to a credit you lose — gets taxed as ordinary income on the way out.

---

**One caveat before acting**

If there are large unrealized gains anywhere in taxable, don't sell to rebalance — the tax cost of moving usually outweighs the asset-location benefit. In that case, direct new contributions to the target locations and let the allocation drift toward optimal over time.

The funds themselves (VTI, VXUS, BND) are all excellent low-cost Vanguard index funds — that part of your setup is solid. This is purely a placement issue, not a fund-quality issue.

Asset location is a second-order optimization — getting your stock/bond ratio right matters more than perfect placement. But with three accounts of presumably meaningful size, getting this right is worth addressing, especially the Roth/bonds issue.
