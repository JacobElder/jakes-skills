## What's wrong and why

### Problem 1: Bonds (BND) in your Roth IRA

The logic of "safe assets belong in your retirement security account" is emotionally appealing but financially mistaken. It confuses psychological safety with tax efficiency.

The Roth IRA is the single most valuable account you have — every dollar of growth inside it comes out tax-free forever. That makes it the best possible home for your *highest-expected-return* assets, not your lowest. Stocks have higher expected long-run returns than bonds. Putting bonds in a Roth means you are sheltering your slowest-growing asset from taxes. The slow growth is already your "safety" — you don't need the Roth on top of it.

Meanwhile, BND currently yields around 4-5%. That interest income, if held in taxable, would be taxed as ordinary income every year. But in the Roth it's tax-free — which is fine, except that you've "spent" your Roth space on a relatively low-return asset when stocks could have been there growing at (historically) 7-10% annually, all tax-free.

The correct framing: **put your highest-expected-return assets in Roth so the maximum amount of compounding is sheltered from taxes.** Bonds in Roth is a waste of Roth space.

### Problem 2: International stocks (VXUS) in your Traditional 401(k)

The reasoning "that's where you have the most money" is not a tax-efficiency argument — it's just saying "we put it somewhere it fits." That's not a rationale for placement.

Here's the issue specific to international funds: international stock funds pay foreign withholding tax on their dividends. When you hold an international fund in a **taxable brokerage account**, the IRS lets you claim a **foreign tax credit** to recover most of that foreign tax. You get that money back.

When you hold the international fund in a 401(k) or IRA — any tax-advantaged account — the foreign tax credit disappears. You still paid the foreign taxes (the fund paid them), but you cannot claim the credit. That's roughly 0.20% of the fund's value per year, quietly lost. Over decades on a meaningful position, that adds up.

Your advisor's logic didn't account for this at all.

### What's actually right: VTI in taxable

Ironically, the one thing that is correctly placed is the VTI in your taxable brokerage. US stock index funds are tax-efficient in taxable accounts because:
- Most of the return is unrealized capital appreciation (not taxed until you sell)
- Qualified dividends are taxed at the preferential LTCG rate (0%, 15%, or 20%), not ordinary income rates
- VTI/VTSAX have very low portfolio turnover, so almost no capital gains distributions

So taxable + VTI is correct. That part stays.

## The correct placement for a three-fund portfolio

Given your three accounts — Roth IRA, Traditional 401(k), and taxable brokerage — the standard Boglehead arrangement is:

| Account | What belongs there | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Traditional 401(k)** | Bonds (BND) | Bond interest is ordinary income; shelter it in tax-deferred. You pay tax at withdrawal, but you've deferred it and gotten the compounding in the meantime. |
| **Roth IRA** | US stocks (VTI) | Highest expected return, never taxed. Don't waste Roth space on bonds. |
| **Taxable brokerage** | International stocks (VXUS) + US stocks (VTI) | Capture the foreign tax credit on VXUS. VTI is also tax-efficient here. |

In practice, you may not have enough space in the Roth to hold all your desired US stock allocation — in that case, overflow into taxable with VTI, and hold the international in taxable as well. The key principles are: bonds out of Roth, international into taxable.

## On your advisor's reasoning

To be direct: the phrases "safe assets belong in your retirement security account" and "that's where you have the most money" are not tax-efficiency arguments. They're intuitive-sounding framing that doesn't engage with how the tax code actually treats different asset classes in different accounts.

A good advisor should know:
1. That Roth's primary advantage is tax-free *growth*, not asset safety
2. That international funds have a specific taxable-account benefit (the foreign tax credit) that is lost in a 401(k)

This doesn't mean your advisor is acting in bad faith — many advisors haven't been trained to think about asset location carefully, and the intuition of "safe stuff in the retirement account" does sound reasonable on the surface. But the outcome is real money left on the table.

## What to do now

A few practical considerations before you make any changes:

1. **Check whether you can swap within your accounts without tax consequences.** In your Roth IRA and Traditional 401(k), you can sell and rebuy freely — no capital gains, no tax event. Make the swap there.

2. **In taxable, be careful.** If your VTI in taxable has significant unrealized gains, don't sell it just to "fix" placement. Hold VTI there (it's fine in taxable) and redirect future contributions toward adding VXUS in taxable over time.

3. **Check what funds are available in your 401(k).** Not every 401(k) plan offers a good bond fund. If your plan's bond options all have high expense ratios (above 0.20%), you may need to hold bonds in your IRA instead and work around the constraint. The least-bad option in a bad plan is still the least-bad option — but this is worth checking.

4. **The asset allocation itself — your overall stock/bond split — matters more than perfect placement.** If you haven't already decided on a target allocation (e.g., 80/20 stocks/bonds, with 20-30% of equities in international), that's worth thinking through before optimizing the location.

This is worth getting right, especially as the balances grow — the tax-efficiency difference compounds over time.
