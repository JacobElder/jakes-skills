## Here's your prioritized waterfall — do these in order

### 1. Capture the full 401(k) employer match (do this first, today)

Before anything else — before extra debt payments, before your IRA, before anything — find out what your employer's 401(k) match is and contribute at least enough to capture every dollar of it.

A typical match is something like "50% of contributions up to 6% of salary." On a $75,000 salary, contributing 6% ($4,500/year) earns a $2,250 employer match. That's an immediate 50% return before the money is even invested. Nothing else in your financial life comes close to that. Not paying off 5.5% student loans. Not any index fund. A 50–100% guaranteed return is free money — take it unconditionally.

If you don't know your match, look at your plan documents or ask HR this week. Set your 401(k) contribution percentage accordingly.

**What to invest in inside the 401(k):** Look for a low-cost total stock market index fund or S&P 500 index fund (expense ratio ideally under 0.10%). If your plan has a target-date fund (e.g., "Target Retirement 2065"), that's a perfectly reasonable one-fund choice — it holds US stocks, international stocks, and bonds in an age-appropriate mix. Check the expense ratio; anything under 0.20% is fine. Avoid any fund with an expense ratio above 0.50% — that's a meaningful drag over decades. On a $500k portfolio at age 50, a 1% expense ratio costs you $5,000 per year, compounding against you.

At 26, you're investing for 35+ years. Lean heavily (90–100%) toward stocks. Bonds are for people closer to retirement.

---

### 2. Your student loans: probably don't race to pay them off

5.5% is in the "gray zone." The Boglehead soft cutoff is roughly 6–7%: above that, paying off debt is almost always the right move because it's a guaranteed risk-free return. Below ~4–5%, most Bogleheads invest instead, since the expected long-run stock market return (~7% real, ~10% nominal) beats the guaranteed debt payoff.

At 5.5%, you're right in the debate zone. The community is split here. Reasonable people make both choices. Here's the honest take:

- **If paying off the loans feels right psychologically** — if the debt bothers you — there's nothing wrong with aggressively paying it down. A guaranteed 5.5% return is solid.
- **If you're comfortable with the debt**, the math slightly favors continuing minimum payments and investing in tax-advantaged accounts, where the tax benefits (Roth IRA, 401k) tilt the comparison further toward investing.

The one clear answer: do NOT race to pay down 5.5% student loans before capturing your employer match. The match almost certainly beats it.

Given the $8,000 balance is relatively small, a practical middle path: after capturing the full match and opening your Roth IRA, put $300–500/month toward the loans. At that pace, you'll be debt-free in 12–18 months without meaningfully derailing your investment timeline.

### 3. Open a Roth IRA and max it (after capturing the match)

At $75,000 income, you're well under the income limits for direct Roth IRA contributions ($146,000 phaseout for single filers in 2024). Open one at Fidelity, Vanguard, or Schwab. All three are excellent. The 2025 contribution limit is $7,000/year.

**Why Roth IRA over Traditional IRA at 26?**

This is one of the legitimate debate topics in the Boglehead community (Roth vs. Traditional), but at your income level and age, Roth is the right default. Here's why:

- You're likely at or near the lowest marginal tax rate you'll ever be. Contributing Roth now means you're paying taxes at your current rate (probably 22% federal on income above ~$47k single) and never paying taxes on decades of growth.
- Roth accounts have no required minimum distributions, so the money can compound for your entire lifetime.
- Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn any time without penalty — it's more flexible than a Traditional IRA as a backstop if your financial situation changes.

**What to put in the Roth IRA:**
- At Fidelity: FSKAX (total US market, 0.015% ER) + FTIHX (total international, 0.06% ER), or FZROX/FZILX (0% expense ratio). Note: FZROX and FZILX are fine *inside* the IRA — they're zero-cost and excellent there — but don't use them in a taxable account. They're Fidelity-proprietary and can't be transferred without a taxable sale event.
- At Vanguard: VTI (total US market ETF, 0.03% ER) + VXUS (total international, 0.07% ER), or VTSAX/VTIAX in mutual fund form. Note: VTI and VTSAX are the same underlying fund in different share classes — no meaningful difference at Vanguard.
- At Schwab: SWTSX (total US, 0.03% ER). For international, note that SWISX is EAFE-only and excludes emerging markets — pair it with SCHE if you want full global coverage, or just use a Vanguard or Fidelity international fund.

A simple split for your Roth IRA: 75–80% US total market, 20–25% total international. Or use VT (the total world stock market in one fund) and skip the decision entirely. Both are solidly Boglehead. How much international to hold is a genuine point of debate in the community — Bogle himself leaned toward US-only late in his career; Vanguard's target-date funds hold ~40% international within equities. Somewhere in the 20–30% range is the common compromise.

### 4. Check your health insurance situation: are you HSA-eligible?

If your employer's health insurance is a High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP), you're eligible for a Health Savings Account (HSA). If so, the HSA goes *above* the Roth IRA in priority — right after the 401(k) match — because it's the most tax-advantaged account that exists.

The HSA is triple-tax-advantaged:
1. Contributions are pre-tax (or above-the-line deductible)
2. Growth is tax-free
3. Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free

The Boglehead move: contribute to the HSA, invest it in index funds (most HSAs let you do this once the balance exceeds $1,000–$2,000), pay current medical expenses from your regular checking account, and keep every receipt. You can reimburse yourself tax-free from the HSA at any point in the future — even 30 years from now — with no time limit. After age 65, it functions like a Traditional IRA for non-medical expenses. So worst case it's a bonus Traditional IRA; best case it's tax-free forever.

2025 HSA limit: $4,300 for individual coverage. If you're on an HDHP, check the box.

### 5. Back to the 401(k): max it (eventually)

Once your match is captured, Roth IRA is funded, HSA is funded (if eligible), and student loans are knocked out, the next use of dollars is maxing the 401(k) up to the full $23,500 employee contribution limit (2025).

At $75,000 salary, you probably can't do all of this simultaneously right now. That's fine. The waterfall is a priority list, not a checklist you complete on day one. The order matters more than the speed.

On Roth 401(k) vs. Traditional 401(k) beyond the match: at $75k, Traditional contributions reduce your taxable income now (saving you ~22 cents per dollar). Roth 401(k) doesn't. Many Bogleheads at your income level split: Traditional 401(k) for the current tax deduction, Roth IRA for tax-free growth. That's a reasonable approach. Either is defensible.

### 6. Complete your full emergency fund

Once the higher-priority items are handled, build your emergency fund to 3–6 months of essential expenses. If your monthly essentials (rent, utilities, food, minimum debt payments) run $2,500–$3,000, you want $7,500–$18,000 in a high-yield savings account.

Your $2,000 in savings covers the starter threshold but leaves you exposed to a large unexpected expense (job loss, medical bill, car repair). As you work through steps 1–5, also be slowly building this buffer in a HYSA earning 4–5% APY. Don't rush this — you don't need to complete it before starting the 401(k) match or the Roth IRA — but don't ignore it either.

## The actual portfolio: keep it simple

You don't need anything complicated. At 26 with a 35+ year horizon:

- **401(k):** 90–100% in a low-cost total stock market index fund or target-date index fund. Whatever the cheapest, broadest option is in your plan.
- **Roth IRA:** Two-fund portfolio — ~75–80% US total market (FSKAX, VTI, or equivalent) + ~20–25% total international (FTIHX, VXUS, or equivalent). Or just VT.
- **Emergency fund:** High-yield savings account — currently paying ~4–5% APY. Not an investment; it's insurance.

That's it. "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack."

No individual stocks. No thematic ETFs. No actively managed funds paying 1%+ in annual fees. No dividend-chasing strategies (SCHD, covered-call ETFs, etc.). Dividends are not free money — a stock that pays a $1 dividend has its share price drop by ~$1 on the ex-dividend date. Total return is what matters, and you get all of it in VTI.

## One thing to avoid as you build wealth

As you start earning real money and people notice, you may encounter a financial advisor — particularly from firms like Northwestern Mutual, New York Life, Edward Jones, or a bank — who recommends a product alongside investing. Often this is whole life insurance or an indexed universal life (IUL) policy, framed as "tax-advantaged growth," "permanent insurance," or "a retirement vehicle."

Almost certainly, this is not the right product for you. For the overwhelming majority of people, the right combination is: term life insurance (cheap, straightforward, only necessary if you have dependents who rely on your income) plus index fund investing in the accounts described above. The commissions on whole life products are enormous — often 50–100% of the first year's premium — which explains why they're frequently recommended by commission-based advisors. This doesn't make them bad people, but it's a real financial incentive worth knowing about.

If you encounter this recommendation, the right response is: "Let me think about it." Then look up the Boglehead position before you decide anything.
