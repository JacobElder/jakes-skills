The "living off dividends" idea is intuitive and emotionally appealing — you're getting paid without touching your shares — but it's built on a misunderstanding of how dividends work, and the specific funds you've chosen compound that problem. Let me unpack this honestly.

---

**Dividends are not free money**

This is the core issue. When a stock or fund pays a $1 dividend, the share price drops by $1 on the ex-dividend date. The market doesn't care about the label "dividend" — it's just a forced liquidation of a portion of your holdings. Your total wealth (shares x price + cash) is identical before and after the dividend is paid.

This means "living off dividends without selling shares" is financially equivalent to "living off proceeds from selling a small number of shares." The mechanics differ; the economics are identical. There is no magic in the dividend that makes it different from a controlled withdrawal.

The distinction that actually matters is *total return* — price appreciation plus dividends together. A fund that pays a 4% dividend yield and grows 4% has the same total return as a fund that pays 0% dividends and grows 8%. Chasing the dividend label leads you away from total return thinking, which is the right framework.

---

**SCHD: decent fund, wrong reason to own it**

SCHD (Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF) has a 0.06% expense ratio and a reasonable track record. It's not a terrible fund. But it's roughly 85-90% correlated with the S&P 500, and what you're really getting is a value tilt — it selects companies that pay dividends, which skews toward mature, lower-growth companies.

The problem: VTI (total US stock market) already holds all of SCHD's holdings, plus the high-growth companies that don't pay dividends (think earlier-stage tech). Those non-dividend payers have historically driven enormous returns. When you switch from VTI to SCHD, you're not adding anything — you're *removing* a chunk of the market because it doesn't happen to pay dividends today.

There's also a tax inefficiency. Dividends are taxable in the year they're paid, even if you don't need the income. Unrealized capital gains compound tax-free until you sell. By owning a dividend-heavy fund in a taxable account, you're paying taxes on "income" you don't need, on an accelerated schedule, for no benefit you couldn't get with a tax-efficient total market fund.

---

**JEPI: this one is more concerning**

JEPI (JPMorgan Equity Premium Income ETF) is a covered-call fund. Here's what it actually does: it holds a portfolio of stocks and then sells call options against those positions to generate premium income, which it pays out as distributions. That income is largely taxed as *ordinary income*, not qualified dividends — a worse tax outcome in taxable accounts.

More importantly, selling covered calls structurally caps your upside. When the market runs up, you've contractually given away the gains above the strike price. In bull markets, JEPI consistently underperforms a basic S&P 500 or total market fund. The "high yield" is real, but it comes at the cost of long-term growth — you're trading future appreciation for current income.

For a retiree with a 20-30 year retirement horizon, capping your portfolio's upside in exchange for current income is a bad trade. Longevity risk — the risk of outliving your money — is far more dangerous than sequence-of-returns risk for most people. You need growth.

---

**The better mental model for retirement withdrawals**

The Boglehead approach to retirement income is not "own funds that pay dividends so I never have to sell." It's: own a diversified, low-cost portfolio (total market stocks + bonds) and withdraw what you need by selling shares systematically.

Selling shares to fund expenses is not scary. If you hold VTI and need to withdraw 4% this year, you sell 4% worth of shares. If those shares have appreciated, the gain is taxed at long-term capital gains rates — currently 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income. That is *more tax-efficient* than receiving ordinary income dividends from JEPI at your marginal income tax rate.

The 4% rule (or a more conservative 3-3.5% for early retirees) is the well-researched framework here. It says: if you withdraw 4% of your initial portfolio annually (adjusted for inflation), a diversified stock/bond portfolio has historically sustained 30-year retirements with very high success rates. That doesn't require dividends. It just requires a sensible asset allocation and the discipline to stay the course.

---

**What I'd suggest instead**

The Boglehead default for retirement:

- **US Total Stock Market** (VTI, VTSAX): the full market, not a filtered slice
- **Total International Stock** (VXUS, VTIAX): 20-40% of equity allocation, depending on your comfort with international
- **Total Bond Market** (BND, VBTLX): your allocation here depends on your time horizon and risk tolerance

Bond allocation in retirement is a genuine debate in the community — Bogle himself suggested 50/50 for many retirees in his later years; others use age-10 or age-20 as a rough formula. What you need isn't dividend yield — it's a stock/bond mix that lets you sleep at night during a 40% equity drawdown without panic-selling.

If you're near retirement and want simplicity: a Vanguard target-date retirement fund is a legitimate, perfectly Boglehead answer. It automatically adjusts the allocation over time and costs around 0.10%.

---

**The short version**

SCHD is a fine fund owned for the wrong reasons. JEPI is a fund that sounds appealing to income-seekers but structurally underperforms for long-term wealth building. The "living off dividends without selling" concept is psychologically comforting but mathematically equivalent to controlled share sales — and less tax-efficient in practice.

Total return in a low-cost, diversified portfolio, withdrawn systematically at a sustainable rate, is the approach that has the research behind it. "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack."
