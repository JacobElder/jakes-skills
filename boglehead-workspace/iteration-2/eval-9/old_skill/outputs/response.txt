The short answer: your instinct to spread things out is understandable, but three years is too long. A 36-month DCA schedule isn't really dollar-cost averaging — it's holding a large cash position while hoping the market cooperates. The evidence says that's likely to cost you money.

---

WHAT THE RESEARCH ACTUALLY SHOWS

Vanguard studied exactly this question: lump sum vs. dollar-cost averaging on a windfall. The result: lump sum beats DCA about two-thirds of the time, across US, UK, and Australian markets, for 10-year holding periods. The average advantage of lump sum was about 2.3% of portfolio value.

Why? Because markets go up more often than they go down. The long-run expected return on a diversified equity portfolio is positive. Every month you're sitting in cash, you're collecting money market rates while forgoing the expected equity premium. You're making a bet that the next 36 months will be one of the one-third of scenarios where waiting paid off. That's a coin flip weighted against you.

Peter Lynch put it plainly: "Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections than has been lost in corrections themselves."

---

ADDRESSING THE CORE FEAR: "WHAT IF THE MARKET CRASHES RIGHT AFTER?"

This is the real fear driving your plan, so let's name it directly: you're worried about deploying a large sum right before a downturn. It's a real psychological concern. It is not, however, a reliable investment strategy.

Even if you're unlucky and the market drops 30% the week after you invest:

1. You haven't lost anything you wouldn't have eventually lost by staying in cash. If a 30% correction was coming, spreading over 3 years just means you invest the last tranche into the crash anyway.

2. Recoveries don't wait for you. The best days in the market cluster near the worst days. Missing the 10 best trading days in a decade typically cuts your return roughly in half. If you're deployed over 3 years, you miss some of those recovery days.

3. The risk runs both directions. If the market gains 20% in the next year (entirely plausible historically), you've permanently missed those returns on $120k+ that was sitting in a savings account.

The deeper issue: if a 30% drop on $175,000 — a $52,500 paper loss — would cause you to panic and sell, that's a signal to revisit your asset allocation, not to DCA for 3 years. A portfolio that genuinely matches your risk tolerance should be survivable through a 50% equity drawdown without you reaching for the sell button. If yours isn't, fix the allocation first.

---

THE BOGLEHEAD-SANCTIONED COMPROMISE

This is actually one of the topics the Boglehead community acknowledges as a genuine area of disagreement. The wiki's position:

- Lump sum is mathematically optimal most of the time.
- DCA over 3–12 months is a legitimate behavioral compromise — if it's the difference between investing and sitting in cash indefinitely, a shorter DCA window is far better than paralysis.

The key word is months, not years. A 6-month DCA schedule ($29,000/month) lets you participate meaningfully in the market while easing the psychological shock of a large one-time commitment. A 12-month schedule ($14,600/month) is the outer edge of what most Bogleheads would call reasonable.

Your 36-month plan at $5,000/month? That's not really DCA — that's a 3-year bet against the market going up, dressed in risk-management clothing. You'd have over $100,000 sitting in cash for the better part of two years.

BEFORE INVESTING: CHECK THE WATERFALL

With $175,000, there are higher-priority uses to check before sending it all to the brokerage. Run through this quickly:

1. Emergency fund: Do you have 3–6 months of expenses in a high-yield savings account? If not, carve that out first — it stays in cash forever, regardless of markets. HYSA currently paying 4–5% (Ally, Marcus, Fidelity SPAXX, etc.).

2. High-interest debt: Anything above ~7% (credit cards, personal loans) gets paid off before investing in taxable. Guaranteed return that beats the market.

3. IRA contribution for this year: $7,000 limit for 2025. If you haven't contributed yet, do it now. If you're above the direct Roth income limits, look at backdoor Roth.

4. HSA if applicable: If you have a qualifying high-deductible health plan, the HSA is triple-tax-advantaged — the best account structure that exists. $4,300 single / $8,550 family in 2025.

5. Max your 401(k): $23,500 in 2025. An inheritance is a great opportunity to temporarily redirect your paycheck to max the 401(k) while using the lump sum for living expenses, or to simply contribute directly if your plan allows after-tax contributions.

Only after those boxes are checked does the remainder go into taxable brokerage.

FOR THE BULK OF THE MONEY: THREE PRACTICAL OPTIONS

You already have a three-fund portfolio — stick with it. This inheritance doesn't require a new strategy, just more of the same. Keep the same allocation you've established; just buy proportionally more.

Option A (mathematically better): Deploy the taxable portion all at once into your existing three-fund allocation.

Option B (psychologically sustainable): Deploy over 6 months, roughly $29,000/month. Automate the purchases so you're not making a decision each time.

Option C (last resort): 12 months maximum, $14,600/month. Commit to this schedule in writing before you start, and do not extend it if the market goes up and you convince yourself to "wait for a better entry point."

While the money waits in whatever DCA queue you choose, park it in a money market fund or HYSA — not a checking account. VMFXX, SNSXX, or a Treasury money market will earn you something while you wait.

---

THE REAL PROTECTION AGAINST CRASH RISK

Market timing (including "slow" market timing via extended DCA) isn't the tool for managing crash anxiety. The actual tools are:

- A stock/bond allocation calibrated to what you can hold through a 50% drawdown without selling. If you'd panic at 40% stocks in the red, you probably shouldn't be 100% equities — and that's a portfolio construction question, not a timing question.

- A full emergency fund in cash, so you're never forced to sell investments at the worst moment because you need the money.

- A written investment policy statement — even just a note to yourself — describing your allocation and your commitment to stay the course. Read it before you act during a market drop.

Those are the real defenses. A 3-year drip schedule provides the illusion of protection while actually costing you expected return.

Bogle: "The two greatest enemies of the equity fund investor are expenses and emotions." You've already solved expenses with the three-fund portfolio. This is the emotions part.
