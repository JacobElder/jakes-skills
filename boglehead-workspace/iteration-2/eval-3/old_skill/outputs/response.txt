This is one of the most common questions in investing, and the Boglehead answer is direct: invest the money now, or if you genuinely can't stomach it, spread it over the next 6-12 months. Do not sit in cash waiting for a 20-30% correction that may or may not arrive.

Here's why.

---

**"The market is at all-time highs" is not a warning signal**

This framing sounds alarming, but it's statistically normal. The market spends the majority of its time at or near all-time highs — that's what a market that trends upward over time looks like. Vanguard has done the research: since 1926, roughly 30% of all monthly observations have been all-time highs. If "all-time high" predicted crashes, you'd have lost a lot of money being out of the market for the past century.

What you're really saying is that the market has gone up lately and might go down. That's true. It might also go up another 20% before any correction arrives.

---

**"Everyone says a crash is coming" is not a timing signal**

When have people *not* said a crash is coming? This is the perennial condition of financial media. In 2012, it was the European debt crisis. In 2015, it was China's slowdown. In 2017, it was CAPE ratios at elevated levels. In 2019, it was the inverted yield curve. After every one of those "obvious" signals, markets continued higher for a while, eventually corrected, then recovered and went higher still.

Peter Lynch put it well: "Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections than has been lost in corrections themselves."

The people who said "I'll wait for a better entry point" after the 2017 run-up missed two more years of gains before the 2020 COVID crash — which itself recovered completely within about five months.

---

**The math of waiting is brutal**

Let's say a 20% correction does come, and let's say you time it perfectly — you buy exactly at the bottom. How much does that help compared to investing today?

If you invest $60,000 now and it drops 20%, you have $48,000. But if the market is currently at $X and you waited in cash earning 5% HYSA, you'd need the drop to come quickly and you'd need to identify the bottom — which nobody can reliably do. Most people who wait for "the crash" see prices drop 10%, get scared it'll drop more, wait some more, then watch the recovery happen without them.

Here's the harder problem: even if a crash comes, you probably won't buy at the bottom. You'll watch it fall and think "maybe it goes lower." The emotional experience of a falling market makes most people want to wait *longer*, not invest. The crash scenario you're imagining — prices fall 25%, you step in confidently, market recovers — almost never plays out that way in real life.

Vanguard's research comparing lump-sum investing versus dollar-cost averaging found that lump sum outperforms roughly two-thirds of the time. Waiting in cash for an indefinite correction performs worse than both.

---

**So what should you actually do?**

You have two reasonable options:

**Option 1: Invest the full $60,000 now (lump sum)**
This is the highest expected-return choice. Set your asset allocation, buy your funds, and don't look at it for six months. If you have a long time horizon (10+ years), a 20-30% drawdown somewhere along the way is not a catastrophe — it's a normal feature of the journey. The market has recovered from every single drawdown in history.

**Option 2: Dollar-cost average over 6-12 months (behavioral compromise)**
If you know you'll panic-sell the moment the portfolio drops $10,000, then lump sum is the wrong choice for *you*, not because of the market, but because of your behavior risk. In that case, put $5,000-$10,000/month into your target funds over 6-12 months. This doesn't optimize expected return, but it manages the emotional experience of investing at what feels like an uncertain time. The tradeoff is real — you'll probably have lower returns than lump sum — but it's worth it if it keeps you invested.

What is *not* on the menu: sitting in cash waiting for a specific percentage drawdown before you act. That's market timing, and it has a terrible track record.

---

**One thing worth making sure of first**

Before any of this: do you have an emergency fund (3-6 months of expenses) already set aside in a high-yield savings account? And is this $60,000 genuinely money you don't need for 5+ years? If there's any chance you'd need this cash within the next 2-3 years, the stock market isn't the right place for it regardless of timing — not because of valuations, but because short time horizons mean drawdown risk is real and recovery time isn't guaranteed.

If those boxes are checked, your allocation question (how much in stocks vs. bonds, how much international) matters more than your timing question. A well-chosen asset allocation is something you can stay in through a crash. An overaggressive one is what causes people to sell at the bottom and lock in losses.

---

**The bottom line**

The market being at all-time highs is not a reason to wait. "Everyone saying a crash is coming" is not a timing indicator. Invest the $60,000 according to your target allocation — lump sum if you can handle the volatility, spread over 6-12 months if you genuinely need the behavioral smoothing. Then stay the course.

Bogle's instruction was simple: "Stay the course." The plan you make when things feel uncertain is the plan that will actually protect you.
