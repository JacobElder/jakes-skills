This is a genuinely mixed situation, and it's worth unpacking carefully — because the fund selection is good, but the fee structure is a problem regardless.

**The honest answer: no, 1% AUM is not a good deal — even when the underlying funds are excellent.**

Here's why, with the math.

---

**What 1% AUM actually costs you**

1% of $350,000 is $3,500 per year. That number grows as your portfolio grows. At $500,000, it's $5,000/year. At $1,000,000, it's $10,000/year. The fee scales with your wealth, but the work the advisor does doesn't.

Over time, the compounding effect is severe. $3,500/year that you're paying in fees, at a 7% annual return over 30 years, represents roughly $330,000 in lost wealth. A 1% AUM fee on a portfolio that would otherwise grow to, say, $2.6 million will instead grow to roughly $2.1 million — the difference being the cumulative drag of the fee, compounding against you instead of for you.

Bogle put it directly: "In investing, you get what you don't pay for." That's not a metaphor — it's an arithmetic fact.

---

**The specific problem with your situation**

Here's the thing that makes your situation particularly worth reconsidering: if your portfolio is already in low-cost Vanguard index funds — VTI, VXUS, BND, or similar — you are paying $3,500/year for a service you could replicate yourself in an afternoon.

A three-fund portfolio in Vanguard index funds has an expense ratio of roughly 0.03–0.05%. The ongoing work it requires is: rebalance once a year, contribute regularly, don't panic during crashes. That's it. You're paying your advisor $3,500/year to do roughly that.

The advisor's value is real at the beginning — they helped you establish a sensible portfolio, presumably talked you out of bad decisions, and gave you a framework. But you've been doing this for six years. The initial setup value has been amortized away. What are you getting for $3,500 now?

---

**What 1% AUM advisors actually tend to deliver**

Studies on the "advisor alpha" concept generally find that the value of a financial advisor — to the extent it exists — comes from:

1. Behavioral coaching: stopping you from selling in a crash, keeping you invested.
2. Tax optimization: tax-loss harvesting, asset location, Roth conversion ladders.
3. Planning: estate planning, withdrawal sequencing, Social Security optimization.

If your advisor is actively doing items 2 and 3 with you — real tax-loss harvesting, intentional asset location across accounts, annual reviews that produce specific decisions — then there's a case to be made that the fee isn't pure cost. But honestly, at $350,000, these services are available for a flat fee of $1,500–$3,000 from a fee-only fiduciary advisor. You pay once, get the plan, and execute it yourself.

If the relationship mostly consists of quarterly statements and an annual check-in call, you're paying for very little.

---

**The Boglehead community's clear position on 1% AUM advisors**

This is one of the specific cases called out explicitly in Boglehead circles: an AUM advisor charging 1%+ for a portfolio of low-cost index funds is one of the clearest examples of a fee that doesn't match the delivered value. The anti-pattern here isn't that your advisor is a bad person — they may have genuinely helped you build good habits. The issue is structural: the AUM model charges more as you accumulate wealth, even though managing a $1M index portfolio is no harder than managing a $350k index portfolio.

The AUM advisor business model creates a real misalignment. The advisor earns more when your portfolio grows, which sounds aligned — but they earn the same whether you make one phone call a year or twenty, and whether they actually optimize your taxes or not.

---

**What to consider doing**

A few concrete paths, in order of how well they fit the Boglehead approach:

**Option 1: DIY.** If you're comfortable with the three-fund portfolio already, you have everything you need. Transfer the account to Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab, maintain the same allocation, and rebalance once a year. Total cost: ~0.04% instead of 1%. The difference compounds significantly.

**Option 2: One-time fee-only review.** Before going fully DIY, hire a fee-only fiduciary advisor (NAPFA directory or the Garrett Planning Network) for a one-time engagement. Pay $1,500–$3,000 for a comprehensive plan: asset location, Roth conversion analysis, tax optimization, withdrawal sequencing. Then execute it yourself.

**Option 3: Vanguard Personal Advisor Services.** If you genuinely want ongoing human advice and can't stomach full DIY, Vanguard PAS charges ~0.30% AUM and uses Vanguard funds — same funds you probably already own. That would cut your annual fee from ~$3,500 to ~$1,050, saving ~$2,450/year. Not as good as DIY, but a major improvement over 1%.

**Option 4: Stay but renegotiate.** At $350,000, you have leverage. AUM fees are negotiable. Some advisors will go to 0.75% or lower for larger accounts, or switch you to a flat-fee retainer ($2,000–$3,500/year). If you like your advisor personally and they do provide real ongoing value, this is worth asking for.

---

**The one thing you should do before deciding**

Ask your advisor, in writing: "Can you walk me through the specific tax optimizations, asset location decisions, and planning work you've done on my account in the last 12 months?" The answer will tell you exactly what you're paying for. If the answer is detailed and specific — real work that saved you real money — the fee might be justifiable. If the answer is vague or centers on "portfolio oversight," that's the answer.

---

**Bottom line**

The portfolio itself sounds excellent — low-cost Vanguard index funds are exactly right. The fee is the problem. A 1% AUM charge on a passively managed index portfolio is paying active-management-tier fees for passive-management work. Over time, on your current trajectory, that difference will likely amount to hundreds of thousands of dollars in foregone wealth.

The Boglehead default in this situation is clear: either go DIY (three-fund portfolio, handle your own rebalancing), hire a fee-only advisor for a one-time plan and then self-manage, or at minimum reduce your AUM fee to 0.30% or a flat retainer. Paying 1% indefinitely for a portfolio you could manage yourself is one of the most expensive habits in personal finance.

Note: this isn't personalized financial advice — your full situation (tax bracket, other accounts, estate considerations) matters. If you want a second opinion on the full picture, a NAPFA fee-only fiduciary is the right resource.
