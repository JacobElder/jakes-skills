The 5-year track record is real. The conclusion you're drawing from it is wrong.

Here's why, and here's the math.

---

**The cost gap is enormous — and permanent**

AGTHX charges 0.63% per year. The Vanguard 500 Index charges 0.03%. That's a 0.60% annual drag that AGTHX has to overcome every single year just to break even on costs. Your $120,000 is not small change here:

- At $120,000, you're paying roughly $756/year in AGTHX expenses versus $36/year in the index fund — a $720 annual difference.
- Over 8 years you've already paid something like $5,500–$6,000 more in expenses than you would have in the index fund (rough estimate; the compounding effect is larger because your balance has grown).
- Over a 30-year horizon, assuming 7% annual growth and that 0.60% drag compounds against you: a $120,000 portfolio would grow to roughly $913,000 in the index fund vs. roughly $830,000 in the higher-cost fund if returns are otherwise *identical*. That's an $83,000 difference from costs alone — before we even discuss whether the outperformance continues.

Bogle's line is apt here: "In investing, you get what you don't pay for."

---

**On the 1.5%/year outperformance over 5 years**

AGTHX is a large-cap growth fund run by Capital Group. It is genuinely one of the better-run active funds in existence — no one is disputing that it has a decent track record. But here's what that 5-year number doesn't tell you:

**1. The look-back period is short and cherry-picked by recency.**
Five years ending in a recent growth bull market is exactly when large-cap growth funds with big tech overweights tend to shine. The relevant question isn't "did it beat the index the past 5 years?" It's "will it beat the index for the next 20–30 years?" The SPIVA scorecards (S&P Dow Jones Indices) are unambiguous: over 15-year periods, roughly 85–90% of active large-cap US funds underperform their benchmark after costs. AGTHX itself has had stretches of underperformance — it lagged badly in 2020–2021 relative to its peak reputation periods, and meaningfully underperformed in years like 2014–2016.

**2. Past outperformance does not predict future outperformance.**
This is the most important point. Academic research on mutual fund persistence (Carhart 1997, Fama/French, and many studies since) consistently finds that past outperformers are no more likely than chance to continue outperforming after costs. The rare funds that do persist tend to see their edge arbitraged away as assets flow in and positions become harder to execute. AGTHX already manages over $150 billion — that size is a significant headwind on nimble stock selection.

**3. The outperformance, net of costs, may be less impressive than it looks.**
If AGTHX beat the S&P 500 by 1.5%/year gross but charged 0.63% vs. the index fund's 0.03%, the real cost-adjusted margin is more like 0.9%/year. A 5-year run of 0.9% net alpha is not nothing — but it's also not extraordinary given the volatility of annual returns. One bad year wipes out years of modest outperformance.

**4. The comparison benchmark matters.**
AGTHX is a large-cap growth fund, not a blend fund. Over the past 5 years, growth has massively outperformed value. A more apples-to-apples comparison would be AGTHX vs. a large-cap growth index (like IVW or SCHG), not the S&P 500 blend. It's possible that growth as a style beat the S&P 500 by 1.5%/year and AGTHX was simply riding that wave rather than demonstrating manager skill.

---

**On "diversifying management styles"**

This is a framing that sounds sophisticated but doesn't hold up.

Diversification across asset classes — US stocks, international stocks, bonds — is real and valuable because these assets have genuinely different risk/return drivers. Diversifying between an active fund and an index fund that holds largely the same underlying large-cap US stocks is not the same thing. You're not adding a different asset class; you're adding a different *wrapper* around a very similar collection of stocks, plus paying extra for it.

"Active vs. passive" is not a risk factor that earns a return premium the way, say, small-cap value or emerging markets exposure does. You're not being compensated for holding active risk — you're just paying for it and hoping.

The correct diversification question here is whether your entire 401(k) is in US large-cap stocks (which both AGTHX and the Vanguard 500 Index are). If so, the real diversification gap is the absence of international equity and bonds — not a split between two funds that both own Apple, Microsoft, and Amazon.

---

**What a Boglehead would actually recommend**

Switch to the Vanguard 500 Index (or better, if your plan offers it, a Vanguard Total Stock Market Index fund). Then check whether your plan also offers:

- A low-cost international index fund (VXUS equivalent, or a Vanguard Total International)
- A low-cost bond index fund (BND equivalent)

If you have those three options, you can build a complete three-fund portfolio inside your 401(k) for something close to 0.03–0.05% in total annual costs. That's the move.

If your time horizon is still long (say, 20+ years to retirement), you likely want a mostly-stock allocation. A simple implementation: 60-80% US index, 20-30% international index, 10-20% bond index. Adjust based on your risk tolerance. Or if your plan offers a Vanguard Target Retirement fund, that's a perfectly fine one-decision answer.

---

**The bottom line**

You're not switching away from a winner. You're switching away from a fund with a 5-year hot streak, a 0.63% fee, $150B+ in assets, a benchmark that it may have beaten partly because growth outperformed blend, and an 85%+ historical chance (based on the full universe of active funds) of underperforming over the next 15 years. The index fund doesn't need to "perform" — it guarantees you capture whatever the market returns, minus almost nothing in costs.

The question isn't "why would I switch to the underperforming fund?" The index fund is not underperforming. Over sufficiently long periods, the index fund *is* the performance that most active managers fail to match.
