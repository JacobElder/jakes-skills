The "living off dividends" idea is appealing — passive income flowing in, never touching principal. But it's built on a misconception about how dividends work, and JEPI in particular has some real problems for a retirement portfolio. Let me break this down.

**Dividends are not free money**

This is the core Boglehead position, and it's backed by basic accounting: when a company pays a $1 dividend, its share price drops by $1 on the ex-dividend date. You haven't gained anything; the value just moved from inside the company to your brokerage account. Total return — price appreciation plus dividends — is what matters, not just the dividend component in isolation.

The intuition that "I'm getting paid without selling shares" is emotionally compelling but financially incorrect. Receiving a dividend and selling an equivalent number of shares produce the same economic result. The difference is that dividends are taxable events you don't control, while selling shares lets you control the timing and amount of taxable income.

**SCHD: a reasonable fund, but not a better mousetrap**

SCHD (Schwab US Dividend Equity ETF, ER: 0.06%) selects large US dividend-payers. It's a decent, low-cost fund — but understand what you're actually holding:

- It's about 90% correlated to the S&P 500. You're not diversifying much.
- It's a value tilt in disguise. The research suggesting dividend stocks "outperform" is mostly just capturing the value factor; once you control for that, the dividend premium disappears.
- It excludes the high-growth non-dividend payers that have driven a significant portion of market returns over the past two decades (think early Amazon, Alphabet, Meta). A total market fund like VTI holds all of those too.
- Dividends are taxable each year whether you want them or not. In a taxable account, this creates annual tax drag compared to a total-return index fund where you control when you realize gains.

If you own SCHD in a tax-advantaged account (IRA, 401k), the tax drag isn't an issue. It's just a mild-value-tilt ETF at low cost — not terrible, but not better than VTI.

**JEPI: this one is a real problem**

JEPI (JPMorgan Equity Premium Income ETF, ER: 0.35%) is a covered-call overlay fund. It sells call options on its stock holdings to generate "income." The result:

- You cap your upside in bull markets. When stocks run up, you've sold away those gains to option buyers. In a sustained bull market, JEPI meaningfully underperforms a simple S&P 500 fund.
- The distributions are largely ordinary income (from the option premiums), not qualified dividends. This means they're taxed at your marginal rate, not the favorable 15% qualified dividend rate. If you're in a taxable account, JEPI is tax-inefficient.
- Since inception (2020), JEPI has trailed VTI significantly in total return. The high "yield" is not extra return — it's return that was there, repackaged into a different pocket, with worse tax treatment and capped growth potential attached.

JEPI is marketed to retirees seeking income. That doesn't make it right for retirees. The marketing exploits the same "living off dividends without selling" intuition you're describing.

**The actual Boglehead approach to retirement income**

The Boglehead community has thought carefully about this. The answer isn't to find the highest-yielding funds; it's to build a total-return portfolio and then withdraw from it systematically.

Here's why this works better:

1. **You control the amount.** A 4% withdrawal from a diversified portfolio is a deliberate, planned decision. Dividend income fluctuates based on what companies decide to pay, which you don't control.

2. **Better tax treatment.** Long-term capital gains (selling appreciated shares) are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income. JEPI's ordinary-income distributions are taxed at your marginal rate — potentially much higher.

3. **No sacrifice of growth.** You don't have to choose funds that limit upside to generate cash. Hold a total-market fund, let it compound, and sell what you need each year.

4. **The math is the same.** A 4% dividend yield and a 4% annual sell-down of a total return portfolio with the same underlying assets produce the same result over time. The psychology feels different, but the economics are identical.

The standard Boglehead retirement portfolio: a three-fund portfolio (VTI for US stocks, VXUS for international, BND for bonds) in an allocation that matches your risk tolerance. Withdraw 3.5–4% per year (or use the "flexible spending" approach). Done.

**What to do from here**

A few questions worth thinking through:

- *Where are these holdings?* If SCHD/JEPI are in a taxable account, selling them may trigger capital gains. Don't sell just for neatness; redirect new contributions toward VTI/VXUS/BND and let the existing positions run off over time. If they're in an IRA, you can rebalance without a tax hit.

- *How close are you to retirement?* The urgency of a course correction depends on your timeline. If you're 30 years out, you have time to glide into a better allocation. If you're 5 years out, it's worth being more deliberate.

- *What's your full portfolio picture?* Employer-sponsored accounts (401k), IRA, taxable, and cash all count. Make sure you're running through the funding waterfall: employer match first, then HSA if eligible, then max the IRA, then max the 401k, then taxable.

The instinct to create passive income without "touching principal" is understandable and worth honoring psychologically — but the tool for that isn't high-yield dividend ETFs. It's a well-sized total-return portfolio with a disciplined withdrawal plan.

As always, this isn't personalized financial advice — your specific tax situation, account types, and timeline matter a lot. The bogleheads.org forum (free, staffed by people who've thought about this for decades) is excellent for pressure-testing a retirement plan with your actual numbers.
