Given your situation, here is the Boglehead funding waterfall applied directly to your $1,200/month. Let's walk through it in order.

## Your situation at a glance

- $1,200/month to allocate
- No employer 401(k) match
- $18,000 in credit card debt at 24% APR
- $3,000 in savings
- Enrolled in a high-deductible health plan (HDHP)

---

## Step 1: You already have a starter emergency fund — mostly.

The Boglehead waterfall starts with a $1,000–$1 month-of-expenses cushion before anything else. Your $3,000 in savings likely covers this depending on your monthly expenses. If your monthly essential spending is under $3,000, you're fine here and can move on. If it's significantly more, keep your $3,000 in a high-yield savings account (Ally, Marcus, SoFi, or similar — currently yielding ~4–5%) and don't touch it.

---

## Step 2: No employer match — skip.

You have no employer match, so step 2 of the waterfall (capture the full match before doing anything else) is irrelevant. This is actually a meaningful loss — an employer match is typically a 50–100% instant return, and nothing else in the waterfall competes with it. Without it, we move directly to high-interest debt.

---

## Step 3: Attack the credit card debt. All $1,200/month goes here.

This is the core of the answer.

Your credit card debt at 24% APR is a guaranteed, risk-free, tax-free 24% return to pay it off. The S&P 500 averages roughly 10% per year *before* inflation and before taxes. No investment reliably returns more than 24%. There is no world where putting money into an IRA or brokerage account while carrying credit card debt at 24% makes mathematical sense.

Here's the math: $18,000 at 24% APR accrues roughly $4,320 in interest per year — about $360/month. If you're only making minimum payments, much of your payment is just servicing interest, not reducing principal. Every dollar you put toward an index fund instead of this debt is effectively a dollar borrowing at 24% to invest.

**Direct all $1,200/month to the credit card debt.**

At $1,200/month, assuming your minimum payments are included in that figure and assuming no new charges:

- $18,000 / $1,200 ≈ 15 months to pay off (roughly — interest will slow you down early, so call it 16–18 months)

The exact payoff timeline depends on your current minimum payment and interest accrual, but you're looking at the debt being gone in approximately 1.5 years if you stay disciplined.

**Do not split the $1,200 between debt payoff and investing.** This is a common mistake. Splitting it feels balanced, but it means you're borrowing at 24% to invest. The guaranteed 24% return from debt payoff will almost certainly beat anything the market does in that 18-month window.

---

## The HSA exception — a small carve-out worth considering

Here's where it gets nuanced: you are enrolled in an HDHP, which makes you eligible for a Health Savings Account (HSA). The HSA is the only account with triple-tax advantages:

1. Contributions are pre-tax (or above-the-line deductible if made outside payroll)
2. Growth is tax-free
3. Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free

This is genuinely the best account structure that exists for US investors. The Boglehead consensus places it *above* IRA contributions in the waterfall because of this.

The 2025 HSA contribution limit is $4,300 for individual coverage (approximately $358/month).

**Should you contribute to the HSA while paying down credit card debt?**

This is one of the trickier judgment calls. The strict Boglehead waterfall says: pay off the high-interest debt first, then HSA. And that math is technically correct — 24% is a hard number to beat, even accounting for the tax advantages of the HSA.

However, there's a legitimate argument for a small monthly HSA contribution even while carrying the debt:

- If your effective marginal tax rate is meaningful (say 22–24%), the immediate pre-tax contribution effectively reduces the cost. At 22%, contributing $1 pre-tax costs you $0.78 after tax. You're borrowing at 24% on a net-of-tax basis on the credit card, and the HSA contribution is saving you 22% right now. The math is close.
- HSA funds can be invested in index funds and grow tax-free for decades. Time in the market has compounding value that's difficult to recover.
- You can pay current medical expenses out of pocket and reimburse yourself from the HSA years later — there's no time limit on reimbursements. This is the "HSA hack" that Bogleheads love.

**My recommendation:** If your tax rate is 22%+, consider contributing at least enough to the HSA to fund expected medical costs this year (your HDHP deductible and likely out-of-pocket expenses), and direct the rest to credit card payoff. If you're in the 12% bracket or below, the math tips toward 100% debt payoff.

The rough allocation if you choose the HSA carve-out: $200–$358/month to HSA, remainder (~$842–$1,000) to credit card debt. Payoff takes a few months longer, but you get the HSA clock ticking.

## Step 4: After the debt is gone — here's the order for your $1,200/month

Once the credit card is paid off (call it 18–20 months from now), the order for your $1,200/month becomes:

1. **Finish the emergency fund** if it's not already at 3–6 months of expenses. Your $3,000 may or may not cover this depending on your expenses. If not, top it up to 3–6 months in a HYSA before heavy investing.

2. **Max the HSA** — $4,300/year individual in 2025 (~$358/month). Invest it in a low-cost index fund inside the HSA (not just the default money market). Pay medical expenses out of pocket if you can; let the HSA grow untouched.

3. **Max a Roth IRA** — $7,000/year in 2025 (~$583/month). Use Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab. At Fidelity: FZROX (total US market) + FZILX (total international) is a simple two-fund portfolio with 0% expense ratios. At Vanguard: VTI + VXUS. At Schwab: SWTSX + SWISX (add SCHE for emerging markets coverage).

   Roth vs Traditional: If you expect your income to grow over your career, Roth is almost certainly better — you pay today's lower tax rate and never pay again. If you're already in a high bracket (32%+), revisit.

4. **Max the 401(k)** — $23,500 in 2025 limit. Even without a match, the 401(k) provides a tax-deferred shelter worth having. After IRA is maxed, this is the next stop.

5. **Taxable brokerage** for any remainder beyond that.

With $1,200/month post-debt:
- HSA: $358/month
- Roth IRA: $583/month
- Remainder ($259/month) → toward 401(k) or taxable

That gets you HSA and Roth fully maxed. Clean and straightforward.

---

## What not to do

- Do not open a brokerage account and start investing in index funds while carrying this debt. The 24% rate is a guaranteed loss you're absorbing while chasing an uncertain 7–10% gain.
- Do not put the $3,000 in savings toward the credit card yet if it leaves you with no cushion. A $0 savings balance means the next car repair or medical bill goes on the credit card, adding to the problem.
- Do not try to time the market or wait for a "better entry point" once the debt is gone. Lump sum beats dollar-cost averaging about two-thirds of the time; when you're ready to invest, invest.
