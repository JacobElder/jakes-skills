The short answer is no — don't wait. Here's why, and what to actually do.

**Market timing doesn't work. Full stop.**

"The market is at all-time highs and a crash is coming" is the most common reason investors sit in cash — and it has cost those investors an enormous amount of money over the decades. Here's the structural problem with the logic:

Markets are at all-time highs roughly 30% of all trading days historically. If you refused to invest on any day that was an all-time high, you'd be out of the market constantly. More importantly, the studies are unambiguous: investors who wait for a better entry point underperform investors who invest immediately, most of the time.

Vanguard ran the numbers: lump-sum investing beats dollar-cost averaging (DCA) about two-thirds of the time, across US markets, UK markets, and Australian markets, across various time horizons. The reason is simple — expected returns are positive, so every day you're out of the market you're paying a cost in expected return. The "wait for a dip" strategy requires you to be right twice: when to get out (or stay out), and when to get back in. Almost nobody does this correctly.

**The 20-30% correction scenario specifically**

Let's say a correction does come. You're waiting on the sidelines with $60,000. A few things to consider:

1. **You don't know when it comes.** The market ran up another 30–40% before the 2022 correction. If you waited in cash from 2019 for a correction before investing, you'd have missed massive gains — and then still faced the same fear when you were "down" from the peak.

2. **When a correction arrives, you won't feel like buying.** This is the cruelest part of market timing psychology. When the market drops 25%, the news is catastrophic, unemployment may be rising, and everything feels like it will get worse. Most people waiting for a dip end up waiting for it to "stabilize" — which means buying after the recovery has already happened.

3. **The math of waiting is brutal.** If you sit in a high-yield savings account at 4–5% for a year waiting for a correction that doesn't come — and the market returns its historical average of ~10% — you've given up roughly $3,000–3,600 in expected returns. If the market returns 20% before it corrects (not unusual), you're even further behind.

**What "everyone says a crash is coming" should tell you**

When the crowd agrees something is imminent, it's usually already priced in. Predictions of imminent crashes are the default noise of financial media in every bull market. There have been "obvious" crash signals every year for decades. Many of the most renowned economists and market strategists have missed calls spectacularly. The market can remain "irrational" far longer than you can afford to wait.

Bogle put it bluntly: "The idea that a bell rings to signal when investors should get into or out of the stock market is simply not credible. After nearly 50 years in this business, I do not know of anybody who has done it successfully and consistently. I don't even know anybody who knows anybody who has done it successfully and consistently."

**What to actually do with $60,000**

First, a quick check on the funding waterfall before putting everything into a taxable brokerage:

- Do you have an emergency fund (3–6 months of expenses in a HYSA or money market)? If not, keep some of this cash there first.
- Have you maxed your IRA for this year ($7,000 in 2025, $8,000 if 50+)?
- Have you maxed your HSA if you're on an eligible HDHP?
- Have you contributed enough to your 401(k) to capture the full employer match?

If those boxes are checked (or mostly checked), then yes — invest the $60,000. The question is how.

**Lump sum vs. DCA as a behavioral compromise**

Mathematically, lump sum wins. But if you're genuinely anxious about investing at "the top," dollar-cost averaging over 6–12 months is a perfectly reasonable behavioral compromise. It won't maximize expected returns, but it may keep you from panicking and selling if the market drops 20% the month after you invest everything. The goal isn't just optimal strategy — it's a strategy you'll actually stick to.

So: if you can invest the $60,000 today and sleep at night, do it. If you'd feel sick watching it drop 25% in month one, consider spreading it over 6 months ($10,000/month). Either is vastly better than waiting indefinitely for a correction.

**What to invest in**

A simple three-fund portfolio is the right answer:

- **US Total Market:** VTI (Vanguard), FSKAX (Fidelity), or ITOT (iShares)
- **Total International:** VXUS (Vanguard), FTIHX (Fidelity), or IXUS (iShares)
- **Total Bond Market:** BND (Vanguard), FXNAX (Fidelity), or AGG (iShares)

If you don't want to choose allocations, a single target-date retirement fund is a completely legitimate, Boglehead-approved answer. Set the target year to around your expected retirement and invest everything there. Vanguard's Target Retirement funds run 0.08% expense ratio — nearly free.

**The bottom line**

The fear of "buying at the top" is real, understandable, and one of the greatest destroyers of investor returns. There is no credible, systematic evidence that any individual or institution can reliably time the market. The decades of data say the same thing: stay the course, buy broadly, keep costs low, and ignore the noise.

Bogle again: "The two greatest enemies of the equity fund investor are expenses and emotions." Waiting for a crash is emotions winning.

Invest the money. Keep your allocation consistent with your risk tolerance and time horizon. Stop watching financial news.
