But the core premise — that you can time your entry around a correction — is one of the most reliably wealth-destroying impulses in personal investing.

Here's why, systematically.

---

**"Everyone says a crash is coming" has been true for most of the last 30 years.**

The market has been "overvalued by historical metrics" for virtually the entire post-1990 period. CAPE ratios above their long-run averages, bond yields at historic lows, headlines about unsustainable valuations — this narrative has been continuous. People who acted on it in 2013 ("the market is at all-time highs, a crash is coming") missed the next decade. People who acted on it in 2016 missed it. In 2019 ("longest bull market ever, a correction is overdue"). In 2021. In 2024. The market being at all-time highs is not a signal — it's the normal state of a growing economy. About 30% of all trading days in market history have been all-time highs.

---

**The math of waiting is almost always worse than the math of investing.**

Vanguard studied this directly. Looking at historical data, lump-sum investing beat dollar-cost averaging roughly two-thirds of the time — simply because markets go up more often than they go down. Waiting in cash while "the crash" fails to arrive costs you real money every month in foregone returns.

Let's make this concrete. Assume you sit in cash earning 4.5% in a high-yield savings account waiting for a 20-30% pullback. That pullback might come in 6 months, or 2 years, or 5 years — or the market might climb another 40% before it does. If the market rises 12% in the next year before a 25% crash, you still needed to get in to benefit from that crash, and you're now buying at a higher price than today even after the correction. Sitting out doesn't guarantee a better entry point.

---

**Peter Lynch said it directly:** "Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections than has been lost in corrections themselves."

This isn't cheerleading. It's an empirical observation about what actually happens to timing strategies. The correction happens, and then the investor — still uncertain, still seeing negative headlines — waits for the "all clear" that never comes cleanly. Missing the 10 best trading days in any given decade typically halves terminal returns. Those best days almost always occur during or just after the worst days, when the news is worst and fear is highest. The people who got out during the crash miss the recovery.

---

**Your specific scenario: waiting for a 20-30% correction.**

Let's grant the most optimistic version of your plan: a real 20-30% crash happens within 12 months, you correctly identify the bottom, and you invest at the trough. Even then, Dichev's research across NYSE/AMEX data from 1926 to 2002 found that investors' actual dollar-weighted returns lagged buy-and-hold returns by about 1.3% annually — because of exactly this pattern of buying high and selling low (or in this case, sitting out and buying late). The behavior gap is persistent and documented across decades.

---

**What you should actually do with $60,000.**

Before the investing question, make sure the waterfall is in order:

1. Do you have a full emergency fund (3-6 months of expenses) in a high-yield savings account? If not, that comes first — not because of market timing, but because an emergency fund prevents you from having to sell investments in a down market.
2. Are you getting the full employer 401(k) match? That's an instant 50-100% return, uncorrelated with market valuations. Maximize that first.
3. Is there any high-interest debt (anything above ~7%)? Pay that down before investing in taxable accounts — it's a guaranteed return.
4. Have you maxed your IRA for this year ($7,000 limit in 2025)? Tax-advantaged space is finite; if you have the cash, use it.

If those boxes are checked, the $60,000 goes into a taxable brokerage account in a broad-market index fund — VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF, 0.03% ER), VXUS for international if you want it, and BND for bonds if your allocation calls for them.

---

**The two defensible approaches:**

**Option A — Lump sum now.** Statistically better outcome about two-thirds of the time. Psychologically harder. If you do this, you must accept that the market might immediately fall 20-30% and you'll sit through it without selling. If that would cause you to panic-sell, you shouldn't do it.

**Option B — DCA over 6-12 months.** Invest $5,000-$10,000 per month on a fixed schedule, automatically, regardless of headlines. This is not market timing — you are not waiting for conditions to change; you are deploying capital systematically. This is the behavioral compromise. Statistically slightly worse in expectation but keeps you invested and removes the decision-making burden.

What is not a defensible option: sitting in cash indefinitely waiting for a crash that may or may not come, at a depth and timing you cannot predict.

---

**On the "everyone says" framing.**

The times when crash consensus is highest are often near — but not at — actual tops, and the people who are loudest about crashes are usually not reliable signals. Many of the same voices who "called" 2022's correction had been calling it since 2017. One correct call in seven years is not a track record worth acting on.

Asset allocation is the right tool for managing crash risk. If a 30% drawdown would cause you to sell, the solution is not to wait for the crash — it's to hold fewer equities. An 80/20 or 70/30 stock/bond portfolio loses less in a crash and lets you stay invested, which is the outcome that produces wealth.

---

**The single honest question to ask yourself:**

If you wait for the crash, invest at the trough, and then the market drops another 20% — will you hold? Because if the answer is "I'll wait until it stabilizes," you're revealing that the crash-timing plan isn't actually a plan to buy at a discount. It's a plan to feel certain before acting. And that feeling of certainty in markets only comes when prices are already high again.

The right asset allocation, chosen now, deployed now, held through whatever comes — that's the play.
