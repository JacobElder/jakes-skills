No, almost certainly not. This is one of the clearest cases where the financial-services industry's incentives and your interests point in opposite directions.

Here's what's actually happening with whole life insurance.

**What you're being sold**

Whole life is a bundled product: term life insurance wrapped together with a tax-deferred savings account. The pitch sounds attractive — tax-free growth, borrow against it later, guaranteed death benefit. But the costs embedded in that bundle are enormous, and they come at your expense.

**The math is bad, especially at the start**

The first several years of your premiums largely go to commissions and fees — not to your cash value. Insurance agents selling whole life earn commissions that can be 50–100% of your first year's premium. That $500/month ($6,000/year) you're about to send: a substantial chunk of year one is paying your advisor's commission.

After those initial years, the internal rate of return on whole life cash value is typically in the range of 0–2% for decades before it becomes even remotely competitive with what you'd get elsewhere. Compare that to the long-run expected return of a total stock market index fund (historically ~7% real, ~10% nominal).

**The "tax-free" claim is misleading**

The loans against your cash value are not free. You're borrowing your own money and paying 5–8% loan interest back to the policy. The "no tax" framing is technically accurate — it's a loan, not a withdrawal — but you are paying interest *to yourself* at a rate that reduces the death benefit if you don't pay it back. It's not the tax-free miracle it sounds like.

**What you should do instead: Term + Invest the Difference**

If you have dependents who rely on your income, you do need life insurance. But you should buy pure life insurance (term), not a savings vehicle wrapped in insurance.

- **Buy 20- or 30-year level term life insurance.** For most people in their 30s-40s, $1M of 20-year term coverage costs $30–60/month. That's the insurance piece.
- **Invest the rest** — the remaining $440–470/month — into tax-advantaged accounts in this priority order:

  1. Get the full employer 401(k) match first (free money, beats everything).
  2. Max an HSA if you're on a high-deductible health plan (triple-tax-advantaged — deductible contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for medical).
  3. Max a Roth or Traditional IRA ($7,000/year in 2025; choose Roth if your income qualifies and you expect your tax rate to be similar or higher in retirement).
  4. Max the 401(k) beyond the match.
  5. If you've done all of that, taxable brokerage in broad index funds.

**What you'd be giving up**

At $500/month, you're investing $6,000/year. In a Roth IRA and 401(k) with a three-fund index portfolio, at a 7% average annual return over 30 years, that grows to roughly $567,000. The whole life policy, with its drag of first-year commissions, ongoing expenses, and sub-market cash value growth, is likely to produce a fraction of that over the same period — despite similar (or higher) contributions.

Bogle put it plainly: "In investing, you get what you don't pay for." Every dollar that goes to commissions and expenses inside a whole life policy is a dollar that never compounds for you.

**Legitimate uses of whole life (rare)**

There are a few situations where permanent life insurance makes genuine sense:
- Very high-net-worth estates ($10M+) needing liquidity for estate taxes via an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT).
- Funding a buy-sell agreement for a business.
- Providing lifetime support for a disabled dependent.

If you're not in one of those situations, the answer is no.

**About your advisor**

Worth knowing: advisors who sell whole life insurance typically earn large commissions on these policies. That doesn't mean they're acting in bad faith — many genuinely believe in the product — but it does mean the recommendation isn't coming from a neutral, fee-only analysis of what's best for you. If you want independent advice, look for a fee-only fiduciary advisor (NAPFA.org or the Garrett Planning Network). They charge a flat fee or hourly rate, don't sell products, and are legally required to act in your interest.

**Bottom line**

If you need life insurance: buy term, invest the difference. If you don't need life insurance (no dependents, already wealthy, etc.), the answer is even clearer — just invest the $500/month in low-cost index funds through tax-advantaged accounts.

*This isn't personalized financial advice — if you have a complex tax situation, large estate, or other factors at play, it's worth pressure-testing this with a fee-only fiduciary or the bogleheads.org forum (free, and the community there has seen thousands of these situations).*
