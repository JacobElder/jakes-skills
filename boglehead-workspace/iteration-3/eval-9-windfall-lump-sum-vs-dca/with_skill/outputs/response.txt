Your instinct to be cautious is completely understandable — $175,000 is real money and the fear of investing at a market peak is a deeply human reaction. But let's be honest about what your plan actually is: you're proposing to spread $175,000 over 36 months at $5,000/month, which means for most of that period the majority of your windfall is sitting in cash. That's a significant bet that waiting will work out better than investing — and the evidence says it won't, most of the time.

**What the research actually says**

Vanguard published a study comparing lump-sum investing (LSI) to dollar-cost averaging (DCA) across US, UK, and Australian markets going back decades. The finding: lump sum outperformed a 12-month DCA about two-thirds of the time. The reason is simple — markets go up more often than they go down, so cash sitting on the sidelines is usually a drag, not protection.

Your plan isn't DCA over 12 months. It's DCA over 36 months. That's much more aggressive as a "wait and see" strategy. The longer the timeline, the worse cash tends to perform relative to invested capital.

Peter Lynch put it bluntly: "Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections than has been lost in corrections themselves."

**The fear is rational but the math doesn't support it**

Yes, the market could drop right after you invest. That's always true. But consider:

- If you invest $175k today and the market drops 30%, you have ~$122k. That's painful.
- If you invest $5k/month and the market rises 20% over the next two years before you finish deploying, you've missed $35k+ in gains on the money sitting in cash.
- The second scenario is roughly twice as likely as the first based on historical frequencies.

You don't feel the cash drag because it's invisible. You feel the loss acutely because it's visible. That's the behavioral asymmetry here, not a real asymmetry in expected outcomes.

**The Boglehead answer: lump sum if you can, 6–12 months DCA if you genuinely cannot**

The community's honest position on windfalls is this:

1. Lump sum is the mathematically correct move. If you can stomach the psychological risk of investing all at once and watching it potentially decline 30% in the short term, lump sum is the right call. The key question: is your asset allocation correct? If you're in a portfolio you could hold through a 50% drawdown without selling, then invest it all. If your allocation is right, the timing of a single entry point matters less than decades of compounding.

2. DCA over 3–12 months is the behavioral compromise. If the thought of investing $175k all at once and watching it drop $50k in a month would cause you to do something irrational — sell, panic, change your allocation — then DCA over a shorter timeframe is fine. It costs you some expected return but buys you peace of mind, which has real value if it keeps you from doing something worse.

3. DCA over 3 years is too long. This isn't a behavioral hedge anymore; it's market timing with extra steps. You're not protecting yourself from your own emotions — you're making a multi-year prediction about market direction. Bogleheads don't do that.

**A more reasonable approach**

If you want to stage this in, compress the timeline dramatically. Some options:

- **3 equal monthly installments ($58,333/month over 3 months)**: Fast enough to mostly capture the market's expected return, slow enough to feel like you're not betting everything on a single day.
- **6 installments (~$29,000/month)**: The upper end of what most Boglehead-minded folks would call a "behavioral compromise."
- **12 installments (~$14,600/month)**: Stretching it, but still defensible.

At 36 months, you'd be leaving a substantial amount in cash for an extended period. Over that time, assuming a modest 8% expected market return, you'd expect the uninvested balance to cost you roughly $10,000–$20,000 in foregone gains on average — and that's in an average-return scenario, not a bull market.

**Before you deploy it: make sure the rest of your financial house is in order**

Since this is a windfall on top of an existing portfolio, run through the basics quickly:

- Is your emergency fund fully funded (3–6 months of expenses in a high-yield savings account)? If not, carve out that amount first before investing any of this.
- Any high-interest debt (credit cards, anything above ~7%)? Pay that off first. Guaranteed return.
- Have you maxed your IRA for this year ($7,000 in 2025)? If not, do that immediately.
- Have you maxed your 401(k) contribution for this year? If you have remaining contribution room, you could accelerate payroll contributions and use this cash to cover living expenses — an indirect way to front-load tax-advantaged space with the windfall.

Once those boxes are checked, whatever's left goes into your three-fund portfolio in a taxable brokerage.

**Fund placement reminder for the taxable portion**

If you're adding to a taxable account, keep it tax-efficient:
- VTI or VTSAX for US stocks (qualified dividends, favorable LTCG treatment, step-up basis at death)
- VXUS for international (holds the foreign tax credit, which is lost if you own this in a tax-deferred account)
- Keep bonds out of taxable if you can — their interest income is taxed at ordinary rates

**The bottom line**

Your $5k/month over 3 years plan is driven by fear, not strategy. The fear is understandable, but the expected cost is real. At a minimum, compress that timeline to 6–12 months. If you can accept some short-term paper losses in exchange for the higher expected return of getting invested faster, lump sum is the right call.

The worst outcome isn't investing on a bad day. The worst outcome is staying in cash for 2 years while the market runs and then panic-buying at the top. That's the scenario the 3-year DCA plan actually risks.
