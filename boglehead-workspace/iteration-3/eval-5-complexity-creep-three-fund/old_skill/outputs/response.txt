Your three-fund portfolio is already doing the job. Let me be direct about what adding these four funds would and wouldn't accomplish.

**The short answer: No, this isn't more diversified in a meaningful way, and there is no reason to expect better returns.**

Here's why, fund by fund.

---

**VIOV (Vanguard S&P Small-Cap 600 Value ETF) — small-cap value tilt**

This is the most defensible add on your list. Factor investing — specifically tilting toward small-cap value — has real academic backing (Fama-French). Larry Swedroe, Rick Ferri, and others in the Boglehead community endorse modest factor tilts. The rationale: small-cap value has historically produced a premium over total market, and because the premium is tied to genuine risk and investor discomfort, there's a theoretical reason to think it persists.

But you need to know what you're signing up for. The SCV premium disappears for long stretches — the 2010s were brutal for value investors. You're making a bet that you'll stick to this tilt through years of underperformance. Most people don't. VTI already holds small-cap stocks (it's a total market fund); you'd be *overweighting* small-cap value, not gaining it for the first time.

This is a legitimate Boglehead disagreement. Taylor Larimore and Bogle himself preferred pure total-market and considered tilts unnecessary. Ferri and Swedroe think a modest tilt is rational. If you understand the tracking error and commit to holding for 20+ years, a small VIOV tilt (10–15% of equities) is defensible. If you're chasing recent performance or read a Reddit post last week, skip it.

**VNQ (Vanguard Real Estate ETF) — REITs**

VTI already includes REITs — about 3.5% of the fund. You are not "missing" REITs. Adding VNQ overweights real estate relative to its market-cap weight in the total economy.

The argument for overweighting REITs is that they have a slightly different return profile than the rest of US equities and may provide some inflation hedge. There's academic support for a mild REIT tilt in the literature. But that argument has weakened since the REIT IPO boom of the 1990s when REITs were genuinely underrepresented in indexes.

More practically: VNQ distributes large ordinary dividends, which are tax-inefficient in a taxable account. If you're using a taxable brokerage, VNQ is a poor choice for that account.

Adding VNQ means your portfolio now has a deliberate sector overweight in real estate. That's the opposite of the total-market philosophy. This is generally a "no" from a Boglehead standpoint unless you have a specific, articulated reason to overweight this sector.

**VIG (Vanguard Dividend Appreciation ETF) — dividend growth**

This one is worth addressing directly because it's a common misconception.

Dividends are not free money. When a stock pays a $1 dividend, its share price drops by $1. You have not received value that wouldn't otherwise exist — you've received your own money back in a different form, minus any taxes you now owe on it.

VIG holds large-cap dividend growers, which means you're getting a quality/profitability tilt but *missing* the rest of the US stock market. VIG underweights value stocks, cyclicals, and sectors that don't consistently raise dividends. Over the last 15 years it has performed roughly in line with VTI — sometimes better, sometimes worse — not because of the dividend screen but because quality/profitability as a factor has had a good run.

Adding VIG alongside VTI means you now hold a bunch of large-cap US stocks twice (VTI already owns all of VIG's holdings), and you're underweighting the parts of VTI that VIG excludes. This is portfolio complexity that generates no benefit. It adds taxable events, rebalancing overhead, and the psychological problem of watching two overlapping funds diverge.

"I want income" is not a Boglehead reason to own dividend funds. Total return is what matters. If you need cash flow, sell shares. The math is identical; the tax treatment is often better.

**VSS (Vanguard FTSE All-World ex-US Small-Cap ETF) — international small-cap**

VXUS holds international developed and emerging markets at roughly their global market-cap weights. It does include some small-cap, but it is tilted toward large/mid-cap (as most international indexes are).

VSS is a legitimate way to get international small-cap exposure, and some Bogleheads who believe in the SCV premium add it for the same reasons as VIOV. If your thesis is "I want a global small-cap value tilt," then VIOV + VSS together express that view.

But again: you're making a bet. International small-cap has had a rough decade relative to US large-cap. The question is whether you're adding VSS because you have a principled view about factor exposure, or because you read that it "diversifies" your portfolio.

---

**The diversification question**

Your current three-fund portfolio — VTI + VXUS + BND — holds approximately 10,000–13,000 individual securities. VTI alone holds the entire US stock market. VXUS holds the entire developed and emerging international market. You are already as diversified as a stock-and-bond investor can get.

What your proposed additions do is not *broaden* diversification — you can't get more diversified than the total world equity market. What they do is *tilt* your portfolio toward specific sectors and factors. That's a different thing. It can be rational. It is not the same as diversification.

The Boglehead principle here is Bogle's own: "When there are multiple solutions to a problem, choose the simplest one." A seven-fund portfolio has more moving parts, more rebalancing complexity, more opportunities to make behavioral mistakes, and no demonstrated return premium over the three-fund version — especially not reliably, especially not after you account for the tracking error you'll experience and the temptation to fiddle.

---

**Will it give better returns?**

No one can promise that. Here's what's honest:

- A small-cap value tilt *may* produce a premium over the very long run, but you will experience years of underperformance. Most investors abandon factor tilts before they pay off. The premium is real partly because most people can't tolerate it.
- REITs and dividend funds have no reliable return premium over total market. They are sector/style bets.
- International small-cap has theoretical support if you believe in global factor exposure, but the timing and magnitude of any premium are uncertain.

Adding complexity to a three-fund portfolio is not neutral. It introduces new failure modes: rebalancing drift, tax inefficiency, style-chasing, and the psychological burden of watching some funds lag and questioning your strategy.

---

**What I'd actually do**

If you're genuinely convinced by the factor-investing literature and want to tilt, the minimal-complexity way is:

- Keep VTI + VXUS + BND as your core.
- Add a modest VIOV position (10–15% of your equity allocation) if you have a long time horizon (20+ years) and can commit to holding it through a decade of potential underperformance.
- Skip VNQ (already in VTI, tax-inefficient, sector bet).
- Skip VIG (redundant overlap with VTI, dividend mythology).
- Consider VSS only if you're building a principled global factor tilt alongside VIOV.

That's a four-fund portfolio at most. Seven funds is where complexity starts costing you more than it earns you.

The three-fund portfolio has earned its reputation not because people couldn't think of anything to add. It's because people thought of everything and concluded that less was more.
