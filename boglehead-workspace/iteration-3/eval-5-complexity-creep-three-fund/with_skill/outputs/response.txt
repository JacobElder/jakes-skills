Your three-fund portfolio isn't broken. Let's be direct about what you're actually proposing before you add four more lines to your spreadsheet.

---

**The core issue: you already own all of these.**

VTI is the US total stock market. That means it holds every publicly traded US company at its market-cap weight — including small-cap stocks, REITs, and dividend-growing companies. When you add VIOV, VNQ, or VIG on top of VTI, you're not adding diversification. You're creating a *deliberate overweight* to those segments. That's a bet that they'll outperform the rest of the market. It's not a neutral improvement.

Let's go fund by fund:

**VIOV (small-cap value):** VTI already includes small-cap value stocks. Adding VIOV means you're making a targeted bet that small-cap value will outperform large-cap growth over your time horizon. To be fair, this is a *legitimate* Boglehead debate — Larry Swedroe and Rick Ferri have argued for modest factor tilts based on the academic literature (Fama-French), while Jack Bogle himself and Taylor Larimore (author of the Boglehead guide) preferred pure total-market. Both positions exist in the Boglehead tradition. But this is a deliberate active decision, not a passive diversification improvement. If you add it, size it modestly (5–10% of equities) and do it because you understand the factor thesis, not because you read that small-cap value "has outperformed."

**VNQ (REITs):** VTI already holds REITs at their current market weight (~3–4% of the US market). VNQ concentrates that exposure. The main academic argument for a REIT tilt is that real estate has historically had low correlation with other equities and acts as an inflation hedge. Again — legitimate in some Boglehead circles, contested in others. What it is *not* is "more diversification." It's more concentration in one asset class.

**VIG (dividend growth):** This one you should remove from the list entirely. Dividends are not free money. When a company pays a $1 dividend, the share price drops by $1 — you're just forcing a taxable event. VIG is a large-cap tilt toward companies with a track record of raising dividends, which in practice is a quality/value tilt in disguise. VTI already owns all of these companies. Adding VIG overweights them and creates extra tax drag (dividend distributions taxed annually vs. unrealized gains deferred until you sell). The Boglehead community has a consistent view here: dividend-focused strategies do not add value that a total-market fund doesn't already capture, and they're less tax-efficient. Total return is what matters, not yield.

**VSS (international small-cap):** VXUS already holds international small-cap stocks, just like VTI holds US small-cap. VSS tilts toward international small-cap specifically. The argument is similar to the VIOV argument — there's academic evidence for a small-cap premium internationally, but it's a deliberate factor bet, not passive coverage you're currently missing.

---

**"But won't more funds give me better returns?"**

Not reliably. Expected return is driven by your asset allocation (how much in stocks vs. bonds, and at what risk level), not by the number of funds. A 90/10 three-fund portfolio will have higher expected returns than an 80/20 seven-fund portfolio, full stop. Adding funds doesn't create return; it adds complexity.

The research on factor tilts (small-cap value in particular) does show a *historical* premium. But:

1. That premium has been weaker and more volatile post-publication, as capital has piled into it
2. It requires discipline to hold through long periods of underperformance — small-cap value underperformed for most of 2007–2020
3. Most retail investors abandon tilts during exactly those periods, turning a paper advantage into a real loss

The honest answer is: a factor tilt *might* add a small amount of long-run return, at the cost of real complexity and behavioral risk. Most people are better served by the simpler portfolio they can actually stick with.

---

**Simplicity isn't a cop-out. It's the strategy.**

Bogle: "When there are multiple solutions to a problem, choose the simplest one."

Your three-fund portfolio (VTI, VXUS, BND) is not a starting point waiting to be improved. It is a complete, globally diversified portfolio that captures the entire world market at near-zero cost. The question "how do I improve it?" is often downstream of reading too many financial blogs and forums — the investment industry profits from convincing you that more complexity equals better outcomes. Usually it doesn't.

---

**If you want to make a change, here's the Boglehead-consistent way to think about it:**

- **Do nothing (recommended for most people):** Your three-fund portfolio is excellent. Rebalance annually or when your allocation drifts 5%+ from target. The best next step is probably increasing contribution rate, not adding funds.

- **If you're genuinely interested in factor tilts:** Consider a small VIOV allocation (5–10% of the equity portion, taken from VTI) as a deliberate, eyes-open small-cap value tilt. Skip VIG entirely. Consider VSS only if you're already committed to a factor-tilt philosophy across your whole portfolio. Skip VNQ as a separate fund — VTI's market-weight REIT exposure is sufficient unless you have a specific thesis.

- **Whatever you do, drop VIG from the list.** It's the one fund here that most consistently fails the Boglehead test. You're paying a small premium for a dividend screen that adds tax drag and provides no diversification VTI doesn't already give you.

---

**The harder question underneath this one**

Reading a lot is great. But reading a lot sometimes leads to this exact pattern: each article you read makes the case for one more fund, and the portfolio creeps from 3 to 7 to 12. The Boglehead tradition has a direct answer here — be suspicious of your own complexity bias. "Is this portfolio I'm building actually better, or does it just feel more sophisticated?" is a question worth sitting with before you change anything.

Your current portfolio is one most retail investors would have been lucky to build. Maintain the asset allocation, stay the course, and let compounding do the work.
