Your advisor is using a real tax fact to lead you to a questionable conclusion. Let's separate those two things.

**The tax concern is legitimate — but it's not a reason to hold.**

You have a $135,000 long-term capital gain ($200k - $65k cost basis). At the 15% federal LTCG rate (or 20% if your income is high enough, plus 3.8% net investment income tax if applicable), you're looking at roughly $20,000–$33,000 in federal taxes. That's real money. But here's the key reframe: the tax is the cost of diversifying, not a reason to stay concentrated.

The question isn't "should I pay $25,000 in taxes?" The question is: **would you buy $200,000 of Tesla stock today with 60% of your portfolio if you were starting from scratch?** If the answer is no — and for most Bogleheads it would be a hard no — then the only reason you're still holding is anchoring to your cost basis and fear of the tax bill. Neither of those is an investment thesis.

**"Let your winners run" is a real thing. It's also frequently wrong.**

There's a version of this advice that makes sense: don't sell a diversified index fund just because it went up. Markets generally go up; letting a total-market position grow is fine. But that logic does not transfer to a single stock. What made Tesla a winner in the past is not evidence about what it will do next. And at 60% concentration, you're not managing a portfolio — you're making a single-company bet.

Bessembinder's research (covering 1926–2016) found that the majority of individual stocks underperform Treasury bills over their lifetime, and nearly all of the wealth creation in the US market came from roughly 4% of stocks. If you hold Tesla specifically, you're betting you own one of those — and that even if it is one, you know that now, when it already has a very large market cap. That's the part your advisor is skipping.

**The concentration risk is the bigger issue, not the tax.**

At 60% of your portfolio in a single stock, here's what that means concretely: a 50% drawdown in Tesla — which has happened twice in the past five years — cuts your total portfolio by 30%. Tesla has had multiple 70%+ drawdowns from peak to trough. A company that went from $65k to $200k can also go from $200k to $60k. The tax on the gain wouldn't feel "enormous" in that scenario; it would feel like a missed opportunity.

The Boglehead view on individual stocks is blunt: even if you're right about the business, concentration risk is real and the expected value of a diversified portfolio typically exceeds the expected value of a concentrated single-stock bet. This isn't a knock on Tesla specifically — it's true of Apple, Amazon, Nvidia, or any other name at 60% weight.

**What to actually do.**

You have a few options, and you don't have to do them all at once:

1. **Sell in tranches over 1–3 years.** If tax minimization is important, you don't have to realize the whole gain at once. Sell a portion each year, staying within your preferred tax bracket. This reduces the tax hit in any single year without requiring you to stay fully concentrated indefinitely.

2. **Donate appreciated shares to charity (or a DAF).** If you give to charity, donating Tesla shares directly to a donor-advised fund lets you deduct the full market value, avoid the capital gains entirely, and invest the DAF in diversified index funds. Even if you don't have a current charitable intent, it's worth knowing this option exists.

3. **Harvest losses elsewhere to offset the gains.** If you have losing positions anywhere in your taxable account, realize those to reduce the net taxable gain.

4. **Accept the tax and redeploy.** Sometimes the simplest thing is right. Pay the tax, move $175k (after-tax approximate) into VTI/VXUS/BND in whatever allocation fits your plan, and stop worrying about one company.

The one thing I'd push back on: doing nothing because of the tax bill while the concentration risk continues to compound. The tax is a known, capped cost. The downside of staying concentrated is open-ended.

**On your advisor.**

"Let your winners run" combined with "the tax would be enormous" is a classic advisory deflection that keeps assets under management without forcing a hard conversation. It's not necessarily bad faith — some advisors genuinely believe it. But it's a phrase that serves the status quo rather than your portfolio's health. A fee-only fiduciary advisor who ran actual scenario analysis on your concentration vs. diversification tradeoffs would likely give you different guidance.

One sentence at the end: this involves enough money and enough tax complexity that talking to an advice-only, fee-only fiduciary (NAPFA directory at napfa.org, or the Garrett Planning Network for hourly options) — or posting your full situation at bogleheads.org — is worth your time before you move.
