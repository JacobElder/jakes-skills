Great starting point. At 26, with a real income, minimal high-interest debt, and an employer 401(k), you're in better shape than most people who ask this question. Here's the Boglehead playbook for your exact situation — not a list of things to consider, but a prioritized action sequence.

---

## Step 1: Nail down your starter emergency fund

You have $2,000 in savings. That's close to the starter target of $1,000–1 month of expenses. Before you do anything else, park that money in a high-yield savings account (HYSA) — Ally, Marcus, SoFi, Wealthfront Cash, or similar. These are currently yielding around 4–5%, which is meaningfully better than a checking account and keeps the money fully liquid. Don't put it in stocks; this is your "car breaks down / ER visit" buffer. Without it, any financial shock pushes you into credit card debt, and that unravels everything else.

Your $2,000 is probably enough to call this done. Move on.

---

## Step 2: Capture the full 401(k) employer match — immediately

Find out what your employer match is. The most common structure is something like "we match 50% of your contributions up to 6% of salary," but it varies. Whatever it is, contribute at minimum enough to get every dollar of that match.

This is the most important investment move you can make. An employer match is an immediate 50–100% return on your money before it's even invested. Nothing else competes. Not paying off student loans, not maxing an IRA — the match comes first.

At $75,000 salary, if your employer matches 50% of up to 6%, contributing 6% ($4,500/year) earns you $2,250 in free employer contributions. That's an instant 50% return. Enroll today if you haven't.

---

## Step 3: Your student loans — probably not a crisis, but worth addressing next

You have $8,000 at 5.5%. That puts you right at the Boglehead gray zone — the soft cutoff where people debate whether to pay it down aggressively or invest instead is roughly 5–7%.

At 5.5%, you're not clearly on either side. The practical Boglehead answer: after capturing the full employer match, make more than the minimum payment on your loans, but don't go scorched-earth on them before funding your IRA. A reasonable approach is to pay them down on an accelerated schedule (maybe 12–18 months at current income) while simultaneously starting your IRA contributions. At $75k income and $8k in debt, you have enough cash flow to do both.

If the psychological weight of the debt bothers you, pay it off first. The math doesn't heavily favor one over the other at 5.5%, and the behavioral clarity of being debt-free is worth something.

---

## Step 4: Max a Roth IRA

After the employer match is captured and you're making real progress on the student loans, open a Roth IRA. At 26 with $75,000 in income, Roth is almost certainly the right call:

- You're likely in the 22% federal bracket now, and your income will probably grow over a 40-year career. Paying tax now at 22% and withdrawing tax-free in retirement — when you might be drawing from a sizable portfolio — is a good trade.
- A Roth IRA also has better flexibility than a traditional: you can withdraw your contributions (not earnings) anytime, penalty-free. It's not an emergency fund, but it's not locked away with zero escape hatch either.
- 2025 limit: $7,000. At $75k, you're well under the income phaseout ($150,000 single for 2025).

Open it at Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab. All three are excellent. Don't overthink this choice.

What to buy inside the Roth IRA: a single target-date fund (e.g., Vanguard Target Retirement 2065, ticker VFFVX, expense ratio ~0.08%) is a perfectly Boglehead answer. It holds a diversified global stock + bond portfolio in one fund and automatically adjusts the allocation as you age. If you want to build a three-fund portfolio yourself, that's also great — but the target-date fund means you can set it up in 20 minutes and never lose sleep over it.

---

## Step 5: Beyond the match in your 401(k)

Once the IRA is funded, go back to the 401(k) and contribute beyond the match up to the annual limit ($23,500 in 2025). You probably won't max it immediately while also paying off student loans, and that's fine. Directional progress matters more than doing everything perfectly in year one.

One thing to check: what's the fund menu in your 401(k)? Some plans have good low-cost index funds; others have expensive active funds. Look for a total US stock market index fund or S&P 500 index fund with an expense ratio below 0.20%. If the best option in your plan is an S&P 500 index at 0.05%, that's great. If the only options are actively managed funds at 0.80%+, that's worth knowing — contribute enough for the match, fund your IRA, and come back to this later.

If your plan has a Roth 401(k) option, the Roth vs. traditional decision is the same as the IRA: at 26 with likely rising income, Roth is probably right.

---

## What to actually invest in

The Boglehead default is a three-fund portfolio:

1. **US Total Stock Market** — VTI (ETF) or VTSAX (Vanguard mutual fund), expense ratio 0.03–0.04%. If you're at Fidelity, FSKAX (0.015%) or FZROX (0.00%) in tax-advantaged accounts (do not use FZROX in taxable — it's not portable and forces a taxable sale if you ever switch brokerages).
2. **Total International Stock Market** — VXUS (Vanguard), FTIHX (Fidelity), IXUS (iShares)
3. **US Total Bond Market** — BND (Vanguard), FXNAX (Fidelity)

At 26 with a long time horizon, most Bogleheads would run heavy in equities — 80/20 or even 90/10 stocks/bonds is defensible. You have decades to ride out volatility. What you cannot afford is bailing during a crash because you held more risk than you could stomach — so pick an allocation you'll hold through a 40% drawdown without panic-selling.

The honest answer: if you don't want to manage this yourself, a single target-date fund in both your 401(k) and IRA is exactly what the Boglehead community endorses. "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack."

---

## Your action sequence, in order

1. Move your $2,000 to a high-yield savings account (starter emergency fund — done).
2. Log into your 401(k) and set contribution to capture the full employer match.
3. Pay off student loans aggressively over the next 12–18 months while simultaneously starting the IRA.
4. Open a Roth IRA (Fidelity or Vanguard); fund it with $7,000 this year if you can.
5. As income grows and the student loan is gone, increase 401(k) contributions beyond the match toward the $23,500 annual limit.
6. Build your emergency fund up to 3–6 months of expenses as cash flow allows.
7. Any money after that: taxable brokerage, same three-fund portfolio.

---

## A few things to ignore for now

You don't need to think about backdoor Roth conversions, mega backdoor Roth, tax-loss harvesting, factor tilts, dividend strategies, or sector ETFs. Those are problems for a later version of you with a more complex financial picture. For the next few years, your job is to: stay out of high-interest debt, capture free money (employer match), fill tax-advantaged accounts with cheap index funds, and leave it alone.

"Stay the course" isn't a platitude — it's the actual instruction. The biggest mistake most investors make isn't picking the wrong fund; it's selling during a downturn and locking in losses. The fund choice barely matters compared to your savings rate and your willingness to stay invested through volatility.
