Your advisor's placement is backwards — nearly a textbook example of what to avoid. Let me walk through why.

**What you have:**
- Roth IRA: BND (bonds)
- Traditional 401(k): VXUS (international stocks)
- Taxable: VTI (US stocks)

**What the problem is:**

The advisors's rationale sounds intuitive — "safe assets in your retirement security account" — but it confuses two different things: (1) your overall stock/bond allocation, which is how much risk you're taking, and (2) *where* you put each asset across your accounts, which is a tax efficiency question. These are separate decisions, and conflating them costs you real money.

Here's the core issue. A Roth IRA is the single most valuable account you own. Every dollar that grows inside it comes out tax-free, forever. You want your highest-expected-return assets there — stocks, not bonds — because you're trying to maximize what grows tax-free. Parking bonds in your Roth is like putting a bicycle in a Ferrari garage. The space is precious; you're filling it with the slowest thing you own.

The right placement, as documented extensively at bogleheads.org, is:

- **Bonds in the Traditional 401(k)/IRA** — bond interest is taxed as ordinary income. Tax-deferred accounts shelter that income until withdrawal. This is where bonds belong.
- **Stocks in the Roth IRA** — you want the highest-growth asset here so the maximum amount of compounding is never taxed.
- **Stocks in taxable** — qualified dividends and long-term capital gains are taxed at favorable rates (0%, 15%, or 20%), and unrealized gains aren't taxed at all until you sell.

So the conventional Boglehead arrangement for a three-fund portfolio across these three accounts would look something like:

| Account | What to hold |
|---|---|
| Traditional 401(k) | BND (bonds) |
| Roth IRA | VTI or VXUS (stocks) |
| Taxable | VTI (stocks) |

There's also a nuance on VXUS: international stock funds pay foreign withholding taxes on dividends, and you can only claim the **foreign tax credit** to recover them if the fund is held in a taxable account. If VXUS is in your 401(k), that foreign tax credit is permanently lost — a small but real drag of roughly 0.20% per year. Ideally, VXUS would live in your taxable brokerage (alongside or instead of VTI), and VTI or some other US equity fund would fill your Roth IRA and 401(k) equity allocation.

**On the advisor's reasoning:**

"Safe assets belong in your retirement security account" conflates the risk of the investment with the tax efficiency of the account. Your retirement security comes from your overall asset allocation — the percentage in stocks vs. bonds — not from which account holds the bonds. Whether BND sits in your Roth or your 401(k) doesn't change how much of your portfolio is in bonds. It only changes how much of BND's growth is tax-free versus tax-deferred, and how much Roth space you're "wasting" on a low-growth asset.

"That's where you have the most money" (justifying VXUS in the 401(k)) is also backwards logic for tax placement. The *why* that matters is the tax character of the asset, not the size of the account. VXUS in the 401(k) means you lose the foreign tax credit; BND in the 401(k) means bond income is properly sheltered from ordinary income tax.

**What to do now:**

The good news is that you're using all three of the right funds — VTI, VXUS, and BND — which are the building blocks of the classic three-fund portfolio. The allocation itself may be fine. The only issue is placement.

Since rebalancing inside tax-advantaged accounts (your Roth IRA and 401(k)) has no tax consequences, you can fix the placement without a taxable event. You'd simply:
1. Sell BND inside the Roth IRA, buy VTI or VXUS.
2. Sell VXUS inside the Traditional 401(k), buy BND (or whatever bond fund is available in your plan).

Check what bond fund options your 401(k) plan offers — most have at least a total bond market index or a stable value fund. If your 401(k) doesn't have a great bond option, that changes the calculus slightly, but the Roth IRA fix (swap bonds for stocks) is unambiguous regardless.

One sentence of due diligence: this is general Boglehead reasoning, not personalized financial advice — if you have a complex tax situation or are close to retirement, bouncing this off a fee-only fiduciary advisor (or posting on bogleheads.org for a free forum review) is worth the time.
