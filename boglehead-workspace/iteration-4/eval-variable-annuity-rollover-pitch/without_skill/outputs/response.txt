This situation raises several serious red flags that you should carefully consider before making any decision. Rolling a $400,000 401(k) into a variable annuity at retirement is a major financial move, and the pitch you're describing has several characteristics that should make you pause.

CONFLICTS OF INTEREST TO UNDERSTAND

First, the uncomfortable truth: brokers who sell variable annuities earn some of the highest commissions in the financial industry — often 5% to 8% of the amount invested. On a $400,000 rollover, your broker could stand to earn $20,000 to $32,000 or more from this single transaction. That doesn't automatically make the recommendation wrong, but it means you should scrutinize it especially carefully. Ask your broker directly: "What commission will you earn if I do this, and are you a fiduciary?" A fiduciary is legally required to act in your best interest. Many brokers are held only to a "suitability" standard, which is a lower bar.

THE CLAIMS DON'T HOLD UP WELL ON EXAMINATION

"Guarantees income for life" — This is technically true for products with a Guaranteed Minimum Withdrawal Benefit (GMWB) or similar rider, but the devil is in the details. These guarantees come at a steep cost in fees, and the "guarantee" is often on a benefit base, not your actual account value. The income amount may be modest relative to what you could generate from a simple low-cost portfolio.

"Tax-deferred growth perfect for retirement" — Your 401(k) is already tax-deferred. Rolling it into an annuity provides zero additional tax benefit on this front. This is one of the most misleading arguments made in favor of variable annuities — putting a tax-deferred product inside an already-tax-deferred account is redundant and provides no marginal tax advantage.

THE FEES ARE THE CRITICAL ISSUE

Variable annuities are typically loaded with multiple layers of fees:
- Mortality and expense (M&E) charges: typically 1.0% to 1.5% per year
- Administrative fees: often 0.10% to 0.30%
- Underlying fund expense ratios: often 0.5% to 1.5%
- Rider fees (for the "lifetime income guarantee"): often 0.5% to 1.5% additional

Combined, total annual costs of 2.5% to 4% or more are common. On a $400,000 account, that's $10,000 to $16,000 per year in fees — every single year, compounding against your growth. Over 20 years of retirement, the drag from fees like these can cost you hundreds of thousands of dollars in lost wealth compared to a simple, low-cost index fund portfolio.

THE 7-YEAR SURRENDER PERIOD IS A SERIOUS CONCERN

The broker says you "won't need to touch the principal." But retirement is unpredictable. Medical emergencies, long-term care needs, a family crisis, an unexpected large expense — any of these could require you to access your savings. During a 7-year surrender period, withdrawing more than a small annual allowance (often 10%) triggers surrender charges, which can be 7% or more of the amount withdrawn in the early years. You would be locking up your life savings with significant penalties for accessing them when you might need them most.

WHAT TO CONSIDER INSTEAD

For most retirees, a straightforward portfolio of low-cost index funds — a mix of stocks and bonds appropriate for your age, risk tolerance, and income needs — has historically provided both growth and income at a fraction of the cost. Products like Vanguard's or Fidelity's index funds charge as little as 0.03% to 0.10% in annual expenses.

If you genuinely want guaranteed lifetime income, a plain immediate annuity (also called a SPIA — Single Premium Immediate Annuity) is far simpler, cheaper, and more transparent than a variable annuity. You pay a lump sum and receive a fixed monthly income for life. There are no hidden fees, no surrender periods, and no complex riders.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO BEFORE DECIDING

1. Get a second opinion from a fee-only fiduciary financial advisor. Fee-only means they charge you directly (hourly or flat fee) and earn no commissions. You can find one at NAPFA.org or Garrettplanningnetwork.com.

2. Request a full disclosure of all fees on the specific variable annuity product being recommended — in writing.

3. Ask the broker to confirm in writing whether they are a fiduciary and what commission they will receive.

4. Read the prospectus carefully — particularly the fee table and the surrender charge schedule.

5. Do not let anyone pressure you into a decision quickly. This is $400,000 and the rest of your retirement. Take the time you need.

BOTTOM LINE

Based on what you've described, this recommendation has several hallmarks of a high-commission product sale dressed up as financial planning advice. The tax-deferral argument is misleading (your 401(k) is already tax-deferred), the fees on variable annuities are typically very high, the surrender period is a serious liquidity risk, and the broker has a significant financial incentive to make this sale. That doesn't mean it's definitely wrong for your situation, but please get an independent second opinion from a fee-only fiduciary before signing anything.
