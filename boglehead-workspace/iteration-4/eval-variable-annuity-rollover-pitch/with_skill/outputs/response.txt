No. Don't do this rollover. This is one of the most commonly flagged bad deals in personal finance, and the pitch you've just described hits nearly every red flag on the list.

Let me explain what's actually being proposed and why the framing obscures the costs.

---

WHAT A VARIABLE ANNUITY ROLLOVER ACTUALLY IS

Your broker is proposing you take $400,000 in a 401(k) — money that is currently in a tax-advantaged account, probably invested in low-cost mutual funds — and move it into an insurance product. That product will:

1. Lock your money up for 7 years under a surrender charge schedule (typically 7% in year 1, declining by 1% per year). If you need $50,000 for a medical emergency in year 2 and exceed the free withdrawal amount, you pay a penalty to your own money.

2. Add an insurance wrapper fee ("mortality and expense risk charge") typically running 1.0–1.5% per year on top of the sub-account expense ratios.

3. Sub-accounts inside variable annuities typically carry expense ratios of 0.5–1.5% — far higher than the index funds you likely have in your 401(k) now.

4. Total all-in annual costs in a typical broker-sold variable annuity: 2–4% per year.

Compare that to a Vanguard target-date fund at ~0.10% or a three-fund index portfolio at roughly 0.05%. On a $400,000 portfolio, the difference between 3% annual fees and 0.10% fees is roughly $11,600 per year in costs — and those costs compound against you every single year.

---

THE "GUARANTEED INCOME FOR LIFE" FEATURE

This is the hook — and it's worth examining carefully. What your broker is describing is called a Guaranteed Lifetime Withdrawal Benefit (GLWB) rider. Here is what it actually does:

- The guarantee is on a "benefit base," not your actual account value. The benefit base can grow at a guaranteed rate (say, 5–6% per year), but this is NOT the same as your money growing. You cannot take this money out in a lump sum; you can only withdraw it as an income stream at a specified withdrawal rate (often 4–5% per year once you start).

- The GLWB rider itself costs an additional 0.5–1.5% per year on top of everything else.

- The guarantee kicks in only if your account value goes to zero — meaning you only need the guarantee if your investments performed poorly enough to drain the account. Given the fee drag, that scenario becomes more likely, not less. You pay for a guarantee against a problem that the fees themselves help create.

- If your account grows and you die before depleting it, the insurance company keeps the excess (or pays a reduced death benefit). The "guarantee" is insuring against a specific downside while capping your upside.

The Bogleheads community has a phrase for this: you're buying insurance against the thing your fees made more likely.

---

"TAX-DEFERRED GROWTH THAT'S PERFECT FOR RETIREMENT"

This pitch only works on someone who doesn't already have a tax-deferred account. Your 401(k) is already tax-deferred. Rolling it into a variable annuity does not add any new tax benefit — you already have one. You are paying 2–4% per year in extra fees for a tax benefit you already possess.

The only scenario where a tax-deferred annuity adds something a 401(k) doesn't have is if you've already maxed out all available tax-advantaged space and need more. You are not in that situation — you are being asked to move money that's already in tax-advantaged space.

---

"YOU WON'T NEED TO TOUCH THE PRINCIPAL"

This is not a meaningful guarantee. People in retirement frequently need liquidity for:
- Medical expenses (the average retiree couple spends $315,000+ out-of-pocket on healthcare in retirement)
- Home repairs
- Helping family
- Long-term care events

A 7-year surrender period on $400,000 — your entire rollover balance, presumably a significant portion of your retirement assets — is a serious constraint on your financial flexibility during exactly the years when you might need that flexibility most.

---

WHY IS YOUR BROKER RECOMMENDING THIS?

Variable annuities carry commission rates of 5–8% of the purchase amount, sometimes higher with a GLWB rider. On a $400,000 rollover, that's $16,000–$32,000 in commission to your broker, paid by the insurance company, funded by the fees embedded in the product.

This is not an accusation of bad faith. Your broker may genuinely believe in this product. But you should understand that this recommendation puts approximately $20,000 in their pocket, while creating a fee structure that will cost you tens of thousands of dollars over time. That is a real conflict of interest, not a hypothetical one.

Commissioned insurance-licensed advisors earn significantly more from annuity sales than from recommending you stay in your existing 401(k) funds. That is the structure of the compensation, and it is worth naming clearly.

---

WHAT TO DO INSTEAD

If you're retiring next year and need retirement income, here is the Boglehead framework:

1. Roll your 401(k) into a Traditional IRA at a low-cost brokerage (Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab). Immediate benefit: dramatically wider fund selection and the ability to use index funds at 0.03–0.10% expense ratios.

2. Build an income strategy from the portfolio itself. A standard approach:
   - Keep 1–2 years of living expenses in cash or short-term Treasuries/money market. This is your "buffer" so you never have to sell stocks in a downturn.
   - Invest the remainder in a three-fund portfolio (US stocks / international stocks / bonds) or a single Vanguard target-date retirement fund suited to your horizon.
   - Withdraw from the portfolio each year. At a 3.5–4% withdrawal rate, a diversified index portfolio has historically sustained 30+ years of retirement spending.

3. If you genuinely want a guaranteed income floor — and this is a legitimate Boglehead use case — consider a Single Premium Immediate Annuity (SPIA), not a variable annuity. A SPIA is simple: you give the insurance company a lump sum; they pay you a fixed monthly income for life. No surrender charges, no sub-account fees, no riders. It is transparent and competitive. You might use 10–25% of your portfolio to buy a SPIA to cover your essential monthly expenses, and invest the rest.

The difference: a SPIA does one thing clearly and cheaply. A variable annuity with a GLWB does the same thing expensively and opaquely, while also locking up your capital and charging fees that compound against you for the rest of your life.

---

BOTTOM LINE

The pitch you've received is optimized for your broker's income, not yours. The three selling points — guaranteed income, tax-deferred growth, no need to touch principal — are either manufactured benefits (the tax deferral) or features you can get more cheaply elsewhere (guaranteed income via SPIA). The surrender period and fee structure are the real story.

Roll the 401(k) into a Traditional IRA at Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab. Invest it in a low-cost target-date fund or three-fund portfolio. If you want a guaranteed income floor, talk to a fee-only fiduciary advisor about a SPIA — and ask them what they get paid before you sign anything.

Given the size of this decision — $400,000, retirement imminent, irreversible moves on the table — I'd strongly recommend spending $1,500–$3,000 on a one-time engagement with a fee-only fiduciary advisor (search NAPFA.org or the Garrett Planning Network) before doing anything. That cost is trivial compared to what a variable annuity will extract from you over 20–30 years of retirement. The bogleheads.org forum is also free, and the community there reviews situations like this daily.

This is not personalized financial or tax advice — your specific situation may have details that change the calculus. But as a general matter, this rollover is a bad deal.
