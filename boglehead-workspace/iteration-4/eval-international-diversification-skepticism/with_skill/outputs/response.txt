Your instinct here is understandable — and also one of the most common reasoning errors in long-term investing. Let me break it down honestly, because the Boglehead community has thought about this one a lot.

---

THE PERFORMANCE CHASING TRAP

You are, without knowing it, making a performance-chasing argument. The structure of your reasoning is: "VXUS has underperformed VTI for the past 10-15 years, therefore I should not hold VXUS going forward."

Bogle himself said: "Buying funds based purely on past performance is one of the stupidest things an investor can do."

That sounds blunt, but here is why it applies directly to your situation: past relative performance between asset classes tells you almost nothing about future relative performance. In fact, the evidence often points the other direction — long periods of underperformance in a broadly-held asset class are frequently followed by mean reversion, not continued underperformance.

---

WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED 2010-2024

You are right that US stocks dramatically outperformed international stocks over roughly the past 10-15 years. VTI compounded at something like 13-14% annually from 2010 through the mid-2020s. VXUS lagged badly — returns in the 5-7% range for much of that window.

But zoom out and the picture changes. Here is what you need to know about that period:

1. It was historically unusual. US dominance of that magnitude — sustained for that long — is rare. The prior decade (roughly 2000-2010) ran exactly the opposite way: international stocks significantly outperformed US stocks. An investor in 2010 looking at the prior 10 years would have had the mirror-image argument: "Why would I hold VTI when VXUS has been outperforming for a decade?"

2. Much of the US outperformance was driven by valuation expansion, not earnings growth alone. US equities went from relatively cheap to historically expensive (by CAPE ratio and other metrics) over that period. That repricing is a one-time event; it cannot repeat indefinitely. Meanwhile, international equities — particularly Europe and emerging markets — sit at valuations that historically have predicted better forward returns than US equities.

3. Currency and macro factors created tailwinds for US stocks and headwinds for international during this window. These factors are not permanent.

None of this means international will outperform in the next decade. It might not. But you should recognize that your argument — "it underperformed, therefore avoid it" — is precisely what gets investors to buy US stocks at peak relative valuations and skip international at trough relative valuations.

---

THE ACTUAL CASE FOR VXUS

The reason Bogleheads recommend international exposure is not that it will outperform. It is that:

1. You do not know which market will outperform. Neither does anyone else. Global market-cap weighting — owning roughly the market's weight of each country proportional to its size — is the default position of humility. When you hold only VTI, you are making an active bet: that the US will continue to outperform every other major economy. That bet might pay off. But it is still a bet against the global market, and you should call it what it is.

2. Diversification works through correlation, not through picking the best performer. VXUS and VTI do not move in perfect lockstep. International stocks have periods where they meaningfully diverge from US stocks — sometimes in the same direction, sometimes not. That imperfect correlation reduces overall portfolio volatility over long time horizons. Adding an asset that sometimes goes up when your main holding goes sideways smooths the ride, even if the long-run expected returns are similar.

3. The US is about 60-65% of global market cap, not 100%. Holding only VTI means you are dramatically overweighting one country — a bet that a single nation's economy will indefinitely dominate the rest of the world. That might be true. But the UK investor saying the same thing in 1900 (when Britain dominated global markets) would have been wrong over the following century. Diversification is the only free lunch in investing, and geographic diversification is a real form of it.

---

WHAT BOGLEHEADS ACTUALLY DISAGREE ON

To be honest with you: international allocation is one of the genuinely contested questions in the Boglehead community. These are not fringe positions:

- ZERO international: Bogle himself, late in his career, argued that US multinationals like the S&P 500 companies already derive 40%+ of their revenues internationally. He said you get indirect international exposure through VTI, and the currency risk plus the historically higher expenses of international funds are not worth it. This is a legitimate, defensible position.

- GLOBAL MARKET CAP weight (~40% of equities): Vanguard's own target-date funds use roughly this allocation. The argument is that you should own the world proportionally, with no country bet.

- SOMEWHERE IN BETWEEN (20-30% of equities): The most common practical compromise. Enough to matter in a divergence scenario; not so much that it dominates the portfolio's character.

The point is: your current allocation (80% VTI, 20% BND, 0% international) is defensible — Bogle himself essentially ran a US-only equity portfolio. But the argument you used to justify it — "VXUS underperformed, so skip it" — is not the right argument. That is performance chasing dressed up as strategic thinking.

---

A PRACTICAL TAKE ON YOUR PORTFOLIO

Your 80/20 VTI/BND portfolio is clean, cheap, and broadly sensible. If you want to stay US-only, own that decision for the right reasons: you believe US multinationals give adequate international exposure, you accept the concentration bet as reasonable given US institutional strength, and you are not doing it because the last decade looked good.

If you want to add international, a reasonable allocation would be to take some of that 80% VTI and allocate 15-25% of total equities to VXUS. So something like 55-60% VTI, 20-25% VXUS, 20% BND. VXUS has an expense ratio of 0.08% — cheap enough that cost is not a reason to avoid it.

What you should not do: avoid VXUS because it underperformed from 2010-2024, then revisit the question in 2030 after VXUS has outperformed for several years and decide to add it then. That is the cycle of performance chasing that destroys long-run investor returns.

Decide your policy now, when the analysis is clear-headed. Then stay the course.
