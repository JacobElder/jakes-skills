# Anti-Patterns: Things Bogleheads Push Back On

This file catalogs the products, strategies, and behaviors that long-time Bogleheads consistently flag as bad ideas, and the reasoning + recommended alternatives. **This is where this skill earns its keep** — these are the cases where generic financial advice (or worse, a financial-services salesperson's pitch) leads people astray, and where a Boglehead response is sharply different.

When the user mentions any of these, **slow down, name the issue, explain the Boglehead view, and offer the better alternative.** Don't just answer the literal question if the literal question is downstream of a bad premise.

---

## 1. Whole life insurance / IUL / VUL ("permanent" life insurance)

**What's pitched:** "It's tax-free growth plus a death benefit, you can borrow against it tax-free, it's a great forced-savings vehicle for high earners who have maxed out everything else."

**The Boglehead reality:**
- Whole life is a bundled product: term life insurance + a tax-deferred savings account, sold together at a high cost.
- The first several years of premiums largely cover commissions and fees. Internal rate of return is often 0–2% for decades before crossing into anything competitive.
- "Tax-free loans" come at 5–8% loan interest and reduce the death benefit. The "no tax" framing is misleading — you're paying interest *to the policy* on your own money.
- Insurance illustrations show projected dividends that have a track record of underperforming over time. Don't trust them.
- The cash value rarely catches up to what you'd have if you bought term life + invested the difference in a low-cost index fund.

**The Boglehead answer:**
- Buy **20- or 30-year level term life insurance** if you have dependents (income replacement).
- Invest the premium difference in tax-advantaged accounts (401k, IRA, HSA), then taxable index funds.
- Cancel term when you no longer have dependents who'd need the death benefit (typically near retirement, once kids are independent and there are sufficient assets).

**If they already own a whole life policy:**
- If owned >15–20 years: usually keep it (worst fees are paid; reasonable cash value; future returns probably 3–5%). Stop adding to it.
- If owned <5 years: surrender it (you've already lost most of the money to fees; cut losses, redeploy).
- If owned 5–15 years: get an **in-force illustration** to see projected future cash value, then compare to alternatives.
- Consider a **1035 exchange** to a low-cost variable annuity (e.g., Fidelity Personal Retirement at 0.25% ER, since Vanguard transitioned theirs to Transamerica). This preserves the cost basis tax-deferred for further growth without the insurance drag. Note: this option is itself niche and not always optimal.

**Legitimate uses of permanent life insurance** (rare):
- Estate-planning for the wealthy ($10M+ estates) to provide liquidity for estate taxes (using ILITs).
- Funding a buy-sell agreement for a business.
- A disabled child who will need lifetime support.
- Some asset-protection purposes in specific states.

If the user fits none of these, the answer is no.

## 2. Annuities (variable, fixed, indexed, immediate)

**The general Boglehead position:** annuities sold by commissioned insurance salespeople are almost always bad investments. The product is complex, the surrender charges are punishing, the internal fees are 2–4% annually (vs. 0.05% for an index fund), and the supposed "guarantees" cost more than they're worth.

**The exceptions:**
- **Single Premium Immediate Annuity (SPIA)** at retirement age, for longevity insurance. Buy with 10–30% of the portfolio to create a guaranteed income floor. SPIAs are simple, transparent, and a legitimate Boglehead tool — but only at retirement, not as a 30-year accumulation strategy.
- **Qualified Longevity Annuity Contract (QLAC)** in retirement accounts to defer RMDs into your 80s.
- **Low-cost deferred variable annuities** (Fidelity Personal Retirement, ~0.25% ER) for high earners with maxed-out tax-advantaged space and a long horizon, *and* who would otherwise hold tax-inefficient assets in taxable. Niche.

**Red flags in any annuity pitch:**
- Equity-indexed / fixed-indexed annuities ("Get market upside with no downside!") — caps on gains, surrender charges, illiquid for 7–15 years. Almost always bad.
- "Hybrid" annuities, "income riders," "GLWB" (guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit) — features that sound like guarantees but reduce your principal balance over time.
- Surrender charges >0% — exit fees are a giant warning sign.
- Commission >3% — paying for someone else's vacation.

## 3. Individual stock picking (as a strategy)

**The Boglehead position:** for fun money you can afford to lose (~5% of net worth), have at it. As a strategy, no.

**Reasoning:**
- The math says most stocks underperform the index. Bessembinder (2018): from 1926–2016, 58% of US stocks underperformed Treasury bills over their lifetime; the entire net wealth creation came from ~4% of stocks. Missing those few winners destroys your return.
- Active managers (who do this for a living, with research staff and Bloomberg terminals) underperform indexes 80%+ of the time over 15 years. A retail investor's odds are worse.
- Concentration risk: even if your pick is great, a single-stock event (fraud, technology disruption, leadership change) can destroy the position.
- Behavioral cost: holding individual stocks invites checking the portfolio daily, which invites churning.

**The Boglehead answer:** total-market index fund. "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack."

**Common follow-up:** "But Warren Buffett." Yes, Buffett famously recommends index funds for almost everyone and instructed his own estate's trust to use them. He is an extreme outlier, not a model. He also has decades of accounting expertise, control of capital allocation at his portfolio companies, and access to deal flow you don't.

## 4. Dividend-focused strategies (SCHD, JEPI, "dividend aristocrats," "dividend growth")

**What's pitched:** "Dividend stocks pay you to hold them. Reinvest the dividends and you have a 'snowball.' Dividend aristocrats have outperformed!"

**The Boglehead reality:**
- A dividend is not free money. When a stock pays a $1 dividend, the share price drops by $1. Total return is what matters: price appreciation + dividends.
- Concentrated dividend strategies are a **value tilt** in disguise — and a tax-inefficient one. Dividends are taxable annually; unrealized capital gains are not.
- Backtests showing "dividend stocks outperform" are usually overlapping with the value factor; once you control for that, the dividend premium disappears.
- A total stock market fund (VTI) already includes all the dividend aristocrats *plus* the high-growth non-dividend payers (which have historically driven big returns).
- In taxable accounts, dividend strategies create tax drag for no benefit you couldn't get with a tax-loss harvested total market position.

**SCHD specifically:** ~90% correlated to S&P 500. You're not really diversifying; you're just tilting and paying a small fee for the privilege.

**JEPI / covered-call income funds:** even worse — they cap upside, generate ordinary-income distributions, and don't perform well in bull markets. Marketed to retirees seeking yield; usually a bad fit for almost everyone.

**The Boglehead answer:** total market index fund. If you need income in retirement, withdraw it (sell shares) — taxed at LTCG rates, lower than ordinary dividends.

## 5. Sector funds (technology, healthcare, energy, etc.)

**What's pitched:** "Tech is going to outperform for the next decade." "Healthcare is recession-proof."

**The Boglehead reality:**
- Sector bets are concentrated bets. They have higher volatility than the total market and no reliable extra return.
- If a sector is "obviously going to outperform," that's already priced in.
- Owning VTI already gives you the right market-cap weight of every sector. If tech does well, your VTI portfolio benefits proportionally.
- Adding a sector fund on top is a deliberate overweight — you're betting against the market's pricing.

**The Boglehead answer:** total market. Don't pick sectors. If you really want to overweight something, ask yourself what edge you have over the millions of professional analysts already analyzing it.

## 6. Leveraged ETFs (TQQQ, UPRO, etc.) for long-term holding

**What's pitched:** "TQQQ is 3x QQQ. Hold it for 20 years and you'll be rich!"

**The Boglehead reality:**
- Leveraged ETFs reset their leverage daily. Over time, in volatile markets, **volatility decay** erodes returns badly.
- Backtests during bull markets look amazing; backtests through 2000–2010 ("lost decade") show 95%+ drawdowns.
- They're designed for *day-trading* and short-term hedging, not long-term holding.
- The expense ratios are high (0.85%+).

**The Boglehead answer:** if you want more equity exposure, use a larger stock allocation (e.g., 100/0 vs 80/20), or genuine portfolio leverage (futures, low-cost margin) understood properly. Don't use daily-rebalanced leveraged ETFs.

## 7. Market timing / "waiting for a crash"

**What's pitched:** "Stocks are overvalued / a crash is coming / I'll wait for a pullback before investing this windfall."

**The Boglehead reality:**
- The market has been "overvalued" by various metrics most of the time since the 1990s. Sitting out cost a fortune.
- Vanguard's research: lump-sum beats DCA on a windfall about 2/3 of the time historically. Sitting in cash for "the crash" almost always loses.
- Even when crashes do happen, the recovery typically starts before people feel safe getting back in. Missing the best 10 days in the market over a decade typically halves your return.
- Peter Lynch: "Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections than has been lost in corrections themselves."

**The Boglehead answer:**
- Lump sum if you can stomach it.
- DCA over 3–12 months as a behavioral compromise if not.
- Pick an asset allocation that lets you stay invested through a 50% drawdown without panicking. That allocation is correct *for you*, even if more aggressive allocations have higher expected returns.

## 8. Performance chasing (buying last year's best-performing fund)

**What's pitched:** "Look at this fund's 5-year return — let's buy it!"

**The Boglehead reality:**
- Past performance does not predict future performance. There is roughly zero correlation between a fund's prior 5-year return and its next 5-year return.
- Funds that just had a great run are usually bought heavily, which often precedes mean reversion.
- ARK Innovation (ARKK) — multi-bagger 2017–2020, then -75%. Cathie Wood's "10-year forecast" promised 40% annualized; reality was -19% annualized through 2024.

**Bogle:** "Buying funds based purely on past performance is one of the stupidest things an investor can do."

**The Boglehead answer:** index funds, by definition, don't have an "outperformance" to chase. They just get the market return at minimum cost.

## 9. Active managed funds with high expense ratios

Anything 0.50%+ in expense ratio for a fund that has an obvious index alternative is suspect. 1%+ is almost always wrong. Look for:

- "American Funds" loads — these are typically sold by commissioned advisors. They charge front-end loads (5.75%!) plus high expense ratios. Bad on both ends.
- 401(k) plans loaded with high-ER target-date funds (Voya, Empower, MassMutual proprietary funds at 0.50–1.0%).
- Annuity sub-accounts with 1.5%+ ER plus annuity wrapper fees.
- "Asset allocation" / "lifestyle" / "multi-asset" actively-managed funds — often 0.75–1.5% ER for what amounts to an index portfolio of index funds.

**The Boglehead answer:** find the lowest-cost broad-market index option in the plan. If everything is high-cost, capture the match and use IRA + taxable for the bulk.

## 10. Robo-advisors and "smart beta"

**The Boglehead position:** mostly tolerable, but unnecessary.

- Robo-advisors (Betterment, Wealthfront) at 0.25% AUM offer tax-loss harvesting + auto-rebalancing, which can justify the cost in a *large taxable account*. In an IRA, paying 0.25% for what amounts to an index portfolio is overpaying.
- "Smart beta" / factor ETFs (multi-factor, quality, momentum) are not crazy in principle (the underlying factor research is real). But the implementation often involves higher fees and tax inefficiency, and the factor premiums have been compressing as more capital chases them.
- For most investors: a three-fund portfolio at <0.05% beats either of these net of cost over time. Robos can be reasonable for people who genuinely won't manage their own portfolio otherwise.

## 11. The "do something" instinct during volatility

**The user is in the middle of a market downturn and says they're thinking of:**
- "Going to cash until things calm down" → no. By the time things "calm down," you've missed the recovery.
- "Selling some to lock in gains before it falls more" → no. You're now also a tax-inefficient timer.
- "Buying more aggressively now that things are cheap" → tempting, but be careful not to abandon your asset allocation. If your IPS says rebalance, rebalance. Don't ad-hoc concentrate.
- "Switching to gold / Bitcoin / 'safer' funds" → that's panic in disguise.

**The Boglehead answer:** the right plan is the one you made when the market was calm. If you didn't make one then, sit on your hands now and make one *after* this resolves. Don't make plans during a panic.

## 12. "My advisor wants me to..."

When the user reports a financial-services-industry recommendation, treat it with skepticism. The default explanation for any advice from a commissioned salesperson is that the recommendation maximizes the seller's compensation, which is often inversely correlated with the buyer's outcome.

Common red flags from advisors:
- Anything called a "concept" — "the LASER fund concept," "the infinite banking concept," "the be-your-own-banker concept." Translation: whole life insurance.
- "We don't charge any fees; the company pays us." Translation: commissions on products you'll buy, often 5–10% loaded into the product price.
- "AUM fees" of 1%+ for a portfolio of 15–30 active funds. Translation: $10k/year on a $1M portfolio for index-fund-equivalent results.
- Replacing existing investments with the advisor's preferred products in your first meeting. Translation: churning.

**The Boglehead answer:**
- For most people: DIY a three-fund portfolio in a discount brokerage. Total cost: 0.05%/year + a few hours of setup.
- If you genuinely need professional help: hire an **advice-only** or **fee-only fiduciary** advisor. Pay a flat fee ($1,500–$5,000) for a plan, or hourly ($300/hr) for specific questions. NAPFA and the Garrett Planning Network maintain directories.
- Vanguard Personal Advisor Services (~0.30% AUM) is a tolerable option for those who want a real human, since they use Vanguard funds and won't push high-cost products.

## 13. Withdrawing from retirement accounts for non-emergency use

**What's pitched:** "I'll just take a 401(k) loan / hardship withdrawal to pay for [vacation / wedding / business / down payment]."

**The Boglehead reality:**
- A pre-59.5 withdrawal from a Traditional 401(k)/IRA is taxed as ordinary income **plus a 10% penalty**. In a 24% federal bracket + state taxes, you can lose 35–45% of the gross withdrawal.
- 401(k) loans are repayable from after-tax money, then taxed again on withdrawal — double taxation. They also accelerate to immediate repayment if you leave the job; default and it becomes a distribution + penalty.
- Lost compounding: $10k withdrawn at age 35, foregoing 30 years of 7% growth, costs about $76k at age 65.

**The Boglehead answer:**
- For a real emergency: emergency fund (which is why you have one). Or a HELOC. Or 0% intro credit card if the timeline allows full repayment.
- For a non-emergency want: save up. The point of a 401(k) is retirement.
- For a first home down payment: $10k lifetime exemption from Traditional IRA penalty exists, but think hard before using it.

## 14. Crypto, NFTs, alternative assets as a "diversifier"

The Boglehead position on crypto has shifted over the years. Current rough consensus:

- Crypto is **speculation**, not investing. It has no cash flows, no intrinsic value model, and returns derive entirely from someone else paying more later.
- Volatility is ~5x equities. Drawdowns of 70–90% are routine.
- If you want to speculate, fine — keep it under 5% of net worth, money you can lose without changing your retirement plan.
- Don't treat it as a diversifier. Its correlation with risk assets has been high, especially in stress periods.

For commodities, gold, and other "alternatives" — similar reasoning. They have no expected real return long-term; they're insurance against specific scenarios (inflation, currency collapse). If you want gold for those reasons, 5–10% is the typical "permanent portfolio" allocation. Most Bogleheads hold zero.

## When the user pushes back

You'll sometimes get pushback: "But my situation is different / my advisor said / I read on Reddit..."

Honor the question, but stick to your reasoning. Acknowledge the legitimate parts ("yes, in *specific* cases like estate tax planning, whole life can have a role"), explain why their case probably isn't one of those, and present the alternative.

Don't lecture, don't moralize. State the position once, give the reasoning, and let them decide. Bogleheads in the wild are often direct but rarely preachy — model that tone.
